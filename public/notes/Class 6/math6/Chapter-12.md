• **Definition of a Triangle**: A triangle is a basic polygon and a closed plane figure formed by three line segments intersecting at their endpoints.
• **Origin of the Term**: The word "triangle" is derived from the Latin word 'triangulus,' where 'tri' means three and 'angulus' means corner or angle.
• **Geometric Construction**: A triangle is formed when three non-collinear points in a plane are joined by line segments.
• **Non-Collinear Points**: For a triangle to exist, the three points (vertices) must not lie on the same straight line; if they were collinear, they would form a single line segment instead of a closed figure.
• **Vertices of a Triangle**: The three points where the line segments meet are called the vertices (singular: vertex) of the triangle, usually denoted by capital letters like A, B, and C.
• **Sides of a Triangle**: The three line segments that form the boundary of the triangle are called its sides, denoted as AB, BC, and CA.
• **Angles of a Triangle**: The space or inclination between two adjacent sides meeting at a vertex forms an angle; a triangle has exactly three interior angles.
• **Notation of a Triangle**: A triangle with vertices A, B, and C is symbolically represented by the symbol 'Δ' followed by the vertex letters, such as ΔABC.
• **Naming Conventions**: A triangle can be named in any order of its vertices, such as ΔABC, ΔBCA, ΔCAB, ΔACB, ΔBAC, or ΔCBA, all representing the same geometric figure.
• **The Six Elements**: Every triangle consists of six primary parts or elements: three sides and three angles.
• **Side-Angle Relationship**: The sides and angles of a triangle are intrinsically linked; the measures of the angles depend on the lengths of the sides and vice versa.
• **Closed Figure Property**: Being a closed figure, a triangle encloses a specific area of the plane, separating the plane into distinct regions.
• **Simple Polygon**: A triangle is the simplest possible polygon because it is the polygon with the minimum number of sides (three) required to enclose a space.
• **Linear Boundaries**: Unlike circles or ovals, the boundaries of a triangle are strictly linear (straight lines), making it a rectilinear figure.
• **Dimensionality**: A triangle is a two-dimensional (2D) figure, meaning it has length and width (base and height) but no thickness or depth.
• **Vertex-Side Correspondence**: In ΔABC, the side opposite to vertex A is BC, the side opposite to vertex B is AC, and the side opposite to vertex C is AB.
• **Angle-Side Correspondence**: In ΔABC, the angle opposite to side BC is ∠A, the angle opposite to side AC is ∠B, and the angle opposite to side AB is ∠C.

• **Detailed Study of Vertices**: A vertex is a point of intersection where two sides of the triangle meet; in ΔPQR, the vertices are the points P, Q, and R.
• **Detailed Study of Sides**: The sides are the line segments PQ, QR, and RP that constitute the framework of the triangle.
• **Length of Sides**: The lengths of the sides are often denoted by lowercase letters corresponding to the opposite vertex; for example, side BC is 'a', side AC is 'b', and side AB is 'c'.
• **Detailed Study of Angles**: The three angles are formed at the vertices; they are denoted as ∠PQR (or ∠Q), ∠QRP (or ∠R), and ∠RPQ (or ∠P).
• **Interior Angles**: These are the angles located inside the boundary of the triangle formed by the intersection of the sides.
• **Vertex Naming**: When naming an angle using three letters, the middle letter always represents the vertex where the angle is located (e.g., in ∠ABC, B is the vertex).
• **Adjacent Sides**: Two sides of a triangle that share a common vertex are called adjacent sides; for example, in ΔABC, AB and BC are adjacent sides sharing vertex B.
• **Included Angle**: The angle formed between two specific sides is called the included angle for those sides; ∠B is the included angle for sides AB and BC.
• **Included Side**: The side common to two angles is called the included side; side BC is the included side for ∠B and ∠C.
• **Opposite Side to a Vertex**: For every vertex in a triangle, there is exactly one side that does not touch that vertex, known as the opposite side.
• **Opposite Vertex to a Side**: For every side in a triangle, there is exactly one vertex that is not an endpoint of that side, known as the opposite vertex.
• **Boundary of the Triangle**: The union of the three line segments AB, BC, and CA forms the boundary or the perimeter path of the triangle.
• **Planar Region**: A triangle lies entirely within a single flat surface or plane.
• **Rigidity of Triangles**: In geometry and engineering, the triangle is considered a rigid shape because its angles cannot change without changing the lengths of its sides.
• **Sum of Elements**: While a triangle has 6 elements (3 sides, 3 angles), knowing only 3 specific elements (like SSS, SAS, or ASA) is often enough to define the triangle completely.
• **Points on the Triangle**: Any point located on the line segments AB, BC, or CA is said to lie "on" the triangle.

• **Triangular Region**: The set of all points consisting of the interior of the triangle and the points on the boundary of the triangle is called the triangular region.
• **Interior of a Triangle**: The part of the plane that is enclosed within the three sides of the triangle is called its interior.
• **Exterior of a Triangle**: The part of the plane that lies outside the boundary of the triangle is called its exterior.
• **Boundary Points**: Points that lie exactly on the line segments forming the sides of the triangle are called boundary points.
• **Point Location - Interior**: A point is in the interior if it does not lie on the sides and is "inside" the enclosure (e.g., Point O inside ΔABC).
• **Point Location - Exterior**: A point is in the exterior if it is neither on the boundary nor in the interior (e.g., Point X outside ΔABC).
• **Point Location - On the Triangle**: A point is "on" the triangle if it lies on any of the segments AB, BC, or CA (e.g., Point P on side AB).
• **Convexity**: A triangle is always a convex figure, meaning any line segment joining two points inside the triangle will lie entirely within the triangle.
• **Separation of the Plane**: A triangle divides the infinite plane into three disjoint sets of points: the interior, the exterior, and the triangle itself (the boundary).
• **Area vs. Perimeter**: The measure of the interior region is called the Area, while the measure of the boundary length is called the Perimeter.
• **Inclusion Property**: The triangular region includes the boundary, whereas the "interior" specifically excludes the boundary.
• **Infinite Exterior**: While the interior and the boundary are finite and bounded, the exterior of a triangle is an infinite region.
• **Intersection of Half-Planes**: The interior of a triangle can be defined as the intersection of three half-planes created by the lines containing the sides.
• **Vertex as Boundary**: The vertices are specific boundary points where two sides meet; they are part of the triangular region.
• **Distance from Vertices**: Every point in the interior of a triangle has a positive distance from all three sides.
• **Collinearity Check**: If a point lies on the line segment AB, it is collinear with vertices A and B and is part of the triangle's boundary.

• **Basis of Classification**: Triangles can be classified into three categories based on the lengths of their sides: Scalene, Isosceles, and Equilateral.
• **Scalene Triangle**: A triangle in which all three sides are of different lengths is called a scalene triangle.
• **Properties of Scalene Triangles**: In a scalene triangle, all three interior angles are also of different measures.
• **Isosceles Triangle**: A triangle in which at least two sides are equal in length is called an isosceles triangle.
• **Base of an Isosceles Triangle**: In an isosceles triangle, the unequal side is usually referred to as the 'base'.
• **Base Angles**: The angles opposite to the equal sides of an isosceles triangle are called base angles, and they are always equal in measure.
• **Vertex Angle**: The angle formed by the two equal sides in an isosceles triangle is called the vertex angle.
• **Equilateral Triangle**: A triangle in which all three sides are equal in length is called an equilateral triangle.
• **Equiangular Property**: Every equilateral triangle is also equiangular, meaning all three of its interior angles are equal.
• **Angle Measure in Equilateral Triangles**: Since the sum of angles is 180° and all three angles are equal, each angle in an equilateral triangle measures exactly 60°.
• **Equilateral as Isosceles**: An equilateral triangle is a special case of an isosceles triangle because it satisfies the condition of having at least two equal sides.
• **Symmetry in Equilateral Triangles**: An equilateral triangle has three lines of symmetry, passing through each vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.
• **Symmetry in Isosceles Triangles**: A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has exactly one line of symmetry, which bisects the vertex angle.
• **Symmetry in Scalene Triangles**: A scalene triangle has no lines of symmetry because all its sides and angles are unequal.
• **Identification by Tick Marks**: In geometric diagrams, equal sides are often marked with the same number of small slashes or tick marks to indicate equality.
• **Relationship between Sides and Angles**: A fundamental rule is that angles opposite to equal sides are equal, and sides opposite to equal angles are equal.

• **Basis of Classification**: Triangles can be classified into three categories based on the measures of their interior angles: Acute-angled, Right-angled, and Obtuse-angled.
• **Acute-angled Triangle**: A triangle in which all three angles are acute (each angle is less than 90°) is called an acute triangle.
• **Right-angled Triangle**: A triangle in which one angle is exactly 90° (a right angle) is called a right-angled triangle.
• **Hypotenuse**: In a right-angled triangle, the side opposite to the 90° angle is called the hypotenuse; it is always the longest side of the triangle.
• **Legs of a Right Triangle**: The two sides that form the right angle are called the legs or the base and the perpendicular.
• **Obtuse-angled Triangle**: A triangle in which one angle is obtuse (greater than 90° but less than 180°) is called an obtuse-angled triangle.
• **Restriction on Right Angles**: A triangle can have at most one right angle; if it had two, the sum of those two would be 180°, leaving 0° for the third angle, which is impossible.
• **Restriction on Obtuse Angles**: A triangle can have at most one obtuse angle; if it had two, the sum would exceed 180°, which violates the angle sum property.
• **Remaining Angles in Right Triangles**: In a right-angled triangle, the other two angles must be acute and their sum must be 90° (they are complementary).
• **Remaining Angles in Obtuse Triangles**: In an obtuse-angled triangle, the other two angles must be acute and their sum must be less than 90°.
• **Right Isosceles Triangle**: A triangle can be both right-angled and isosceles; in this case, the angles are 90°, 45°, and 45°.
• **Obtuse Isosceles Triangle**: A triangle can be both obtuse-angled and isosceles; for example, angles of 120°, 30°, and 30°.
• **Acute Isosceles Triangle**: An equilateral triangle is a specific type of acute isosceles triangle where all angles are 60°.
• **Scalene Combinations**: A scalene triangle can be acute, right-angled, or obtuse-angled.
• **Visual Identification**: A right angle is usually denoted by a small square symbol at the vertex.
• **Longest Side Rule**: The side opposite the largest angle is always the longest side; thus, in an obtuse triangle, the side opposite the obtuse angle is the longest.

• **Statement of the Property**: The sum of the measures of the three interior angles of any triangle is always equal to 180° or two right angles.
• **Universal Truth**: This property holds true for every triangle, regardless of its shape, size, or classification by sides or angles.
• **Mathematical Expression**: For ΔABC, the property is expressed as: ∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180°.
• **Deduction of Third Angle**: If the measures of two angles of a triangle are known, the third angle can be found by subtracting the sum of the two known angles from 180°.
• **Constraint on Angle Measures**: No triangle can have more than one angle that is 90° or more.
• **Minimum Acute Angles**: Every triangle must have at least two acute angles.
• **Equilateral Case**: In an equilateral triangle, since all angles are equal (x), then x + x + x = 180°, which means 3x = 180°, so x = 60°.
• **Right-Angled Case**: In a right triangle, if one angle is 90°, the sum of the other two must be 180° - 90° = 90°.
• **Obtuse-Angled Case**: If one angle is 110°, the sum of the other two must be 70°, ensuring both are acute.
• **Verification by Paper Cutting**: If you cut out the three corners (angles) of a paper triangle and arrange them side-by-side at a point, they will form a straight line (180°).
• **Verification by Folding**: Folding the vertices of a paper triangle to meet at a point on the base also demonstrates that they fill a straight angle.
• **Parallel Line Proof**: By drawing a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side, alternate interior angles can be used to prove the sum is 180°.
• **Non-Existence of Certain Triangles**: A triangle with angles like 90°, 90°, and 0° cannot exist because it does not form a closed figure.
• **Invalid Angle Sets**: A set of angles like 70°, 80°, and 40° cannot form a triangle because 70 + 80 + 40 = 190°, which is greater than 180°.
• **Application in Problems**: This property is used to solve for unknown variables when angles are given in terms of 'x' (e.g., x, 2x, 3x).
• **Foundation of Euclidean Geometry**: The angle sum property is a cornerstone of Euclidean geometry and is linked to the Parallel Postulate.

• **Statement of the Property**: The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle is always strictly greater than the length of the third side.
• **Mathematical Conditions**: For a triangle with sides a, b, and c, the following three conditions must all be true: (1) a + b > c, (2) b + c > a, and (3) a + c > b.
• **Existence of a Triangle**: If any one of these conditions fails, a triangle cannot be formed with those side lengths.
• **The "Straight Line" Case**: If the sum of two sides equals the third side (a + b = c), the three points are collinear, and they form a line segment, not a triangle.
• **Shortest Path Principle**: This property is based on the principle that a straight line is the shortest distance between two points; hence, going from A to B directly is shorter than going from A to C and then C to B.
• **Difference Property**: A corollary to the inequality property is that the difference between the lengths of any two sides of a triangle is always less than the length of the third side.
• **Mathematical Expression of Difference**: |a - b| < c, |b - c| < a, and |a - c| < b.
• **Testing Side Lengths**: To check if three lengths can form a triangle, it is sufficient to check if the sum of the two smaller lengths is greater than the largest length.
• **Example of Invalid Sides**: Sides of 2 cm, 3 cm, and 6 cm cannot form a triangle because 2 + 3 = 5, which is not greater than 6.
• **Example of Valid Sides**: Sides of 3 cm, 4 cm, and 5 cm can form a triangle because 3 + 4 > 5, 4 + 5 > 3, and 3 + 5 > 4.
• **Range of the Third Side**: If two sides are given as 'x' and 'y', the third side 'z' must be: (x - y) < z < (x + y).
• **Application in Real Life**: This property is used in structural engineering to ensure stability and in navigation to calculate possible routes.
• **Scalene Inequality**: In a scalene triangle, the side lengths follow the order of the angle measures; the smallest side is opposite the smallest angle.
• **Isosceles Inequality**: In an isosceles triangle with sides 5, 5, and 8, the property holds because 5 + 5 > 8.
• **Equilateral Inequality**: In an equilateral triangle with sides s, s, and s, the property holds because s + s > s (2s > s) is always true for positive lengths.
• **Geometric Visualization**: If you try to connect two short sticks to a very long stick, they won't meet unless their combined length exceeds the long stick.

• **Definition of Exterior Angle**: When any side of a triangle is extended or produced beyond the vertex, the angle formed outside the triangle between the extended side and the adjacent side is called an exterior angle.
• **Interior Adjacent Angle**: The angle inside the triangle that shares a vertex with the exterior angle is called the interior adjacent angle.
• **Interior Opposite Angles**: For any given exterior angle, the two interior angles of the triangle that are not adjacent to it are called the interior opposite angles (or remote interior angles).
• **Exterior Angle Property Statement**: The measure of an exterior angle of a triangle is equal to the sum of the measures of its two interior opposite angles.
• **Mathematical Expression**: If side BC of ΔABC is extended to point D, then Exterior ∠ACD = ∠A + ∠B.
• **Linear Pair Relationship**: An exterior angle and its adjacent interior angle always form a linear pair, meaning their sum is 180°.
• **Deduction from Angle Sum Property**: Since ∠A + ∠B + ∠C = 180° and Exterior ∠ACD + ∠C = 180°, it logically follows that Exterior ∠ACD = ∠A + ∠B.
• **Inequality of Exterior Angle**: An exterior angle of a triangle is always greater than either of its interior opposite angles individually.
• **Number of Exterior Angles**: At each vertex of a triangle, two exterior angles can be formed (by extending either of the two sides meeting at that vertex); these two are vertically opposite and thus equal.
• **Total Exterior Angles**: A triangle has a total of six exterior angles (two at each vertex).
• **Sum of Exterior Angles**: The sum of the exterior angles of a triangle (taking one at each vertex) is always 360°.
• **Visualizing the Property**: If you "walk" around the perimeter of a triangle and turn at each vertex to face the next side, you will have completed one full rotation of 360°.
• **Application in Finding Angles**: This property allows for the calculation of interior angles when an exterior angle is provided, without needing the adjacent interior angle.
• **Right Triangle Exterior**: In a right-angled triangle, the exterior angle adjacent to the 90° angle is also 90°.
• **Equilateral Triangle Exterior**: In an equilateral triangle, every exterior angle measures 120° (60° + 60°).
• **Obtuse Triangle Exterior**: The exterior angle adjacent to an obtuse angle will always be an acute angle.

• **Definition of Median**: A line segment joining a vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of the opposite side is called a median.
• **Midpoint**: The point on a side that divides it into two equal segments is the midpoint.
• **Number of Medians**: Every triangle has exactly three medians, one from each vertex.
• **Centroid**: The three medians of a triangle always intersect at a single common point inside the triangle, called the Centroid (usually denoted by G).
• **Concurrency**: Since all medians meet at one point, they are said to be concurrent.
• **Location of Centroid**: The centroid always lies in the interior of the triangle, regardless of whether the triangle is acute, right, or obtuse.
• **Definition of Altitude**: The perpendicular line segment drawn from a vertex of a triangle to the opposite side (or the line containing the opposite side) is called an altitude.
• **Height of a Triangle**: Altitude is often referred to as the 'height' of the triangle relative to the opposite side, which is called the 'base'.
• **Number of Altitudes**: Every triangle has exactly three altitudes.
• **Orthocenter**: The three altitudes of a triangle intersect at a single common point called the Orthocenter (usually denoted by H).
• **Location of Orthocenter (Acute)**: In an acute-angled triangle, the orthocenter lies in the interior of the triangle.
• **Location of Orthocenter (Right)**: In a right-angled triangle, the orthocenter coincides with the vertex containing the right angle.
• **Location of Orthocenter (Obtuse)**: In an obtuse-angled triangle, the orthocenter lies in the exterior of the triangle.
• **Altitudes in Obtuse Triangles**: To draw altitudes from the acute vertices in an obtuse triangle, the opposite sides must be extended.
• **Special Case - Equilateral Triangle**: In an equilateral triangle, the medians and altitudes are the same line segments; they coincide.
• **Special Case - Isosceles Triangle**: In an isosceles triangle, the altitude drawn from the vertex angle to the base is also the median to the base.
• **Area Connection**: The area of a triangle is calculated using the altitude: Area = 1/2 × Base × Altitude.
