• **Definition of Modals**: Modals, also known as modal auxiliary verbs, are special types of helping verbs that express the mode or manner of the action indicated by the main verb.
• **Role in Sentences**: Unlike primary auxiliary verbs (be, do, have), modals provide additional meaning such as ability, possibility, permission, obligation, or necessity.
• **The "Helping" Nature**: Modals cannot stand alone as the main verb in a sentence; they must always be followed by a principal (main) verb in its base form.
• **Invariant Form**: Modals do not change their form regardless of the person (first, second, or third) or the number (singular or plural) of the subject.
• **No Suffixes**: Modals never take suffixes like ‘-s’, ‘-es’, ‘-ing’, or ‘-ed’; for example, we say "he can," never "he cans" or "he canning."
• **Base Verb Requirement**: A modal is always followed by the root form of the verb (the infinitive without 'to'), such as in "I must go" rather than "I must to go."
• **Expressing Mood**: The term "modal" is derived from "mood," indicating that these verbs reflect the speaker's attitude or feelings toward the action being described.
• **List of Primary Modals**: The core modals covered in the Class 6 curriculum include Can, Could, May, Might, Will, Shall, Should, Must, and Ought to.
• **Semi-Modals**: Certain expressions like 'Need', 'Dare', and 'Used to' are called semi-modals because they sometimes function as modals and sometimes as main verbs.
• **Negative Formation**: To make a modal sentence negative, the word ‘not’ is placed immediately after the modal verb (e.g., cannot, should not, must not).
• **Interrogative Formation**: In questions, the modal verb is moved to the beginning of the sentence, before the subject (e.g., "Can you help me?").
• **Lack of Participles**: Modals do not have present participle or past participle forms; there is no such thing as "musting" or "shoulded."
• **Function of Ability**: One of the primary functions of modals is to indicate whether a subject has the physical or mental power to perform an action.
• **Function of Permission**: Modals are used to seek, give, or refuse permission in various social contexts, ranging from informal to very formal.
• **Function of Possibility**: Modals help express how likely it is that an event will occur, ranging from weak possibility to strong certainty.
• **Function of Obligation**: Modals like 'must' and 'should' are used to express duties, rules, or moral requirements.
• **Contextual Meaning**: The meaning of a modal often changes based on the context of the sentence; for instance, 'can' can mean both ability and permission.

• **The Modal 'Can' - General Ability**: 'Can' is primarily used to express a general ability or capacity to do something in the present time.
• **Mental Ability**: 'Can' describes cognitive skills, such as "She can solve complex mathematical equations easily."
• **Physical Ability**: 'Can' describes physical power or skills, such as "He can swim across the river" or "I can lift this heavy box."
• **Learned Skills**: 'Can' is used for skills acquired through practice, such as "They can speak three different languages fluently."
• **Informal Permission**: In casual or informal settings, 'can' is frequently used to ask for or give permission, such as "Can I use your pen?"
• **Granting Permission**: When someone is allowed to do something, 'can' is used to signify that right, e.g., "You can go to the park after finishing your homework."
• **Prohibition (Cannot)**: The negative form 'cannot' or 'can't' is used to refuse permission or state that something is forbidden, e.g., "You cannot enter the lab."
• **General Possibility**: 'Can' is used to express statements that are generally true or possible, such as "It can get very cold in the mountains during winter."
• **Theoretical Possibility**: It describes things that are theoretically possible even if they don't happen all the time, e.g., "Electricity can be dangerous."
• **Informal Requests**: 'Can' is used to make informal requests to friends or family, such as "Can you pass me the salt, please?"
• **Informal Offers**: 'Can' is used when offering help in a friendly manner, such as "Can I help you carry those bags?"
• **Sensory Perception**: 'Can' is often used with verbs of the senses like see, hear, feel, smell, and taste (e.g., "I can smell something burning").
• **Present Tense Focus**: It is important to note that 'can' refers strictly to the present or future capability, never the past.
• **Contraction**: In spoken English, 'cannot' is almost always contracted to 'can't', though 'cannot' remains the standard for formal writing.
• **Difference from 'May'**: While 'can' is used for informal permission, 'may' is reserved for formal situations, a key distinction in DAV English Practice.
• **Subject-Verb Agreement**: Regardless of whether the subject is 'I', 'You', 'He', or 'They', the form 'can' remains identical.
• **Immediate Action**: 'Can' often implies an immediate ability to perform an action right now.

• **The Modal 'Could' - Past Ability**: 'Could' is the past tense form of 'can' and is used to describe abilities that a person had in the past.
• **General Past Capacity**: It refers to a skill or power someone possessed over a period of time, e.g., "When I was young, I could run very fast."
• **Specific Past Instances**: While 'could' shows general ability, it is often replaced by "was able to" for specific successful achievements in the past.
• **Polite Requests**: 'Could' is considered more polite and formal than 'can' when asking someone to do something, e.g., "Could you please open the window?"
• **Polite Permission**: When seeking permission from a stranger or an authority figure, 'could' is a softer, more respectful choice than 'can'.
• **Remote Possibility**: 'Could' expresses a weaker or more uncertain possibility than 'can', suggesting that something might happen but is not certain.
• **Hypothetical Situations**: 'Could' is used in conditional sentences to describe what would be possible if certain conditions were met.
• **Suggestions**: 'Could' is used to make gentle or tentative suggestions, such as "We could go to the cinema if you are bored."
• **Past Permission**: 'Could' can be used to report that someone was allowed to do something in the past, e.g., "In my old school, we could wear casual clothes."
• **Negative Form (Could not)**: The negative 'could not' or 'couldn't' indicates a lack of ability in the past, e.g., "I couldn't find my keys yesterday."
• **Expressing Annoyance**: Sometimes 'could' is used to express mild annoyance in a request, such as "You could at least say thank you!"
• **Comparison with 'Can'**: While 'can' is direct and certain, 'could' is indirect, tentative, and more socially cautious.
• **Could with Senses**: Just like 'can', 'could' is used with verbs of perception to describe past experiences, e.g., "I could hear music coming from the house."
• **Future Possibility**: Interestingly, 'could' can also refer to the future when the outcome is uncertain, e.g., "It could rain later this evening."
• **Structure in Questions**: To form a question, 'could' is placed before the subject: "Could (Modal) + Subject + Verb (Base)?"
• **Formal Correspondence**: In formal letters or emails, 'could' is the preferred modal for making requests to ensure a professional tone.
• **Inability in the Past**: 'Couldn't' is the standard way to express that an attempt was made in the past but failed.

• **The Modal 'May' - Formal Permission**: 'May' is the standard modal for seeking and granting permission in formal settings, such as between a student and a teacher.
• **Asking Permission**: The classic example used in schools is "May I come in, Sir?" which shows respect and follows formal etiquette.
• **Granting Permission**: An authority figure responds with 'may' to give consent, e.g., "Yes, you may leave the room now."
• **Refusing Permission**: 'May not' is used to formally deny a request, e.g., "You may not use your mobile phones during the exam."
• **High Probability**: 'May' expresses a strong possibility that something is true or will happen, e.g., "It may rain today" (indicating a 50% or higher chance).
• **Expressing Wishes**: 'May' is uniquely used at the beginning of a sentence to express a wish, prayer, or blessing, e.g., "May God bless you!"
• **Purpose Clauses**: 'May' is used in clauses that express purpose, often following 'so that', e.g., "We eat so that we may live."
• **The Modal 'Might' - Past of May**: 'Might' is technically the past tense of 'may', used primarily in indirect speech (e.g., He said that it might rain).
• **Weak Possibility**: 'Might' indicates a very low or remote possibility, much weaker than 'may', e.g., "If I win the lottery, I might buy a car."
• **Extremely Polite Requests**: 'Might' can be used to ask for permission or make a request in an extremely hesitant or overly formal way.
• **Suggestions with Might**: 'Might' is used to make very tentative suggestions, such as "You might want to try this cake; it's quite good."
• **Might vs. May in Probability**: In the DAV curriculum, the distinction is clear: 'May' is for likely events, while 'Might' is for unlikely or distant events.
• **Negative Possibility**: 'May not' and 'might not' are used to say that it is possible something will not happen.
• **Conditional Contexts**: 'Might' is frequently used in the "if-clause" structures to show what would happen in an unlikely scenario.
• **Formal Prohibitions**: In formal notices, 'may not' is used to state rules, e.g., "Visitors may not feed the animals."
• **Interrogative Usage**: While "May I...?" is very common, "Might I...?" is rare and used only in very specific, high-formality British English contexts.
• **Base Verb Rule**: Like all modals, both 'may' and 'might' are followed by the first form of the verb without 'to'.

• **The Modal 'Will' - Future Certainty**: 'Will' is used to express pure future actions that are certain to happen, e.g., "The sun will rise at 6:00 AM."
• **Determination and Resolve**: 'Will' expresses a strong intention or a firm decision made at the moment of speaking, e.g., "I will finish this work tonight."
• **Promises**: 'Will' is the standard modal for making promises to others, such as "I will help you with your project."
• **Willingness**: It indicates a person's readiness to do something, e.g., "I will carry the bags for you."
• **Predictions**: 'Will' is used to make predictions about the future based on belief or knowledge, e.g., "I think India will win the match."
• **Characteristics of 'Will'**: It is used with second person (you) and third person (he, she, it, they) subjects for simple future.
• **Emphasis with First Person**: When 'will' is used with 'I' or 'We', it shifts from simple future to expressing a strong promise or threat.
• **The Modal 'Shall' - First Person Future**: Traditionally, 'shall' is used with 'I' and 'We' to express simple future time in formal English.
• **Offers of Help**: 'Shall' is commonly used in questions to offer help or service, e.g., "Shall I open the door for you?"
• **Suggestions**: 'Shall' is used with 'We' to make suggestions or ask for advice, e.g., "Shall we go for a walk?"
• **Legal and Formal Rules**: In formal documents or commands, 'shall' is used to express a mandatory requirement, e.g., "The students shall maintain silence."
• **Asking for Advice**: 'Shall' is used to seek instructions, such as "What shall I do now?"
• **Negative Forms**: The negative of 'will' is 'will not' (won't), and the negative of 'shall' is 'shall not' (shan't).
• **Modern Usage Note**: In modern conversational English, 'will' has largely replaced 'shall' for the first person, except in offers and suggestions.
• **Threats**: 'Will' can be used to express a threat, e.g., "I will report you to the principal if you do that again."
• **Habitual Actions**: 'Will' can describe characteristic habits or predictable behaviors, e.g., "A cat will always land on its feet."
• **Requesting Action**: 'Will you...?' is a common way to ask someone to do something, e.g., "Will you please shut the door?"

• **The Modal 'Should' - Giving Advice**: 'Should' is the most common modal used to give or ask for advice and suggestions.
• **Moral Obligation**: It expresses what is the right or best thing to do in a given situation, e.g., "You should respect your elders."
• **Duty and Responsibility**: 'Should' indicates a sense of duty, though it is less forceful than 'must', e.g., "We should keep our city clean."
• **Logical Expectation**: 'Should' is used to say that something is likely to happen based on a logical deduction, e.g., "He has studied hard; he should pass."
• **Asking for Suggestions**: When we are unsure of a course of action, we use 'should', e.g., "Should I wear a coat today?"
• **Softened Commands**: Instead of giving a direct order, 'should' is used to make a command sound like a recommendation.
• **After 'Lest'**: In formal English, 'should' is used after the word 'lest' to express fear of a negative outcome, e.g., "Walk carefully lest you should fall."
• **Past Obligation (Should have)**: To express that something was a good idea in the past but was not done, we use 'should have + V3'.
• **Negative Form (Should not)**: 'Should not' or 'shouldn't' is used to advise against doing something, e.g., "You shouldn't eat too much junk food."
• **Comparison with 'Must'**: While 'must' implies a compulsory rule, 'should' implies a personal recommendation or a moral choice.
• **Polite Instructions**: Teachers often use 'should' to guide students without being overly harsh, e.g., "You should write your name on the top left."
• **Expressing Opinion**: 'Should' is used to state a personal opinion about what is ideal, e.g., "There should be more parks in this area."
• **Conditional Sentences**: 'Should' can occasionally be used in place of 'if' at the start of a sentence for formal conditions, e.g., "Should you need help, call me."
• **Correctness**: It is used to indicate that something is not right or not as it should be, e.g., "The light should be green, but it's red."
• **Frequency in DAV Exercises**: In the Practice Book, 'should' is frequently tested in the context of health advice and school rules.
• **Invariable Nature**: Like other modals, 'should' does not change for singular or plural subjects.
• **Base Verb Consistency**: Always follow 'should' with the base form (e.g., "should go," not "should goes").

• **The Modal 'Must' - Strong Compulsion**: 'Must' is used to express a very strong obligation or a requirement that cannot be ignored.
• **Internal Obligation**: It often reflects the speaker's own feelings that something is necessary, e.g., "I must finish this book today."
• **Rules and Laws**: 'Must' is used to state official rules or laws that are mandatory, e.g., "Drivers must stop at the red light."
• **Prohibition (Must not)**: The negative 'must not' or 'mustn't' is used to express that something is strictly forbidden, e.g., "You must not smoke here."
• **Logical Certainty**: 'Must' is used to express a strong belief that something is true based on evidence, e.g., "She is shivering; she must be cold."
• **Strong Recommendation**: When we feel very strongly about an advice, we use 'must' instead of 'should', e.g., "You must see that movie; it's amazing."
• **Necessity**: It indicates that an action is essential for a particular result, e.g., "Plants must have water to grow."
• **Difference from 'Should'**: 'Should' is optional advice; 'Must' is a non-negotiable requirement.
• **Fixed Obligation**: 'Must' does not have a past tense form; for past obligation, we usually switch to 'had to'.
• **Interrogative Use**: 'Must' can be used in questions to ask if something is necessary, though it often sounds quite formal, e.g., "Must I attend the meeting?"
• **Written Orders**: 'Must' is frequently found in written instructions, such as exam papers: "Candidates must answer all questions."
• **Urgent Invitations**: 'Must' is used to give a warm, insistent invitation, e.g., "You must come and have dinner with us sometime."
• **Deduction of Fact**: It is used for making a logical conclusion, e.g., "The ground is wet; it must have rained."
• **No 'To' After Must**: A common error is saying "must to go"; the correct form is always "must go."
• **Emphasis on Authority**: When a teacher says "You must submit your work," it implies a deadline with consequences.
• **Absolute Prohibition**: 'Mustn't' is the strongest way to tell someone not to do something.
• **Subjective vs. Objective**: 'Must' is often subjective (the speaker's authority), whereas 'have to' is often objective (external rules).

• **The Modal 'Ought to' - Moral Duty**: 'Ought to' is very similar to 'should' but carries a stronger sense of moral obligation or social duty.
• **Social Expectations**: It is used to express what is socially or morally right, e.g., "We ought to help the poor."
• **Advice Based on Standards**: 'Ought to' suggests that an action is the right thing to do according to a standard of behavior.
• **Logical Probability**: Like 'should', 'ought to' can express that something is likely, e.g., "The train ought to be here by now."
• **The 'To' Requirement**: Unlike other modals, 'ought' is always followed by the particle 'to' before the main verb.
• **Negative Form**: The negative is 'ought not to', e.g., "You ought not to speak to your parents like that."
• **Interrogative Form**: In questions, the subject is placed between 'ought' and 'to', e.g., "Ought we to call him?" (though this is rare in modern speech).
• **'Have to' - External Obligation**: While not a pure modal, 'have to' functions like one to express necessity coming from outside circumstances.
• **Rules and Regulations**: 'Have to' is used for obligations imposed by others, e.g., "I have to wear a uniform at school."
• **Changing with Subject**: Unlike true modals, 'have to' changes its form: 'has to' for third-person singular and 'have to' for others.
• **Past Form (Had to)**: 'Had to' is used to express an obligation that existed in the past, e.g., "Yesterday, I had to stay back late."
• **Future Form (Will have to)**: To express a future necessity, we use 'will have to', e.g., "You will have to work harder next term."
• **Negative (Don't have to)**: 'Don't have to' means there is no obligation; it is optional, e.g., "You don't have to come if you are busy."
• **Must vs. Have to**: Use 'must' when the speaker decides it's necessary; use 'have to' when someone else or a rule decides it.
• **Questions with 'Have to'**: Questions are formed using 'do/does/did', e.g., "Do you have to leave so early?"
• **Emphasis on Necessity**: 'Have to' is often used for very practical, everyday requirements.
• **DAV Practice Focus**: The distinction between 'must' (internal) and 'have to' (external) is a key concept in the Class 6 Practice Book.

• **The Semi-Modal 'Need'**: 'Need' is used to express necessity or requirement.
• **Need as a Modal**: In its modal form, it is mostly used in negative sentences and questions, e.g., "You need not go there."
• **Need as a Main Verb**: As a main verb, it is followed by 'to' and changes with the subject, e.g., "He needs to study."
• **Absence of Necessity**: 'Need not' (needn't) indicates that there is no obligation to do something, e.g., "You needn't pay the bill now."
• **The Semi-Modal 'Dare'**: 'Dare' means "to have the courage to do something."
• **Dare in Questions**: It is often used to ask if someone has the boldness to do something, e.g., "How dare you touch my books?"
• **Dare in Negatives**: 'Dare not' (daren't) expresses a lack of courage, e.g., "I daren't tell him the truth."
