• **Capacity**: The total amount of liquid a container can hold when it is completely full.
• **Liquid**: A state of matter that flows and takes the shape of the container it is poured into.
• **Measurement of Capacity**: The process of determining how much liquid fits inside a vessel, such as a bottle, bucket, or cup.
• **Standard Units**: Units used globally to measure capacity, primarily Liters and Milliliters.
• **Non-Standard Units**: Informal ways of measuring capacity using everyday objects like cups, spoons, or bowls.
• **Container**: Any object designed to hold or store substances, specifically liquids in this context.
• **Volume**: Often used interchangeably with capacity in primary mathematics, referring to the space occupied by a liquid.
• **Full Capacity**: The state where a container is filled to its maximum limit without overflowing.
• **Empty**: A state where a container holds no liquid at all.
• **Half-Full**: A state where a container holds exactly 50% of its total capacity.
• **Comparison**: The act of checking which container holds more or less liquid than another.
• **Estimation**: Making an educated guess about the capacity of a container before measuring it.
• **Visual Inspection**: Observing the size and shape of a container to predict its capacity.
• **Water**: The most common liquid used for experiments and demonstrations in primary mathematics.
• **Practical Application**: Understanding that different containers serve different purposes based on their capacity.

• **Liter (L)**: The standard unit of measurement for larger quantities of liquid.
• **Milliliter (mL)**: The standard unit of measurement for smaller quantities of liquid.
• **Relationship**: 1 Liter is equal to 1000 Milliliters.
• **Conversion Principle**: To convert Liters to Milliliters, we multiply the number of Liters by 1000.
• **Small Quantities**: Milliliters are used for items like medicine spoons, eye drops, or small juice boxes.
• **Large Quantities**: Liters are used for items like water tanks, buckets, or large soda bottles.
• **Measuring Tools**: Specialized equipment used to measure capacity accurately, such as measuring cylinders or jugs.
• **Graduated Markings**: Lines on a measuring tool that indicate specific volumes of liquid.
• **Accuracy**: The importance of reading the level of liquid at eye level to ensure correct measurement.
• **Meniscus**: The curve seen at the top of a liquid in a container; measurement should be taken from the bottom of this curve.
• **Standardization**: Why we use Liters and Milliliters instead of cups or spoons to ensure consistency.
• **Global Consistency**: The reason why a Liter is the same amount of liquid in every country.
• **Abbreviation**: 'L' is the standard symbol for Liter.
• **Abbreviation**: 'mL' is the standard symbol for Milliliter.
• **Prefix 'Milli'**: Derived from Latin, meaning one-thousandth part.

• **Non-Standard Measurement**: Using arbitrary units like a glass, a mug, or a ladle to fill a larger container.
• **Variability**: The main drawback of non-standard units is that different glasses have different sizes.
• **Consistency Issue**: If you use a small cup vs. a large cup, the number of units required to fill a bucket will change.
• **Introduction to Standard Units**: Why we move from non-standard to standard units in mathematics.
• **Experimentation**: Filling a bucket with a mug and counting how many mugs it takes to reach the top.
• **Direct Comparison**: Placing two containers side-by-side to see which one is taller or wider.
• **Pouring Method**: Transferring liquid from one container to another to determine which holds more.
• **Overflow**: What happens when you add more liquid than a container’s capacity can hold.
• **Spillage**: The loss of liquid during the transfer process, which affects measurement accuracy.
• **Uniformity**: The goal of using standard units is to ensure everyone gets the same amount.
• **Household Examples**: Identifying items in the kitchen that hold 1 Liter, such as milk cartons.
• **Household Examples**: Identifying items that hold less than 1 Liter, such as a shampoo bottle.

• **Logical Sequence**: Understanding that smaller containers usually have smaller capacities.

• **Addition of Capacity**: Combining the volumes of two or more containers to find the total capacity.
• **Addition Rule**: Always add Liters to Liters and Milliliters to Milliliters.
• **Regrouping**: If the sum of Milliliters reaches or exceeds 1000, it must be converted into Liters.
• **Example**: 500 mL + 600 mL = 1100 mL, which is 1 Liter and 100 mL.
• **Column Addition**: Setting up capacity problems in columns to prevent calculation errors.
• **Subtraction of Capacity**: Finding the difference between the capacities of two containers.
• **Subtraction Rule**: Subtract Milliliters from Milliliters and Liters from Liters.
• **Borrowing**: If the Milliliters in the top number are smaller than the bottom, borrow 1 Liter (1000 mL) from the Liter column.
• **Word Problems**: Translating real-life scenarios into mathematical addition or subtraction equations.
• **Key Phrases**: Words like "total," "altogether," and "sum" indicate addition.
• **Key Phrases**: Words like "left," "difference," and "how much more" indicate subtraction.
• **Mathematical Logic**: Ensuring that the units are identical before performing any arithmetic operation.
• **Verification**: Checking the answer by ensuring the total capacity makes sense in a real-world context.
• **Precision**: The importance of keeping track of units throughout the entire calculation process.
• **Step-by-Step Method**: Breaking down complex word problems into smaller, manageable steps.
