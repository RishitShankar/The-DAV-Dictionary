• **Chapter Overview**: Introduction to the fundamental chemical categories of acids, bases, and salts as presented in the DAV Class 7 Science curriculum, emphasizing their presence in our daily lives and surroundings.
• **Introduction to Matter**: Matter in our environment exhibits diverse chemical properties, which allow scientists to classify substances into acidic, basic, or neutral categories based on their taste, touch, and interaction with other chemicals.
• **Sensory Perception Limitations**: Direct tasting or touching of unknown chemical substances is strictly prohibited in science laboratories because many acids and bases are corrosive, toxic, or capable of causing severe skin burns and tissue damage.
• **Definition of Acids**: **Acids** are chemical substances that possess a characteristic sour taste and are capable of turning blue litmus paper red.
• **Etymology of Acid**: The word 'acid' is derived from the Latin word *‘acere’*, which literally translates to 'sour', reflecting the primary gustatory property observed in early chemistry.
• **Natural Occurrence (Organic Acids)**: Acids found naturally in plants and animals are termed **organic acids**, which are generally weak and safe to consume in dietary sources.
• **Examples of Organic Acids**: **Citric acid** is naturally present in citrus fruits like lemons, oranges, and grapefruits, contributing to their refreshing sourness.
• **Acetic Acid**: **Acetic acid** is the primary active and sour component found in vinegar, widely used for food preservation and culinary seasoning.
• **Lactic Acid**: **Lactic acid** is produced during the curdling of milk and is present in sour milk and yoghurt, and also accumulates in human muscles during strenuous exercise.
• **Formic Acid (Methanoic Acid)**: **Formic acid** is injected by ants and stinging nettle leaves when they sting or scratch, causing localized pain, irritation, and skin inflammation.
• **Tartaric Acid**: **Tartaric acid** is an organic acid found abundantly in grapes, tamarind, and unripe mangoes, frequently used in baking powder formulations.
• **Ascorbic Acid**: **Ascorbic acid**, commonly known as Vitamin C, is essential for human health and is naturally present in amla, citrus fruits, and green leafy vegetables.
• **Mineral Acids (Inorganic Acids)**: Acids prepared from minerals found in the Earth's crust are called **mineral acids** or **inorganic acids**, which are typically strong and highly corrosive.
• **Examples of Mineral Acids**: **Hydrochloric acid ($HCl$)**, **sulphuric acid ($H_2SO_4$)**, and **nitric acid ($HNO_3$)** are the three most common and industrially important mineral acids.
• **Sulphuric Acid Application**: **Sulphuric acid** is often referred to as the 'King of Chemicals' because of its extensive usage in fertilizer manufacturing, paints, plastics, and car batteries.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Nitric Acid Utility**: **Nitric acid** is a powerful mineral acid utilized extensively in the production of fertilizers such as ammonium nitrate, as well as in the manufacturing of explosives like TNT.
• **Hydrochloric Acid in Industry and Biology**: **Hydrochloric acid** is used for cleaning metal surfaces before galvanization and is also naturally secreted in the human stomach to aid digestion.
• **Concentrated vs. Dilute Acids**: An acid is designated as **concentrated** when it contains a very small amount of water relative to the acid, and **dilute** when a large volume of water is added to reduce its strength.
• **Safety Rule for Dilution**: Dilution of concentrated mineral acids must always be performed by slowly pouring the acid into water with constant stirring, never by adding water directly to the acid.
• **Exothermic Dilution Process**: Adding water to concentrated acid releases an enormous amount of heat exothermically, which can cause the solution to splash violently and break glassware or burn the experimenter.
• **Corrosive Nature of Mineral Acids**: Mineral acids are highly corrosive in nature, meaning they eat away at metals, fabrics, wood, and human skin upon direct contact.
• **Definition of Bases**: **Bases** are chemical substances that have a bitter taste, feel soapy or slippery to the touch, and turn red litmus paper blue.
• **Nature of Basic Substances**: Basic substances are chemically opposite to acids and are frequently encountered in cleaning agents, soaps, and antacids.
• **Water-Soluble Bases (Alkalis)**: Bases that are completely soluble in water are specifically termed **alkalis**, and all alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis due to varying solubility.
• **Sodium Hydroxide ($NaOH$)**: **Sodium hydroxide**, also known as caustic soda, is a strong alkali used in the manufacturing of soaps, paper, and artificial silk fibers.
• **Potassium Hydroxide ($KOH$)**: **Potassium hydroxide**, known as caustic potash, is a strong alkali utilized heavily in the production of liquid soaps, shampoos, and alkaline storage batteries.
• **Calcium Hydroxide ($Ca(OH)_2$)**: **Calcium hydroxide**, commonly called slaked lime, is used in whitewashing walls, manufacturing bleaching powder, and treating acidic soils.
• **Magnesium Hydroxide ($Mg(OH)_2$)**: **Magnesium hydroxide**, known commonly as milk of magnesia, is a mild base used as an antacid to neutralize excess stomach acidity and relieve indigestion.
• **Ammonium Hydroxide ($NH_4OH$)**: **Ammonium hydroxide** is a common weak base found in window cleaners and hair dyes, effective at removing grease and stubborn stains.
• **Corrosive Nature of Strong Bases**: Strong bases like sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are highly caustic and can severely burn human skin and damage organic tissues.

• **Definition of Indicators**: **Indicators** are special chemical substances that exhibit different colors when introduced into acidic or basic mediums, allowing safe identification of chemical nature.
• **Classification of Indicators**: Indicators are broadly classified into **natural indicators** (obtained from plants), **synthetic indicators** (man-made chemicals), and **olfactory indicators** (detected by smell).
• **Litmus as a Natural Indicator**: **Litmus** is the most widely used natural indicator, extracted from symbiotic organisms called **lichens** belonging to the division Thallophyta.
• **Litmus Solution and Strips**: Litmus is available commercially as a neutral purple liquid solution or impregnated onto paper strips as red litmus paper and blue litmus paper.
• **Action of Acids on Litmus**: Acids turn **blue litmus paper red**, while leaving red litmus paper completely unchanged in color.
• **Action of Bases on Litmus**: Bases turn **red litmus paper blue**, while leaving blue litmus paper completely unaffected.
• **Neutral Substances on Litmus**: Neutral substances do not cause any color change on either red or blue litmus paper, maintaining their original appearance.
• **Turmeric as a Natural Indicator**: **Turmeric** (haldi) is a yellow natural indicator that turns bright red or reddish-brown when exposed to basic solutions, but remains yellow in acidic or neutral solutions.
• **Practical Demonstration of Turmeric**: Yellow turmeric stains on white clothes turn reddish-brown when washed with basic soap, and revert to yellow once rinsed thoroughly with plenty of water.
• **China Rose Indicator**: **China rose** (Hibiscus) petals can be soaked in warm water to yield a light pink indicator solution that turns dark pink (magenta) in acidic solutions and green in basic solutions.
• **Red Cabbage Indicator**: Extracts from **red cabbage** leaves act as natural indicators, turning red in acidic environments, purple in neutral environments, and green or yellow in basic environments.
• **Olfactory Indicators**: **Olfactory indicators** are substances whose characteristic odor changes or disappears when they are mixed with acidic or basic solutions.
• **Examples of Olfactory Indicators**: **Vanilla extract**, **onion juice**, and **clove oil** are classic examples of olfactory indicators used by visually impaired students in laboratories.
• **Behavior of Onion Juice with Acids and Bases**: Onion juice retains its distinct pungent smell in an acidic medium, but its smell is completely destroyed or neutralized when placed in a basic medium.
• **Phenolphthalein as Synthetic Indicator**: **Phenolphthalein** is a synthetic laboratory indicator that remains colorless in acidic and neutral solutions, but turns a striking pink in basic solutions.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Methyl Orange**: **Methyl orange** is another synthetic laboratory indicator that imparts a red color to acidic solutions and turns yellow in basic solutions.
• **Universal Indicator**: A **universal indicator** is a specialized mixture of several different indicators that provides a wide spectrum of distinct colors corresponding to different pH values of a solution.
• **Concept of Neutralization**: **Neutralization** is a chemical reaction in which an acid and a base react completely with each other to form a salt and water, accompanied by the release of heat.
• **General Word Equation for Neutralization**: **Acid + Base $\rightarrow$ Salt + Water + Heat**, representing the fundamental stoichiometry of a standard neutralization process.
• **Nature of Products in Neutralization**: The salt formed during neutralization can be acidic, basic, or neutral depending upon the relative strengths of the reacting acids and bases.
• **Exothermic Nature of Neutralization**: The neutralization reaction is always **exothermic**, meaning thermal energy is liberated, causing the reaction mixture to become noticeably warm to the touch.
• **Laboratory Demonstration of Neutralization**: Dilute hydrochloric acid is added drop-by-drop to dilute sodium hydroxide solution containing phenolphthalein, observing the sudden transition from pink to colorless at the exact neutralization point.
• **Digestive System and Acidity**: Human stomachs naturally produce hydrochloric acid to digest food, but overeating, stress, or spicy food can trigger excessive acid production, leading to painful acidity or indigestion.
• **Role of Antacids**: **Antacids** are mild, safe basic substances such as milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) or sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) administered orally to neutralize excess stomach acid.
• **Mechanism of Antacid Action**: Antacids work by chemically reacting with excess stomach acid to raise the pH back to a comfortable, neutral level, thus providing immediate relief from burning sensations.
• **Ant Sting Treatment**: Ant stings inject acidic **formic acid** into human skin, which causes localized pain that can be immediately neutralized and alleviated by rubbing basic substances like moist baking soda or calamine lotion.
• **Calamine Lotion Composition**: Calamine lotion contains **zinc carbonate**, a mild basic compound that helps soothe and neutralize the acid from insect stings and plant scratches.
• **Wasp Sting Treatment**: Wasp stings are alkaline in nature; therefore, they require treatment with a weak acid such as **dilute vinegar** (acetic acid) to neutralize the venom and reduce pain.
• **Soil Treatment in Agriculture**: Plants require neutral soil for optimal growth, but excessive use of chemical fertilizers or acid rain can make agricultural soil too acidic for crops to thrive.
• **Correction of Acidic Soil**: When soil becomes too acidic, farmers treat it by mixing in basic substances such as **quicklime (calcium oxide)** or **slaked lime (calcium hydroxide)** to restore optimal pH balance.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Correction of Basic Soil**: If agricultural soil becomes excessively basic due to alkaline mineral deposits, organic matter such as **compost or manure** is added to release acids that neutralize the excess alkalinity.
• **Factory Waste Management**: Industrial wastes discharged from factories often contain high concentrations of toxic mineral acids that can destroy aquatic life if dumped directly into water bodies.
• **Treatment of Factory Effluents**: Industrial acidic wastes are strictly neutralized by treating them with basic chemicals such as **slaked lime** before releasing them safely into rivers, lakes, or sewage systems.
• **Definition of Salts**: **Salts** are ionic chemical compounds formed by the neutralization reaction between an acid and a base, consisting of positively charged metal ions and negatively charged non-metal ions.
• **Common Salt ($NaCl$)**: **Sodium chloride**, commonly known as table salt or common salt, is the most abundant and familiar salt used worldwide in food seasoning and preservation.
• **Sources of Common Salt**: Common salt is obtained naturally from seawater through evaporation, from underground rock salt deposits mined mechanically, and from natural inland saltwater lakes.
• **Rock Salt Deposits**: **Rock salt** is formed due to the evaporation of ancient seas over millions of years, often appearing as large brown crystals mixed with impurities.
• **Importance of Sodium Chloride**: Sodium chloride is vital for human survival, acting as an electrolyte in body fluids, preserving perishable foods, and serving as a raw material in the chemical industry.
• **Chlor-Alkali Process**: The electrolysis of aqueous sodium chloride (brine) produces **chlorine gas**, **hydrogen gas**, and **sodium hydroxide**, forming the backbone of the chlor-alkali industrial process.
• **Washing Soda**: **Sodium carbonate decahydrate**, commonly known as **washing soda**, is a useful commercial salt utilized in glass manufacturing, paper production, and softening hard water.
• **Baking Soda**: **Sodium bicarbonate**, known as **baking soda**, is a mild basic salt used in baking cakes and pastries, and as a key ingredient in fire extinguishers and antacids.
• **Baking Powder Composition**: **Baking powder** is a dry mixture of baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) and a mild edible acid such as tartaric acid, which releases carbon dioxide gas when mixed with water and heated.
• **Role of Baking Powder in Baking**: The carbon dioxide gas trapped inside dough expands upon heating in an oven, making cakes, bread, and pastries rise and become soft, spongy, and porous.
• **Bleaching Powder**: **Calcium oxychloride**, commonly known as **bleaching powder**, is manufactured by passing chlorine gas over dry slaked lime and is used for bleaching cotton, linen, and disinfecting drinking water.
• **Plaster of Paris**: **Calcium sulphate hemihydrate**, known as **Plaster of Paris**, is prepared by heating gypsum at $373\text{ K}$, and sets into a hard solid mass when mixed with water.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Applications of Plaster of Paris**: Plaster of Paris is used by doctors to immobilize fractured bones in a fixed position, by sculptors to make decorative casts, and by builders to smooth wall surfaces.
• **Water of Crystallization**: **Water of crystallization** refers to the fixed number of water molecules chemically bound in a crystalline structure of a salt, as seen in copper sulphate pentahydrate ($CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$).
• **Hydrated vs. Anhydrous Salts**: Salts containing water of crystallization are termed **hydrated salts** (which are often colored), whereas salts that have lost their water molecules are termed **anhydrous salts** (often white or colorless).
• **Thermal Dehydration of Blue Vitriol**: When blue copper sulphate crystals ($CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$) are heated gently, they lose their water of crystallization, turning from brilliant blue to a powdery white anhydrous form.
• **Reversal of Dehydration**: Adding a few drops of water to anhydrous white copper sulphate immediately restores its blue color and releases heat, proving that water molecules are structurally integrated into the crystal.
• **Gypsum Chemical Structure**: **Gypsum** is calcium sulphate dihydrate ($CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O$), which contains two molecules of water of crystallization bound within its calcium sulphate lattice.
• **Preparation Reaction of Plaster of Paris**: Heating gypsum to $100^\circ\text{C}$ ($373\text{ K}$) causes it to lose three-fourths of its water of crystallization, forming Plaster of Paris ($CaSO_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}H_2O$).
• **Setting Reaction of Plaster of Paris**: When Plaster of Paris is mixed with water, it reabsorbs water molecules to reform gypsum within minutes, expanding slightly and setting into a rigid, hard mass.
• **Acid Rain Phenomenon**: **Acid rain** is caused when rainwater mixes with high concentrations of dissolved sulphur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide gases released from burning fossil fuels and vehicle exhaust.
• **Environmental Impact of Acid Rain**: Acid rain significantly lowers the pH of soil and water bodies, corrodes historical marble and limestone monuments like the Taj Mahal, and harms forests and aquatic life.
• **Taj Mahal Marble Decay**: The marble of the Taj Mahal is calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$), which reacts with atmospheric acid rain to form soluble sulphates, leading to corrosion, yellowing, and degradation of the stone surface.
• **Antacid Safety and Caution**: While antacids provide quick relief from acidity, relying on them excessively without addressing the root cause of indigestion can mask underlying medical conditions like stomach ulcers.
• **Role of pH Scale in Daily Life**: The pH scale measures the relative acidity or alkalinity of a substance from $0$ (strongly acidic) to $14$ (strongly basic), with $7$ representing a perfectly neutral substance.
• **Neutral Substances on pH Scale**: Pure distilled water and common salt solutions have a neutral pH value of exactly $7$, indicating that they possess neither acidic nor basic properties.
• **Human Blood pH Range**: Human blood operates within a very narrow, strictly regulated pH range of $7.35$ to $7.45$, and any significant deviation can result in serious medical emergencies or death.

• **Tooth Decay and Acid Production**: Tooth decay is caused when bacteria in the mouth break down leftover food particles to produce acids that lower the mouth's pH below $5.5$.
• **Action of Toothpaste**: Toothpastes are generally basic in nature; brushing teeth regularly after meals neutralizes the bacterial acids in the mouth, preventing tooth enamel erosion and cavities.
• **Plant Growth and Soil pH**: Most common agricultural plants grow best in soil that is near neutral (pH between $6.5$ and $7.5$), requiring farmers to test soil acidity regularly before planting.
• **Stinging Nettle Defense**: Stinging nettle plants possess specialized hollow hairs on their leaves that inject painful methanoic acid when touched, acting as a natural self-defense mechanism against herbivores.
• **Dock Plant Remedy**: Nature often provides a cure close to hazards; leaves of the **dock plant**, which are basic in nature, grow abundantly near stinging nettles and can be rubbed on stings to provide relief.
• **Digestion in Ruminants**: Grass-eating animals (ruminants) swallow grass quickly and store it in the rumen, where fermentation by specialized microorganisms produces specific organic acids that aid cellulose digestion.
• **Natural Vinegar Production**: Vinegar is produced commercially through the two-stage fermentation of sugar-rich liquids, first converting sugars to alcohol via yeast, and then converting alcohol to acetic acid via bacteria.
• **Industrial Uses of Nitric Acid**: Beyond fertilizers and explosives, nitric acid is extensively used in the purification of noble metals like gold and silver by acting as a strong oxidizing agent.
• **Safety Precautions in Chemistry Labs**: Laboratory safety rules mandate wearing protective goggles, lab aprons, and gloves whenever handling concentrated acids or strong bases to prevent accidental chemical burns.
• **First Aid for Acid Spills**: If an acid spills accidentally on human skin, the affected area must be immediately flushed with a continuous, copious flow of cold running water, followed by washing with a mild basic solution like sodium bicarbonate.
• **First Aid for Base Spills**: If a strong base comes into contact with the skin, it must be washed immediately with plenty of running water, followed by treatment with a weak, safe acid like dilute vinegar or boric acid solution.
• **Storage of Acids and Bases**: Acids and bases must never be stored in reactive metal containers (like aluminum or iron) because they undergo chemical reactions that corrode the containers and leak chemicals; instead, they are stored in glass or specialized plastic bottles.
• **Role of Salt in Food Preservation**: Common salt acts as an effective preservative by drawing water out of microbial cells through osmosis, thereby inhibiting the growth of bacteria and fungi in pickles, fish, and meat.
• **Iodized Salt**: Common table salt is frequently fortified with small amounts of potassium iodide or potassium iodate to supply essential iodine to the human diet, preventing nutritional iodine deficiency disorders like goiter.
• **Electrolytes in Human Body**: Dissolved salts dissociate into charged ions in bodily fluids, creating essential **electrolytes** that transmit nerve impulses, regulate muscle contractions, and maintain water balance.

• **Summary of Acids**: Acids are characterized by a sour taste, turn blue litmus red, possess a pH below $7$, and are classified into organic (natural, weak) and mineral (laboratory, strong) categories.
• **Summary of Bases**: Bases are characterized by a bitter taste, soapy feel, turn red litmus blue, possess a pH above $7$, and water-soluble bases are specifically called alkalis.
• **Summary of Neutralization**: Neutralization is an exothermic reaction between an acid and a base yielding salt, water, and heat, used widely in medicine, agriculture, and industry.
• **Summary of Indicators**: Indicators are diagnostic chemical tools that change color (litmus, phenolphthalein, turmeric) or odor (onion, vanilla) to signal whether a test solution is acidic, basic, or neutral.
• **Significance of Salts in Daily Life**: Salts are ionic compounds formed during neutralization, encompassing vital substances like common salt ($NaCl$), baking soda ($NaHCO_3$), washing soda ($Na_2CO_3 \cdot 10H_2O$), and plaster of Paris ($CaSO_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}H_2O$).
• **Biological Significance of pH**: Maintaining optimal pH levels in the human stomach, blood, and oral cavity is critical for biological health, enzymatic activity, and the prevention of chronic illnesses.
• **Environmental Care and Chemistry**: Understanding the chemistry of acids, bases, and salts enables humanity to combat environmental challenges such as soil acidification, acid rain, and industrial water pollution.
• **Practical Laboratory Identification**: Students acquire practical competence in identifying unknown chemical substances safely by using standard natural and synthetic laboratory indicators under supervision.
• **Culinary Chemistry**: The culinary arts rely heavily on the chemical properties of acids (vinegar, lemon juice for tenderizing and souring) and bases (baking soda for leavening dough and softening chickpeas).
• **Role of Chemistry in Sanitation**: Basic cleaning products leverage the saponification and grease-cutting properties of alkalis to maintain hygiene in households, hospitals, and public institutions.
• **Historical Context of Chemistry**: The classification of substances into acids and bases represents one of the earliest milestones in human chemical knowledge, evolving from sensory tasting to sophisticated molecular theories.

• **Advanced Acid Properties**: Beyond sour taste, acids conduct electricity when dissolved in water because they dissociate into mobile hydrogen ions ($H^+$) and corresponding negative ions, acting as strong electrolytes.
• **Advanced Base Properties**: Aqueous solutions of bases conduct electricity due to the presence of mobile hydroxide ions ($OH^-$), allowing basic solutions to function efficiently as electrolytic conductors.
• **Strength of Acids and Bases**: The chemical strength of an acid depends directly on the degree of its ionization in water, with strong acids ionizing completely and weak acids ionizing only partially.
• **Degree of Dissociation**: Weak acids like acetic acid exhibit very low dissociation percentages in aqueous solution, whereas strong mineral acids like hydrochloric acid exhibit nearly 100% dissociation.
• **Alkali vs. Base Distinction**: All alkalis are soluble bases that release hydroxide ions ($OH^-$) in water, but insoluble metal hydroxides like copper hydroxide are classified merely as bases, not as alkalis.
• **Mechanism of Olfactory Indicators**: Olfactory indicators function by altering their volatile molecular structure in the presence of hydronium or hydroxide ions, causing an alteration or loss of their characteristic scent profile.
• **Preparation of Natural Indicators at Home**: Students can easily prepare homemade acid-base indicators by boiling red cabbage leaves or hibiscus flowers in water and decanting the colored filtrate for experiments.
• **Neutralization in Chemical Titrations**: Quantitative analysis in chemistry utilizes neutralization titrations, where an acid of known concentration is added precisely to a base of unknown concentration using a burette.
• **Endpoint of a Titration**: The exact point at which an acid and base have completely neutralized each other during a titration is called the **endpoint**, indicated immediately by a sharp color change of the added indicator.
• **Industrial Production of Sodium Carbonate**: Sodium carbonate (washing soda) is manufactured industrially through the **Solvay process**, utilizing ammonia, carbon dioxide, and sodium chloride as raw materials.
• **Properties of Crystalline Water**: Water of crystallization is not considered liquid water trapped mechanically; rather, it is chemically bonded in definite stoichiometric proportions within the crystal lattice.
• **Efflorescence in Hydrated Salts**: Certain hydrated salts lose their water of crystallization to the surrounding dry atmosphere spontaneously over time, a surface phenomenon known as **efflorescence**.
• **Deliquescence**: Certain soluble salts absorb moisture from humid air spontaneously until they dissolve completely in the absorbed water, a physical property known as **deliquescence** (e.g., calcium chloride).
• **Role of Chemistry in Agriculture**: Scientific soil testing allows farmers to apply exact quantities of lime or organic matter, preventing both over-acidification and over-alkalinization of arable farmland.
• **Conclusion of Chapter Study**: Mastery of the concepts of acids, bases, and salts equips Class 7 students with essential scientific knowledge required to interpret chemical phenomena in nature, industry, and daily human existence.

• **Chemical Reactions of Acids with Metals**: Active metals such as zinc, iron, and magnesium react vigorously with dilute mineral acids to produce a metal salt and evolve flammable **hydrogen gas** ($H_2$).
• **Identification Test for Hydrogen Gas**: The gas evolved during the reaction of an acid with a metal can be positively identified by bringing a burning matchstick near it, which extinguishes with a distinct **pop sound**.
• **Chemical Reactions of Acids with Metal Carbonates**: Acids react with metal carbonates and bicarbonates to produce a metal salt, liquid water, and brisk effervescence of **carbon dioxide gas** ($CO_2$).
• **Identification Test for Carbon Dioxide**: Carbon dioxide gas evolved during acid-carbonate reactions turns **limewater milky** due to the formation of insoluble white calcium carbonate precipitate.
• **Extended Limewater Reaction**: Passing excess carbon dioxide gas through milky limewater for an extended duration causes the milky precipitate to dissolve completely, forming soluble calcium hydrogen carbonate.
• **Reactions of Acids with Metal Oxides**: Metal oxides are generally basic in nature and react with dilute acids to produce a salt and water exclusively, behaving similarly to a standard neutralization reaction.
• **Reactions of Non-Metal Oxides with Bases**: Non-metal oxides are acidic in nature and react readily with basic solutions to yield a salt and water, confirming the acidic nature of oxides like carbon dioxide or sulphur dioxide.
• **Role of Carbon Dioxide in Global Acidification**: Increasing atmospheric carbon dioxide emissions dissolve into oceans to form carbonic acid, slowly lowering oceanic pH and threatening marine ecosystems and coral reefs.
