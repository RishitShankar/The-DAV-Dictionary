• **Chapter 16 Introduction**: Welcome to the DAV Dictionary Academic Notes for Class 5 Primary Mathematics, focusing comprehensively on Chapter 16, titled "Triangles".
• **Basic Geometric Foundation**: A triangle is introduced as one of the fundamental closed geometric figures in 2D space, possessing a specific set of properties and structures.
• **Etymology of Triangle**: The word 'triangle' literally means 'three-angled', derived from Latin roots 'tri' (three) and 'angulus' (corner or angle).
• **Core Definition**: **Triangle**: A closed two-dimensional geometric plane figure bounded by exactly three straight line segments that intersect at their endpoints.
• **Essential Components**: Every standard triangle consists of three primary structural components: three sides, three interior angles, and three vertices.
• **Definition of a Vertex**: A **Vertex** is the specific point where two straight line segments (sides of the triangle) meet or intersect.
• **Plural of Vertex**: The plural form of vertex is vertices, and a triangle invariably possesses exactly three distinct vertices.
• **Naming a Triangle**: A triangle is typically named using the capital letters assigned to its three vertices, written with a small triangle symbol ($\Delta$) in front.
• **Example of Naming**: $\Delta ABC$ denotes a triangle with vertices named $A$, $B$, and $C$.
• **Sides of $\Delta ABC$**: The three line segments forming the boundary of $\Delta ABC$ are denoted as side $AB$, side $BC$, and side $CA$.
• **Angles of $\Delta ABC$**: The three interior angles of $\Delta ABC$ are denoted as $\angle ABC$ (or $\angle B$), $\angle BCA$ (or $\angle C$), and $\angle CAB$ (or $\angle A$).
• **Non-Collinear Points**: The three vertices of any valid triangle must be non-collinear, meaning they do not lie on a single straight line.
• **Degenerate Triangles**: If three points are collinear, connecting them yields a straight line segment rather than a true geometric triangle.
• **Real-World Relevance**: Triangles are heavily emphasized in DAV curricula due to their structural rigidity and wide-scale applications in engineering and architecture.
• **Rigidity Property**: Unlike quadrilaterals or polygons with more sides, a triangle cannot be deformed without changing the lengths of its sides, making it inherently rigid.

• **Classification Overview**: Triangles are systematically classified into two main categories: classification based on the lengths of their sides, and classification based on the measures of their interior angles.
• **Side-Based Classification**: When categorized by side lengths, triangles are divided into three distinct types: Scalene, Isosceles, and Equilateral.
• **Angle-Based Classification**: When categorized by interior angles, triangles are divided into three distinct types: Acute-angled, Right-angled, and Obtuse-angled.
• **Definition of Scalene Triangle**: A **Scalene Triangle** is a triangle in which all three sides are of completely different, unequal lengths.
• **Angles in a Scalene Triangle**: Because all three sides of a scalene triangle are unequal in length, all three of its interior angles are also unequal in measure.
• **Definition of Isosceles Triangle**: An **Isosceles Triangle** is a triangle that has at least two sides of equal length.
• **Equal Sides and Angles**: In an isosceles triangle, the angles opposite to the two equal sides are also equal in measure.
• **Definition of Equilateral Triangle**: An **Equilateral Triangle** is a specialized triangle in which all three sides are exactly equal in length.
• **Equiangular Nature**: Because all three sides of an equilateral triangle are equal, all three of its interior angles are also equal to one another.
• **Measure of Equilateral Angles**: Each interior angle of a standard equilateral triangle measures exactly $60^\circ$.
• **Definition of Acute-Angled Triangle**: An **Acute-Angled Triangle** (or acute triangle) is a triangle in which all three interior angles are acute angles (each measuring less than $90^\circ$).
• **Definition of Right-Angled Triangle**: A **Right-Angled Triangle** (or right triangle) is a triangle in which exactly one interior angle measures a precise $90^\circ$ (a right angle).
• **Hypotenuse Definition**: In a right-angled triangle, the **Hypotenuse** is the side directly opposite to the right angle, representing the longest side of the triangle.
• **Definition of Obtuse-Angled Triangle**: An **Obtuse-Angled Triangle** (or obtuse triangle) is a triangle in which exactly one interior angle is an obtuse angle (measuring greater than $90^\circ$).
• **Mutual Exclusivity in Angles**: A triangle can never have more than one right angle or more than one obtuse angle due to the geometric sum constraints of its interior angles.

• **Angle Sum Property Introduction**: One of the most fundamental theorems in geometry regarding triangles is the **Angle Sum Property**.
• **Statement of Angle Sum Property**: The sum of the measures of all three interior angles of any given triangle is always equal to $180^\circ$.
• **Mathematical Expression**: For any triangle with interior angles $\angle 1$, $\angle 2$, and $\angle 3$, the equation $\angle 1 + \angle 2 + \angle 3 = 180^\circ$ always holds true.
• **Universal Applicability**: The angle sum of $180^\circ$ applies universally to all types of triangles, whether scalene, isosceles, equilateral, acute, right, or obtuse.
• **Verification Method**: Students can verify the angle sum property by drawing any triangle on paper, tearing off its three corners, and placing them adjacent to each other on a straight line.
• **Straight Line Angle Correlation**: When the three torn corners of a triangle are placed adjacent on a straight line, they form a straight angle, which measures $180^\circ$.
• **Missing Angle Calculation**: If the measures of two interior angles of a triangle are known, the third interior angle can be easily found by subtracting their sum from $180^\circ$.
• **Example Calculation**: If $\angle A = 50^\circ$ and $\angle B = 60^\circ$, then $\angle C = 180^\circ - (50^\circ + 60^\circ) = 180^\circ - 110^\circ = 70^\circ$.
• **Equilateral Angle Verification**: Using the angle sum property for an equilateral triangle, since all three angles are equal ($x + x + x = 180^\circ$), $3x = 180^\circ$, yielding $x = 60^\circ$.
• **Right Triangle Angle Property**: In a right-angled triangle, since one angle is $90^\circ$, the sum of the remaining two acute angles must equal $90^\circ$ ($180^\circ - 90^\circ$).
• **Complementary Acute Angles**: The two acute angles in any right-angled triangle are always complementary to each other because their sum is exactly $90^\circ$.
• **Maximum Angle Constraint**: No single interior angle in a triangle can ever be equal to or greater than $180^\circ$, as that would collapse the figure into a straight line.
• **Minimum Angle Constraint**: Every interior angle in a triangle must be strictly greater than $0^\circ$ to maintain valid non-zero side lengths.
• **Acute Angle Requirement**: Every triangle must possess at least two acute interior angles regardless of its classification.
• **Problem-Solving Utility**: DAV examination problems frequently test this property by providing algebraic expressions for the angles of a triangle, requiring students to solve for $x$.

• **Exterior Angle Introduction**: Beyond interior angles, triangles feature exterior angles formed by extending their sides outward.
• **Definition of Exterior Angle**: An **Exterior Angle** of a triangle is an angle formed outside the triangle by any side of the triangle and the extension of an adjacent side.
• **Formation Mechanism**: To create an exterior angle, choose any side of a triangle and extend it in a straight line beyond its vertex.
• **Adjacent Interior Angle**: The interior angle that shares the same vertex as the exterior angle is called the **Adjacent Interior Angle**.
• **Linear Pair Formation**: An exterior angle and its adjacent interior angle always form a linear pair, meaning their angle measures add up to exactly $180^\circ$.
• **Remote Interior Angles**: The two interior angles of a triangle that are not adjacent to a given exterior angle are called the **Remote Interior Angles** (or opposite interior angles).
• **Exterior Angle Theorem**: The measure of any exterior angle of a triangle is always equal to the sum of the measures of its two remote interior angles.
• **Mathematical Representation**: If an exterior angle is denoted as $\angle EXT$ and its remote interior angles are $\angle A$ and $\angle B$, then $\angle EXT = \angle A + \angle B$.
• **Inequality Relation**: Because an exterior angle equals the sum of two remote interior angles, it is always strictly greater in measure than either of the individual remote interior angles.
• **Total Exterior Angles**: Every triangle has a total of six exterior angles (two at each vertex, formed by extending the two meeting sides in opposite directions).
• **Sum of Exterior Angles**: The sum of one exterior angle at each vertex of a triangle (taken in order around the perimeter) is always equal to $360^\circ$.
• **Practical Application of Exterior Theorem**: The exterior angle theorem is heavily utilized in DAV textbook problems to quickly find unknown angles without needing to calculate adjacent interior angles first.
• **Example Application**: If the remote interior angles of a triangle measure $40^\circ$ and $75^\circ$, the corresponding exterior angle measures $40^\circ + 75^\circ = 115^\circ$.
• **Checking Consistency**: If an exterior angle is $115^\circ$, its adjacent interior angle must be $180^\circ - 115^\circ = 65^\circ$, confirming that $40^\circ + 75^\circ + 65^\circ = 180^\circ$.
• **Visual Identification**: Students must carefully distinguish between the adjacent interior angle and the two remote interior angles when applying geometric theorems.

• **Triangle Inequality Theorem Introduction**: Not any three random lengths of line segments can be joined together to form a valid triangle.
• **Triangle Inequality Theorem Statement**: The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must always be strictly greater than the length of the third remaining side.
• **Mathematical Inequalities**: For a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, and $c$, the following three conditions must simultaneously hold true: $a + b > c$, $b + c > a$, and $c + a > b$.
• **Testing Feasibility**: To check if side lengths $3\text{ cm}$, $4\text{ cm}$, and $5\text{ cm}$ form a triangle: $3+4>5$ (True), $4+5>3$ (True), $3+5>4$ (True); thus, a triangle is possible.
• **Failure Condition Example**: Consider side lengths $2\text{ cm}$, $3\text{ cm}$, and $6\text{ cm}$. Testing yields $2 + 3 = 5$, which is not greater than $6$. Hence, no triangle can be formed.
• **Shortest Distance Principle**: The triangle inequality is rooted in the fundamental geometric axiom that the shortest distance between two points is a straight line segment.
• **Difference Property**: As a corollary to the triangle inequality, the absolute difference between the lengths of any two sides of a triangle is always strictly less than the length of the third side.
• **Range of Third Side**: If two sides of a triangle are given as $a$ and $b$, the length of the third side ($c$) must fall within the open range: $(a - b) < c < (a + b)$.
• **Example Range Calculation**: If two sides of a triangle are $7\text{ cm}$ and $4\text{ cm}$, the third side must be greater than $7 - 4 = 3\text{ cm}$ and less than $7 + 4 = 11\text{ cm}$.
• **Integer Side Questions**: In DAV assessments, students are frequently asked to find the possible whole-number lengths for the third side of a triangle given the other two sides.
• **Perimeter of a Triangle**: The **Perimeter** of a triangle is defined as the total distance all the way around its outer boundary.
• **Perimeter Formula**: The perimeter ($P$) of any triangle is calculated simply by adding the lengths of all three of its sides together: $P = a + b + c$.
• **Perimeter of Equilateral Triangle**: For an equilateral triangle with side length $s$, the perimeter formula simplifies to $P = 3 \times s$.
• **Perimeter of Isosceles Triangle**: For an isosceles triangle with equal sides of length $a$ and a base of length $b$, the perimeter formula is $P = 2a + b$.
• **Unit Consistency**: All side lengths must be converted to the same unit of measurement (e.g., centimeters or meters) before calculating perimeters or testing inequalities.

• **Altitude of a Triangle Introduction**: Geometric analysis of triangles often requires measuring heights and perpendicular lines within the figure.
• **Definition of Altitude**: An **Altitude** (or height) of a triangle is a perpendicular line segment drawn from any vertex of the triangle to the straight line containing the opposite side.
• **Perpendicular Symbol**: The altitude makes a right angle ($90^\circ$) with the base line, denoted by the perpendicular symbol ($\perp$).
• **Number of Altitudes**: Every triangle possesses exactly three altitudes, corresponding to its three different vertices and their respective opposite sides.
• **Base and Altitude Pair**: Every altitude corresponds to a specific base; the base is the side perpendicular to the drawn altitude height.
• **Altitudes in Acute Triangles**: In an acute-angled triangle, all three altitudes lie completely inside the interior region of the triangle.
• **Altitudes in Right Triangles**: In a right-angled triangle, the two legs that form the right angle also double as two of its altitudes.
• **Altitudes in Obtuse Triangles**: In an obtuse-angled triangle, two of the altitudes fall outside the triangle, requiring the extension of the corresponding base sides.
• **Orthocenter Definition**: The **Orthocenter** is the unique single point of concurrency where all three altitudes of a triangle intersect each other.
• **Location of Orthocenter**: The orthocenter lies inside an acute triangle, at the vertex containing the right angle in a right triangle, and outside an obtuse triangle.
• **Median of a Triangle Definition**: A **Median** of a triangle is a straight line segment joining any vertex of the triangle to the exact midpoint of the opposite side.
• **Number of Medians**: Just like altitudes, every triangle possesses exactly three medians connecting its three vertices to the midpoints of their opposite sides.
• **Centroid Definition**: The **Centroid** is the point of concurrency where all three medians of a triangle intersect, representing the geometric center of mass or balancing point of the triangle.
• **Centroid Location**: Unlike the orthocenter, the centroid of any triangle (acute, right, or obtuse) always lies strictly inside the interior of the triangle.
• **Median Bisecting Area**: A median divides a triangle into two smaller triangles that have equal areas, a property frequently explored in advanced geometry extensions.

• **Special Properties of Isosceles Triangles**: Isosceles triangles possess unique symmetry properties that make them central to geometry problem-solving in DAV curriculums.
• **Base Angles Theorem**: In any isosceles triangle, the angles opposite to the two equal sides are also equal in measure. These are known as the **Base Angles**.
• **Vertex Angle**: The angle formed by the intersection of the two equal sides of an isosceles triangle is called the **Vertex Angle**.
• **Symmetry in Isosceles**: An isosceles triangle has exactly one line of symmetry passing through the vertex angle and bisecting the opposite base.
• **Coincidence of Lines**: In an isosceles triangle, the line of symmetry from the vertex angle acts simultaneously as the altitude, the median, and the angle bisector to the base.
• **Solving Isosceles Problems**: If the vertex angle of an isosceles triangle is given as $40^\circ$, the two base angles can be found by calculating $(180^\circ - 40^\circ) \div 2 = 140^\circ \div 2 = 70^\circ$.
• **Equilateral Triangle Symmetry**: An equilateral triangle possesses three lines of symmetry, one passing through each vertex and bisecting the opposite side.
• **Rotational Symmetry**: An equilateral triangle has rotational symmetry of order 3, meaning it looks identical when rotated by $120^\circ$, $240^\circ$, and $360^\circ$.
• **Scalene Triangle Symmetry**: A scalene triangle has no lines of symmetry and no rotational symmetry (order 1) because all sides and angles are completely different.
• **Right Isosceles Triangle**: A special category of triangle exists that is both right-angled and isosceles, possessing one $90^\circ$ angle and two equal acute angles measuring $45^\circ$ each.
• **Angle Verification for Right Isosceles**: $90^\circ + 45^\circ + 45^\circ = 180^\circ$, satisfying both the right-angle condition and the angle sum property of triangles.
• **Hypotenuse in Right Isosceles**: In a right isosceles triangle, the hypotenuse is always the longest side, and the two legs forming the right angle are equal in length.
• **Geometric Construction Basics**: DAV mathematics students learn to construct triangles using standard geometric instruments such as a ruler, compass, protractor, and set-squares.
• **SSS Construction Criterion**: A unique triangle can be constructed when the lengths of all three sides (Side-Side-Side) are provided, provided the triangle inequality is met.
• **SAS Construction Criterion**: A unique triangle can be constructed when two sides and the included angle (Side-Angle-Side) between them are given.

• **ASA Construction Criterion**: A unique triangle can also be constructed when two angles and the included side (Angle-Side-Angle) between them are specified.
• **RHS Construction Criterion**: For right-angled triangles, a unique triangle can be constructed when the length of the hypotenuse and one leg (Right-angle-Hypotenuse-Side) are given.
• **Use of Protractor**: A protractor is utilized in geometry to accurately measure and draw angles to a precision of one degree ($1^\circ$).
• **Use of Compass**: A compass is employed to draw arcs of precise radii, enabling students to construct line segments of exact lengths and locate intersection points.
• **Drafting Rough Sketches**: Before performing precise geometric constructions, students are advised to draw a rough freehand sketch and label all given measurements.
• **Labeling Conventions**: Standard DAV convention dictates that vertices are labeled with capital letters, sides with lowercase letters or two capital letters, and angles with degree symbols.
• **Error Prevention in Construction**: Ensuring compass needles are sharp and pencil leads are finely pointed prevents accumulation of errors during geometric constructions.
• **Concurrency Concept**: In geometry, **Concurrency** refers to three or more lines, rays, or line segments that intersect or pass through the exact same single point.
• **Point of Concurrency**: The specific point where concurrent lines meet is called the **Point of Concurrency**.
• **Incenter of a Triangle**: The **Incenter** is the point of concurrency of the three interior angle bisectors of a triangle, serving as the center of the inscribed circle.
• **Circumcenter of a Triangle**: The **Circumcenter** is the point of concurrency of the perpendicular bisectors of the three sides of a triangle, serving as the center of the circumscribed circle.
• **Summary of Triangle Centers**: A triangle features four primary notable centers: Centroid (medians), Orthocenter (altitudes), Incenter (angle bisectors), and Circumcenter (perpendicular bisectors).
• **Euler Line Concept**: In any non-equilateral triangle, the orthocenter, centroid, and circumcenter all lie collinear on a single straight line known as the **Euler Line**.
• **Structural Stability**: Engineers favor triangles over other geometric shapes because applying a force to the corner of a triangle distributes stress evenly across its rigid sides.
• **Truss Bridge Architecture**: Bridges, communication towers, cranes, and roof trusses are heavily constructed using interconnected triangular networks to maximize load-bearing capacity.

• **Review of Angle Classifications**: Re-emphasizing that triangles are classified by angles into acute-angled, right-angled, and obtuse-angled categories based on their single largest interior angle.
• **Review of Side Classifications**: Re-emphasizing that triangles are classified by sides into equilateral (three equal sides), isosceles (two equal sides), and scalene (zero equal sides).
• **Dual Classification Capability**: Every single triangle belongs simultaneously to one side-based category and one angle-based category (e.g., an isosceles right-angled triangle).
• **Impossible Combinations**: An equilateral triangle can never be obtuse or right-angled; it must always be an acute-angled triangle because all its angles are $60^\circ$.
• **Scalene Flexibility**: A scalene triangle can be acute-angled, right-angled, or obtuse-angled depending on the relative lengths of its sides.
• **Isosceles Flexibility**: An isosceles triangle can also be acute-angled (e.g., angles $50^\circ, 50^\circ, 80^\circ$), right-angled (angles $90^\circ, 45^\circ, 45^\circ$), or obtuse-angled (angles $20^\circ, 20^\circ, 140^\circ$).
• **Sum Property Application Drills**: DAV examinations frequently present multi-step word problems requiring the calculation of unknown angles in composite figures containing multiple attached triangles.
• **Common Supplemental Line Technique**: Solving complex geometry figures often requires drawing an auxiliary construction line to divide a complex polygon into individual solvable triangles.
• **Exterior Angle Quick-Check**: When verifying exam answers, students should always check if an exterior angle equals the algebraic sum of its two remote interior angles.
• **Triangle Inequality Sanity Check**: When designing word problems or checking manual measurements, side lengths must always pass the strict triangle inequality test before assuming a physical triangle can exist.
• **Precision in Geometry Notation**: Omitting degree symbols ($^\circ$) or triangle symbols ($\Delta$) in DAV written examinations results in loss of academic marks; proper mathematical notation is vital.
• **Mathematical Reasoning**: Students must provide brief geometric justifications (such as "Angle Sum Property" or "Base Angles of Isosceles Triangle") alongside their numerical calculation steps.
