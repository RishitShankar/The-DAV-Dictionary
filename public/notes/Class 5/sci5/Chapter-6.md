• **Chapter Overview**: Introduction to the fundamental biological and physical concepts governing food spoilage, food wastage, and methods of food preservation as outlined in Class 5 *My Living World* (DAV Public School Board syllabus).
• **Definition of Food**: Food is defined as any nutritious substance that humans and animals eat or drink, or that plants absorb, in order to maintain life and growth, provide energy, and build bodily tissues.
• **Role of Nutrients**: Food contains essential chemical components known as nutrients—namely carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals—which are vital for proper physiological functioning.
• **Universal Human Need**: Acquiring adequate, clean, and nutritious food is a basic biological necessity for all living organisms, particularly humans, to sustain daily metabolic activities.
• **Concept of Food Spoilage**: Food spoilage refers to the process where a food product becomes undesirable or unacceptable for human consumption due to changes in its sensory qualities (smell, taste, texture, appearance).
• **Signs of Spoilage**: Obvious indicators of spoiled food include foul odours, discoloration, unusual or sour tastes, slimy textures, and the visible growth of molds or fungi.
• **Primary Cause of Spoilage**: Microorganisms—specifically microscopic bacteria, yeasts, and molds—are the primary biological agents responsible for breaking down the organic compounds present in food.
• **Favorable Conditions for Microorganisms**: Microorganisms multiply rapidly in environments that provide warmth, moisture, oxygen, and a rich supply of nutrients, which are naturally abundant in most fresh foods.
• **Role of Moisture**: Water content is a critical factor in microbial growth; dry foods spoil much slower than moist foods because microorganisms require moisture to carry out their life processes.
• **Role of Temperature**: Warm temperatures accelerate the reproductive rates of bacteria and fungi, whereas cold temperatures slow down their metabolic processes significantly.
• **Enzymatic Action**: Natural chemical catalysts called enzymes, present within plant and animal tissues, continue to cause ripening and eventual decay even after harvesting or slaughter.
• **Pest Contamination**: Insects (such as flies, ants, and weevils), rodents (rats and mice), and birds act as vectors that contaminate food supplies and accelerate physical degradation.
• **Chemical Oxidation**: Exposure to air (oxygen) causes chemical reactions in certain foods, particularly fats and oils, leading to rancidity, unpleasant tastes, and loss of nutritional value.
• **Improper Handling**: Inadequate hygiene during food preparation, unclean utensils, and unwashed hands transfer harmful germs directly into edible items, speeding up spoilage.
• **Improper Storage**: Storing perishable food items at room temperature instead of refrigerating them promotes rapid bacterial proliferation and shortens shelf life.

• **Definition of Food Wastage**: Food wastage refers to the discarding or loss of food that is safe and nutritious for human consumption, occurring at all stages of the food supply chain.
• **Stages of Food Supply Chain**: Food wastage happens at multiple levels, including agricultural production, post-harvest handling, processing, distribution, retail markets, and final household consumption.
• **Farm-Level Waste**: Wastage during agricultural production occurs due to unharvested crops, pest infestations, unpropitious weather conditions, and lack of modern storage facilities in rural areas.
• **Transportation Losses**: Poor infrastructure, bumpy roads, and lack of refrigerated transport vehicles cause significant damage and bruising to fruits and vegetables before they reach markets.
• **Retail and Market Waste**: Supermarkets and local vendors often discard perfectly edible food items simply because they do not meet cosmetic standards regarding shape, size, or color.
• **Social and Cultural Factors**: Large-scale food wastage frequently occurs during social gatherings, weddings, parties, and festivals where excessive amounts of food are prepared and left unconsumed.
• **Household Wastage**: Individual households contribute significantly to food wastage through over-purchasing, poor meal planning, cooking more food than required, and letting items expire in refrigerators.
• **Leftover Mismanagement**: Failing to store leftovers properly leads to quick spoilage, forcing families to throw away cooked meals that could otherwise have been consumed safely.
• **Global Impact of Food Waste**: Food wastage is not just a moral issue; it represents a massive misuse of natural resources, including water, land, energy, and labor used to produce the food.
• **Environmental Consequences**: Decaying food in landfills produces large amounts of methane gas, a potent greenhouse gas that significantly contributes to global warming and climate change.
• **Economic Loss**: Wasted food translates directly into financial losses for farmers, manufacturers, retailers, and households, draining economic resources unnecessarily.
• **The Paradox of Hunger**: Food wastage stands in stark contrast to global hunger, where millions of people, especially children, suffer from malnutrition and lack access to daily meals.
• **Ethical Responsibility**: Every individual has a moral and ethical duty to respect food, take only what is needed on their plates, and minimize waste at home and school.
• **Awareness and Education**: Educating school children about the value of food helps cultivate lifelong habits of mindful consumption and resource conservation.
• **Community Sharing**: Promoting systems where surplus food from functions and restaurants is safely collected and distributed to the underprivileged helps bridge the gap between waste and hunger.

• **Definition of Food Preservation**: Food preservation is the science and practice of treating and handling food in a way that stops or greatly slows down spoilage, preventing quality degradation and food-borne illnesses.
• **Primary Objective**: The core purpose of food preservation is to extend the shelf life of seasonal and perishable foods so they remain safe, nutritious, and appetizing for long periods.
• **Historical Significance**: Since ancient times, humans have devised various ingenious methods to preserve food to survive famines, harsh winters, and long sea voyages.
• **Disruption of Microbial Growth**: Preservation techniques work by creating hostile environments for microorganisms, thereby killing them, removing their water source, or inhibiting their reproduction.
• **Inactivation of Enzymes**: Effective preservation methods also deactivate natural enzymes within food that would otherwise cause over-ripening, browning, and decay.
• **Classification of Preservation Methods**: Food preservation techniques are broadly categorized into traditional household methods and modern industrial preservation techniques.
• **Role of Refrigeration (Freezing/Chilling)**: Keeping food in a refrigerator (0°C to 4°C) slows down the metabolic rate of bacteria and fungi, keeping milk, vegetables, and cooked items fresh for a few days.
• **Deep Freezing**: Storing food at sub-zero temperatures (below 0°C) turns water into ice crystals, halting microbial activity almost entirely and preserving meat, fish, and peas for months.
• **Principle of Dehydration (Drying)**: Sun drying or machine drying removes moisture from food items, as microorganisms cannot grow and multiply without water.
• **Commonly Dried Foods**: Staple items traditionally preserved through drying include pulses, red chillies, raw mango slices, potato chips, fish, and various herbs.
• **Salting (Curing)**: Adding salt to food draws out moisture through the process of osmosis and creates an environment where most spoilage bacteria cannot survive.
• **Application of Salting**: Salting is extensively used for preserving raw mangoes, lemons, amla (Indian gooseberry), fish, and meat to make pickles or cured products.
• **Sweetening (Sugar Preservation)**: High concentrations of sugar bind with available water molecules in food, preventing microorganisms from utilizing moisture for growth.
• **Preservation with Sugar**: Sugar is the primary preservative agent used in making jams, jellies, marmalades, and murabbas from seasonal fruits.
• **Use of Chemical Preservatives**: Adding specific, food-grade chemical agents like sodium benzoate or potassium metabisulphite helps prevent microbial growth in bottled squashes, ketchups, and packaged juices.

• **Detailed Study of Freezing**: Freezing is one of the most popular and convenient modern preservation methods used in households worldwide to maintain food quality.
• **Mechanism of Freezing**: At very low temperatures inside a deep freezer, the water content in food freezes into ice, making water unavailable for microbial use and stopping chemical reactions.
• **Maintenance of Nutritional Value**: Unlike heating, freezing retains most of the original vitamins, minerals, texture, and natural flavor of vegetables and fruits.
• **Blanching Before Freezing**: Vegetables are often briefly dipped in boiling water (blanched) and then plunged into ice water before freezing to kill surface bacteria and deactivate enzymes.
• **Limitations of Freezing**: Freezing requires continuous electrical power; a prolonged power outage can thaw the food, making it susceptible to rapid spoilage once refrozen.
• **Detailed Study of Dehydration (Drying)**: Sun drying is the oldest and most cost-effective method of food preservation utilized extensively in rural agricultural households.
• **Solar Heat Utilization**: Harnessing natural heat from the sun and circulating air evaporates moisture from food surfaces and inner tissues evenly.
• **Hygienic Drying Concerns**: Traditional sun drying must be done carefully on clean sheets or rooftops to prevent contamination from dust, dirt, flies, and birds.
• **Modern Dehydration**: Industrial food dehydrators use controlled heat and forced air circulation to dry fruits (like raisins and banana chips) rapidly and hygienically.
• **Rehydration Property**: Dried foods can often be restored to near-original form by soaking or cooking them in water before preparation.
• **Detailed Study of Salting (Pickling Base)**: Salting acts as a natural dehydrating agent and antimicrobial barrier that has been trusted across generations.
• **Osmosis in Salting**: Salt draws out internal moisture from food cells via osmosis, creating a concentrated hypertonic solution that dehydrates bacterial cells.
• **Pickling Tradition**: In Indian households, raw mangoes, limes, and mixed vegetables are mixed with high amounts of salt, oil, and spices to create shelf-stable pickles (*achaar*).
• **Role of Oil in Pickling**: Pouring mustard or sesame oil over pickled vegetables creates an airtight seal on top, preventing oxygen from reaching the food and blocking aerobic bacteria.
• **Spices as Preservatives**: Turmeric, red chilli powder, mustard seeds, and fenugreek used in pickles possess natural antibacterial and antifungal properties that enhance preservation.

• **Detailed Study of Boiling and Heating**: Applying heat is a fundamental method used daily in every kitchen to kill harmful pathogens present in raw food ingredients.
• **Pasteurization Process**: Named after Louis Pasteur, pasteurization involves heating liquids (such as milk) to a specific high temperature for a short duration, followed by immediate cooling.
• **Target of Pasteurization**: This process kills harmful disease-causing bacteria (pathogens) like *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and *Salmonella* without altering the nutritional composition or taste of milk.
• **Boiling Water**: Boiling raw drinking water destroys all active water-borne bacteria and parasites, making it safe for human consumption and preventing water-borne diseases.
• **Canning and Bottling**: Canning involves placing food items into airtight metal cans or glass jars and heating them to a high temperature to sterilize the contents.
• **Vacuum Sealing in Cans**: The sealing process removes air and creates a vacuum, preventing oxygen-dependent bacteria and molds from entering or surviving inside the container.
• **Long Shelf Life of Canned Goods**: Properly canned vegetables, soups, and baked beans can remain safe and edible for years if the seal remains unbroken.
• **Significance of Airtight Containers**: Oxygen promotes both microbial growth and chemical oxidation; therefore, airtight containers are essential for storing snacks, biscuits, and dry grains.
• **Vacuum Packaging**: Modern food industries use vacuum packing machines to suck all air out of plastic pouches containing meat, cheese, or nuts before sealing them tightly.
• **Gas Flushing**: Some packaged foods (like potato chips bags) are flushed with inert nitrogen gas instead of air to prevent crushing and inhibit lipid oxidation.
• **Use of Natural Preservatives**: Lemon juice (citric acid), vinegar (acetic acid), salt, and sugar are natural substances added to foods to increase acidity and inhibit bacterial growth.
• **Acidic Environment**: Bacteria thrive in neutral or alkaline conditions; adding acidic agents like vinegar creates a low pH environment that most spoilage microbes cannot tolerate.
• **Preservation of Onions and Garlic**: Storing root vegetables like onions, potatoes, and garlic in cool, dry, well-ventilated dark spaces prevents sprouting and fungal rot.
• **Inspection Before Consumption**: Consumers must always inspect packaged and stored foods for bulging cans, unusual smells, or mold patches before eating to avoid food poisoning.
• **Food Safety Guidelines**: Observing strict personal hygiene, washing hands before cooking, and cleaning kitchen platforms are non-negotiable rules for preventing food contamination.

• **Role of Microorganisms in Food**: While many microorganisms cause food spoilage, certain specific microbes are highly beneficial and essential for producing various food items.
• **Fermentation Definition**: Fermentation is a metabolic process where microorganisms like yeast and beneficial bacteria convert carbohydrates (sugars and starches) into alcohols or organic acids.
• **Role of Yeast in Baking**: Single-celled fungi called yeast are added to dough to produce carbon dioxide gas through fermentation, causing the dough to rise and making breads fluffy and soft.
• **Curd Formation (Lactobacillus)**: Warm milk inoculated with a small amount of pre-existing curd undergoes bacterial fermentation where *Lactobacillus* bacteria convert milk sugar (lactose) into lactic acid.
• **Acidification of Milk**: Lactic acid thickens the milk proteins (casein), turning liquid milk into semi-solid curd with a pleasant sour taste and high probiotic value.
• **Production of Vinegar and Wine**: Alcoholic fermentation by yeasts converts fruit sugars into alcohol, which acetic acid bacteria further ferment into vinegar used for cooking and pickling.
• **Cheese Making**: Beneficial bacteria and molds are deliberately introduced into milk during cheese production to develop unique textures, flavors, and preservation qualities.
• **Probiotics and Gut Health**: Fermented foods rich in live beneficial bacteria aid human digestion, boost the immune system, and maintain a healthy balance of gut flora.
• **Food Safety and Spoilage Distinction**: It is crucial to distinguish between controlled beneficial fermentation (like curd and idli batter) and unwanted microbial spoilage (like rotten vegetables).
• **Spoilage Microbes vs. Pathogens**: Spoilage microorganisms alter taste and texture, whereas food-borne pathogens (like *E. coli* or *Salmonella*) may not change food appearance but cause severe illness.
• **Symptoms of Food Poisoning**: Consuming spoiled or contaminated food leads to food poisoning, characterized by severe stomach cramps, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and dehydration.
• **Danger of Stale Food**: Cooked food left outside the refrigerator for long hours during hot summer days becomes a breeding ground for dangerous toxins produced by bacteria.
• **The "Sniff Test" Fallacy**: Relying solely on the sniff test is dangerous because harmful pathogenic bacteria and lethal toxins do not always produce a foul smell or visible mold.
• **Safe Thawing Practices**: Frozen meat and poultry should be thawed safely inside the refrigerator or microwave rather than on kitchen counters to prevent bacterial multiplication at room temperature.
• **Cross-Contamination Prevention**: Using separate cutting boards and knives for raw meats and ready-to-eat vegetables prevents harmful bacteria from transferring across food items.

• **Interdependence of Agriculture and Preservation**: Food preservation plays a vital role in connecting agricultural harvest seasons with year-round consumer demand across different geographical regions.
• **Overcoming Seasonal Limitations**: Many fruits and vegetables grow abundantly only during specific seasons (e.g., mangoes in summer, peas in winter); preservation allows people to enjoy them year-round.
• **Surplus Management During Bumper Crops**: When farmers experience a bumper harvest, preservation techniques absorb excess produce, preventing massive crop wastage and sudden price crashes.
• **Food Security and Storage**: Effective preservation and warehousing ensure stable food supplies for urban populations, military troops, disaster-stricken areas, and space missions.
• **Role of Cold Chain Logistics**: A cold chain is a temperature-controlled supply chain comprising refrigerated storage and transport vehicles necessary for perishable items like dairy, vaccines, and frozen foods.
• **Importance in Disaster Management**: Non-perishable canned, dried, and vacuum-packed foods are essential relief materials airlifted to regions affected by floods, earthquakes, or droughts.
• **Economic Benefits for Farmers**: Processing and preserving surplus crops into jams, pickles, and dehydrated flakes allows farmers and rural cooperatives to earn higher incomes.
• **Small-Scale Industries (SSI)**: Pickle making, papad rolling, jam manufacturing, and flour milling form vibrant cottage industries that provide rural employment, particularly for women.
• **Packaging Innovations**: Modern food science utilizes multilayer flexible packaging, aseptic cartons (Tetra Pak), and modified atmosphere packaging to preserve liquid foods without chemical preservatives.
• **Labeling and Expiry Dates**: Packaged food items display manufacturing dates, ingredients, nutritional information, and "Best Before" or "Use By" dates to guide consumers safely.
• **Difference Between 'Best Before' and 'Expiry'**: 'Best Before' indicates optimal quality and flavor, whereas 'Expiry Date' denotes the absolute limit beyond which the food is unsafe to consume.
• **Role of Government Agencies**: Government bodies (like FSSAI in India) set strict quality standards, hygiene regulations, and safety inspections for commercial food processors and manufacturers.
• **Consumer Awareness**: Citizens must cultivate the habit of checking packaging seals, manufacturing tags, and expiry dates before purchasing processed food items from grocery stores.
• **Sustainable Food Habits**: Minimizing food waste and adopting traditional home-preservation methods contributes to personal health, economic savings, and environmental sustainability.

• **Key Terms Revision - Food Spoilage**: The alteration in the physical and chemical properties of food making it unfit, unpalatable, or unsafe for human consumption due to microbial or chemical action.
• **Key Terms Revision - Microorganisms**: Tiny, microscopic living organisms including bacteria, fungi (molds and yeasts), and viruses that inhabit the environment and interact with organic matter.
• **Key Terms Revision - Food Wastage**: The discarding of edible, nutritious food at any point along the food supply chain from farm production to retail and final household consumption.
• **Key Terms Revision - Food Preservation**: The application of physical, chemical, or biological methods to prevent or slow down food spoilage while maintaining nutritional quality.
• **Key Terms Revision - Dehydration**: A preservation method involving the complete removal of moisture (water) from food items using solar heat or industrial drying machines.
• **Key Terms Revision - Pasteurization**: A thermal process of heating liquids like milk to a specific high temperature for a short duration followed by rapid cooling to kill pathogenic bacteria.
• **Key Terms Revision - Fermentation**: A metabolic biochemical process where beneficial microorganisms convert carbohydrates into acids, gases, or alcohol under controlled conditions.
• **Key Terms Revision - Refrigeration**: Storing perishable food items at low temperatures (0°C to 4°C) to slow down microbial reproduction and enzyme activity.
• **Key Terms Revision - Freezing**: Storing food below 0°C to convert water content into ice crystals, halting microbial growth and locking in freshness for extended periods.
• **Key Terms Revision - Salting (Curing)**: Adding salt to raw food items to draw out internal moisture through osmosis, creating a hostile environment for spoilage bacteria.
• **Key Terms Revision - Pickling**: Preserving vegetables or fruits by submerging them in a concentrated mixture of salt, oil, vinegar, and antimicrobial spices.
• **Key Terms Revision - Canning**: Packing food items in airtight metal or glass containers and applying high heat sterilization to eliminate all living microorganisms.
• **Key Terms Revision - Vacuum Packing**: Removing all ambient air from packaging pouches and sealing them tightly to prevent oxidation and aerobic microbial growth.
• **Key Terms Revision - Pathogens**: Disease-causing microorganisms that contaminate food supplies and lead to severe health issues, infections, or food poisoning.
• **Key Terms Revision - Expiry Date**: The precise date marked on packaged food products beyond which the item is considered spoiled, unsafe, or nutritionally degraded.
