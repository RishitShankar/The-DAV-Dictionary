• **Chapter Title:** Regional Powers (Class 7 Social Science, We and Our World - DAV Public School Board).
• **Core Subject Focus:** The political, economic, and cultural developments in various Indian regions following the decline of major pan-Indian empires.
• **Historical Timeline:** The period spanning roughly from the 8th to the 12th century CE, often characterized in Indian history as the early medieval era.
• **Decline of Central Authority:** The weakening of centralized imperial structures like the Gupta Empire and later Harshavardhana’s empire led to the emergence of numerous independent regional kingdoms.
• **Rise of Regional Kingdoms:** Smaller states that were once subordinate feudatories or provincial governors declared independence and established powerful regional dynasties.
• **Geographical Division:** The Indian subcontinent during this era was broadly divided into three main spheres of regional conflicts and developments: Northern India, the Deccan, and Southern India.
• **Tripartite Struggle:** A prolonged, multi-cornered conflict among three major dynasties—the Gurjara-Pratiharas, the Palas, and the Rashtrakutas—for the control of the coveted city of Kannauj.
• **Strategic Importance of Kannauj:** Kannauj served as the symbol of imperial authority and sovereignty in Northern India, much like Pataliputra in earlier centuries, due to its fertile location along the Ganga river valley.
• **Feudal Structure:** The political system of this era was heavily marked by *samantas* or feudal lords who wielded substantial local military and administrative power.
• **Military Obligations of Samantas:** Feudal lords were expected to provide military support, horses, and troops to their overlord (the king) during times of war.
• **Emergence of Titles:** Ambitious local chiefs adopted grand titles such as *Maha-samanta*, *Mahamandaleshwara* (great lord of a circle/region), and *Tribhuvana-chakravartin* (lord of the three worlds).
• **Agricultural Expansion:** The economy of these regional powers was overwhelmingly agrarian, driven by the clearing of forests, extension of cultivable land, and construction of irrigation works.
• **Land Grants (*Agraharas*):** Kings frequently issued copper-plate land grants to Brahmanas, temples, and military officers, which promoted agriculture in remote areas but also created autonomous pockets of power.
• **Temple Patronage:** Regional rulers legitimized their political authority and displayed their wealth by constructing magnificent stone temples that doubled as economic and social hubs.
• **Art and Architecture:** The period witnessed the flowering of distinct regional architectural styles, notably the Nagara style in the north and the Dravida style in the south.

• **The Gurjara-Pratiharas:** A prominent dynasty that ruled over western India and parts of northern India, originating from the Gurjara lineage in Rajasthan and Gujarat.
• **Nagabhata I:** The real founder and most prominent early ruler of the Gurjara-Pratihara dynasty, who successfully checked the Arab expansion from Sind into western India.
• **Bhoja I (Mihira Bhoja):** The greatest ruler of the Pratihara dynasty, under whose rule the empire expanded significantly and achieved peak political stability and military glory.
• **Commercial Prosperity under Pratiharas:** The Pratihara kingdom was praised by Arab travelers for its vast trade networks, security of roads, and flourishing horse trade from Central Asia.
• **Artistic Legacy of Pratiharas:** They were great patrons of art and literature; the famous poet and dramatist Rajashekhara lived in the royal court of the Pratiharas.
• **The Palas of Bengal:** Founded by Gopala around 750 CE to restore law and order, the Pala dynasty dominated the eastern region of Bengal and Bihar.
• **Gopala’s Election:** According to historical accounts, Gopala was elected by the prominent notables of the region to end the prevailing anarchy (*matsyanyaya* or rule of the fish).
• **Dharmapala:** Son of Gopala, who expanded the Pala empire, revived Kannauj temporarily, and was a great champion of Buddhist learning.
• **Founding of Vikramashila University:** Established by King Dharmapala on a hillock on the banks of the Ganga in Bihar, becoming a world-renowned center of Buddhist education and esoteric practices.
• **Somapura Mahavihara:** Another architectural masterpiece built by Dharmapala at Paharpur (now in Bangladesh), showcasing complex terracotta artwork and Buddhist monastic planning.
• **Devapala:** Successor of Dharmapala who extended Pala boundaries to Assam, Orissa, and parts of modern Nepal, maintaining a formidable navy on the eastern rivers.
• **Trade with Southeast Asia:** The Palas maintained vibrant maritime trade links with Southeast Asia (Suvarnadvipa), bringing immense wealth and cultural exchange to eastern India.
• **Buddhist Patronage:** The Palas were staunch supporters of Mahayana Buddhism at a time when Buddhism was declining in other parts of India; they patronized Nalanda and Vikramashila.
• **Pala Art Style:** Development of the distinctive Eastern Indian school of art, characterized by exquisite bronze casting and stone sculptures with meticulous detailing.
• **Decline of the Palas:** Following Devapala’s death, internal rebellions and pressures from the Senas and Southern invaders led to the gradual fragmentation of the Pala empire.

• **The Rashtrakutas:** An imperial dynasty that ruled the Deccan plateau from the 8th to the 10th century CE, originating as feudatories of the Chalukyas of Badami.
• **Dantidurga:** The founder of the Rashtrakuta imperial power who overthrew the Chalukya ruler Kirtivarman II and performed the *Hiranya-garbha* (golden womb) ritual to legitimize his sovereign status.
• **Krishna I:** Uncle and successor of Dantidurga, known for expanding Rashtrakuta territories and commissioning the awe-inspiring monolithic Kailash Temple at Ellora.
• **Dhruva Dharavarsha:** The Rashtrakuta monarch who successfully challenged both the Pratiharas and the Palas in the north, establishing Rashtrakuta dominance in the Tripartite Struggle.
• **Govinda III:** Another formidable military leader of the dynasty who led successful campaigns from the Himalayas down to Cape Comorin (Kanyakumari), striking terror in northern rulers.
• **Amoghavarsha I:** The most celebrated Rashtrakuta king, who ruled for over six decades and preferred the pursuits of peace, literature, and religion over aggressive warfare.
• **Kavirajamarga:** Written by or under the patronage of Amoghavarsha I, this is the earliest available work on poetics and grammar in the Kannada language.
• **Religious Tolerance:** The Rashtrakuta monarchs practiced religious pluralism, patronizing Jainism, Shaivism, Vaishnavism, and Buddhism alike.
• **Capital City of Manyakheta:** The Rashtrakutas established their magnificent capital at Manyakheta (modern Malkhed in Karnataka), known for its palaces, markets, and cosmopolitan culture.
• **Arab Accounts of Rashtrakutas:** Arab merchants and travelers, such as Suleiman, visited the Rashtrakuta court and noted that their king was one of the four great sovereigns of the world.
• **Feudal Administration:** The empire was divided into *rashtras* (provinces), headed by *rashtrapatis*, which were further subdivided into *vishayas* (districts) and *bhuktis*.
• **Military Strength:** The Rashtrakutas maintained a massive, well-organized army consisting of infantry, cavalry, and a powerful elephant corps, supplemented by fleets in coastal waters.
• **Jain Patronage:** Several Rashtrakuta ministers and generals, as well as the kings themselves (like Amoghavarsha in his later years), were ardent patrons of Jain *acharyas* and temple building.
• **Decline of Rashtrakutas:** The dynasty declined due to palace intrigues, weak successors, and a final devastating attack by the Paramara king Tailapa II, who overthrew the last Rashtrakuta ruler.
• **Cultural Legacy:** The rock-cut cave architecture at Ellora and Elephanta stands as an enduring testament to the engineering genius and aesthetic vision of the Rashtrakutas.

• **The Rajputs:** A group of martial clans that emerged in Northern and Western India during the post-Gupta period, claiming descent from ancient solar (*Surya*), lunar (*Chandra*), and agniveksha lineages.
• **Agnikula Theory:** According to the legendary account in Chand Bardai’s *Prithviraj Raso*, several Rajput clans originated from the sacrificial fire pit (*agni-kunda*) of sage Vasishtha on Mount Abu.
• **Major Rajput Clans:** The prominent clans included the Chauhans of Ajmer/Delhi, Chandellas of Bundelkhand, Paramaras of Malwa, Tomaras of Delhi, and Solankis of Gujarat.
• **The Chauhans (Chahamanas):** Originally rulers of Shakambhari (Sambhar) in Rajasthan, who later shifted their power center to Ajmer and eventually gained control over Delhi.
• **Vigraharaja IV:** A powerful Chauhan ruler who captured Delhi from the Tomaras and extended his empire across large parts of northern India, also known as a Sanskrit scholar and poet.
• **Prithviraj III (Prithviraj Chauhan):** The most famous Chauhan ruler, celebrated in folklore and literature for his unmatched valor, romance with Samyogita, and resistance against foreign invasions.
• **First Battle of Tarain (1191 CE):** Fought near Karnal between Prithviraj Chauhan and Muhammad Ghori, in which the Ghurid invader was decisively defeated by the Rajput coalition.
• **Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE):** Fought at the same battlefield a year later; Muhammad Ghori returned with superior tactics and strategy, defeating Prithviraj Chauhan and changing the course of Indian history.
• **Significance of Tarain II:** The defeat of Prithviraj marked the virtual collapse of Rajput political resistance in northern India and paved the way for the establishment of Delhi Sultanate.
• **The Chandellas of Jejakabhukti (Bundelkhand):** Rulers who carved out an independent kingdom between the Yamuna and Narmada rivers, famous for their magnificent temple-building activities.
• **Khajuraho Temple Complex:** Built by the Chandella rulers between 950 and 1050 CE, renowned worldwide for their stunning sculpture, architectural harmony, and intricate erotic carvings.
• **Dhangadeva:** A notable Chandella king who expanded the territory and built the magnificent Vishvanatha temple at Khajuraho.
• **The Paramaras of Malwa:** Rulers based in the fertile Malwa region, with their capital at Dhara, known for cultivating arts, sciences, and literature.
• **King Bhoja Paramara:** The quintessential philosopher-king of Malwa, an author of numerous treatises on architecture, medicine, grammar, and poetry, who also built a great lake near Bhopal.
• **Rajput Society and Code of Honor:** Characterized by a fierce sense of clan loyalty, chivalry, hospitality, and a preference for death over dishonor (exemplified by *jauhar* and *saka*).

• **The Cholas of Thanjavur:** One of the most brilliant imperial dynasties of Southern India, who revived the ancient Chola power and built a vast maritime empire.
• **Vijayalaya:** The founder of the later Chola dynasty, who captured Thanjavur from the Muttaraiyar chiefs in the 9th century CE and made it his capital.
• **Parantaka I:** An early Chola ruler who consolidated power in the south, defeated the Pandya and Pandyas-Pallava alliances, and secured the submission of the ruler of Lanka (Sri Lanka).
• **Rajaraja I (985–1014 CE):** The greatest of the early Chola monarchs, who transformed the Chola state into a military superpower through aggressive conquests and naval expeditions.
• **Naval Supremacy of Rajaraja I:** He built a formidable navy that conquered the Maldives, parts of Sri Lanka (Mummudicholamandalam), and coastal regions of the Indian Ocean.
• **Rajendra I (1014–1044 CE):** Son and successor of Rajaraja I, who extended Chola imperial reach far beyond the Indian mainland into Southeast Asia and up to the Ganga river.
• **Gangaikondacholapuram:** The new capital city founded by Rajendra I to commemorate his successful expedition to the Gangetic plains, featuring an extensive irrigation tank called *Cholaganga*.
• **Naval Expedition to Southeast Asia:** Rajendra I’s navy crossed the Bay of Bengal and attacked the Srivijaya empire, subduing kingdoms in modern-day Sumatra, Malaya, and Indonesia to secure trade routes.
• **Chola Administration:** Highly organized and efficient bureaucratic machinery that balanced centralized royal control with robust local self-government institutions.
• **Village Assemblies (*Ur*, *Sabha*, *Nagaram*):** The hallmark of Chola administration, where local communities managed their own irrigation, temples, land records, and judicial matters.
• **The *Uttiramerur* Inscriptions:** Two famous stone inscriptions found in a temple at Uttiramerur that provide detailed rules regarding the qualifications, disqualifications, and election process for village committees (*Kudavolai* system).
• **The *Kudavolai* System:** An ingenious method of secret ballot election where slips of palm leaf bearing candidate names were drawn from a pot (*kudam*) by a young child.
• **Economic Prosperity under Cholas:** Flourishing agrarian economy supported by massive river-bunding projects, river-fed canals, and stone-lined tanks (*eris*).
• **Guilds of Merchants (*Manigramam*, *Ayyavole*):** Powerful merchant associations that carried out extensive domestic and overseas trade with China, Southeast Asia, and Western Asia.
• **Chola Bronze Sculptures:** The pinnacle of South Indian metallurgy, best exemplified by the magnificent bronze casting of *Nataraja* (Lord Shiva as the cosmic dancer), created using the lost-wax method.

• **The Pallavas of Kanchipuram:** A major regional power that dominated northern Tamil Nadu and parts of Andhra Pradesh from the 4th to the 9th century CE, paving the way for South Indian art.
• **Mahendravarman I:** Early Pallava ruler, playwright, and patron of rock-cut architecture, who transitioned from being a Jain to an ardent follower of Shaivism under the influence of Appar.
• **Narasimhavarman I (Mamalla):** The greatest Pallava monarch who avenged earlier defeats by defeating Pulakeshin II of the Chalukyas and capturing the Chalukyan capital, Vatapi (*Vatapikonda*).
• **Founding of Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram):** Established by Narasimhavarman I as a major port city and cultural center, famous for its monolithic rock-cut shrines (*rathas*) and bas-relief sculptures.
• **Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram:** Built by Narasimhavarman II (Rajasimha), this structural stone temple stands on the edge of the Bay of Bengal, representing the transition to structural architecture.
• **Pallava Art Stages:** Rock-cut cave temples (Mahendra style), monolithic rathas (Mamalla style), and structural stone temples (Rajasimha and Nandivarman styles).
• **The Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi):** A prominent imperial dynasty of the Deccan that ruled from the 6th to the 8th century CE, controlling key trade routes and mountain passes.
• **Pulakeshin II:** The most illustrious Chalukya monarch who halted the northward march of Harshavardhana of Kannauj on the banks of the Narmada River around 618 CE.
• **Aihole Inscription:** Composed by the court poet Ravikirti in the Meguti temple at Aihole, providing valuable historical details about Pulakeshin II’s military victories and poetic accomplishments.
• **The Cradle of Indian Temple Architecture:** The town of Aihole, along with Badami and Pattadakal, served as an experimental laboratory for early stone temple construction under the Chalukyas.
• **Pattadakal Monument Group:** A UNESCO World Heritage site featuring a unique blend of northern Nagara and southern Dravida architectural styles in its cluster of ten major temples.
• **Virupaksha Temple at Pattadakal:** Built by Queen Lokamahadevi to commemorate her husband Vikramaditya II's victory over the Pallavas, serving as a direct inspiration for the Kailash Temple at Ellora.
• **Decline of Badami Chalukyas:** Weakened by continuous wars with the Pallavas, the dynasty suffered a severe blow when the Rashtrakutas overthrew Kirtivarman II in 753 CE.
• **Later Chalukyas (Western Chalukyas of Kalyani):** A revival of the Chalukya power in the late 10th century by Tailapa II, ruling from Kalyani (modern Basavakalyan) until the late 12th century.
• **Cultural Contributions of Later Chalukyas:** Notable patrons of Kannada literature, temple building (such as the Mahadeva temple at Itagi), and legal treatises like the *Mitakshara* by Vijnaneshwara.

• **The Yadavas of Devagiri:** A powerful dynasty that ruled the region between the Tapti and Tungabhadra rivers, with their capital at Devagiri (modern Daulatabad in Maharashtra).
• **Bhillama V:** The founder of the independent Yadava kingdom who defeated the Later Chalukyas and established Devagiri as an impregnable fortress city.
• **Singhana II:** The most powerful ruler of the Yadava dynasty, who expanded the kingdom into Karnataka, Gujarat, and parts of the Konkan coast.
• **Literary and Scientific Patronage:** The Yadava court was a vibrant intellectual hub, patronizing scholars like Hemadri (who wrote on law and architecture) and Sharangadeva (author of *Sangita Ratnakara*, a seminal text on Indian music).
• **Decline of the Yadavas:** The kingdom declined in the late 13th century following invasions by Ala-ud-din Khilji, leading to the annexation of Devagiri into the Delhi Sultanate.
• **The Hoysalas of Darsamurasthana (Dwarasamudra):** A prominent dynasty that ruled over the core regions of modern Karnataka, with their capital at Halebid (Dwarasamudra).
• **Vishnuvardhana:** The most famous Hoysala ruler who broke away from Western Chalukya overlordship, embraced Vaishnavism under the guidance of Saint Ramanujacharya, and built magnificent temples.
• **Hoysaleshvara Temple at Halebid:** A masterpiece of Hoysala architecture, famous for its star-shaped platform, intricate friezes of elephants, horses, and celestial dancers, and soapstone carvings.
• **Chennakeshava Temple at Belur:** Another architectural marvel commissioned by King Vishnuvardhana to commemorate his military victories.
• **Hoysala Art Characteristics:** Characterized by highly ornate, miniature-like sculptural details, soft soapstone material, and multifaceted star-shaped temple layouts.
• **The Kakatiyas of Warangal:** A regional power that ruled over the Telangana region of Andhra Pradesh, emerging initially as feudatories of the Western Chalukyas.
• **Ganapatideva:** The greatest Kakatiya monarch, who unified the Telugu-speaking regions, expanded trade through coastal ports like Motupalli, and ensured internal security.
• **Queen Rudramadevi:** One of the rare female monarchs in Indian history who ruled efficiently, successfully repelling invasions from neighboring Yadavas and Pandyas while strengthening fortifications at Warangal.
• **Pratapachandra (Prataparudra II):** The last major Kakatiya ruler during whose reign the Delhi Sultanate launched massive military expeditions, eventually ending Kakatiya rule in 1323 CE.
• **Thousand Pillar Temple and Ramappa Temple:** Exceptional architectural creations of the Kakatiyas, with the Ramappa temple famed for its floating bricks and intricate bracket figures.

• **Agrarian Expansion and Technology:** The regional powers heavily invested in agricultural infrastructure, recognizing that land revenue was the primary source of state income.
• **Canal and Tank Construction:** Rulers in both North and South India built massive irrigation tanks (*eris* in Tamil Nadu, *kere* in Karnataka) and canal networks to protect agriculture from erratic monsoons.
• **Inam Lands and Brahmanas:** Large tracts of fertile land were granted tax-free to Brahmanas, educational institutions, and temples, which served as centers of agrarian management and rural employment.
• **Trade and Commerce:** Despite the dominance of the feudal agrarian economy, internal and external trade flourished under regional kingdoms due to active merchant guilds and secure trade routes.
• **Maritime Trade Networks:** Coastal kingdoms like the Cholas, Palas, and Kalingas maintained active commercial ties with Southeast Asia, China, and the Persian Gulf, exporting textiles, spices, and precious stones.
• **Currency Systems:** While gold coins (*suvarna*, *gadyana*, *pon*) were used for high-value transactions and by imperial courts, daily local trade relied heavily on cowry shells, silver, and copper coins.
• **Social Stratification:** Society remained deeply stratified based on the *varna* system, with the rise of numerous sub-castes (*jatis*) associated with specific occupations and regional identities.
• **Position of Women:** While royal women sometimes held administrative responsibilities, owned property, and patronized arts (such as Queen Lokamahadevi and Queen Rudramadevi), the general status of women faced restrictions, with practices like *jauhar* emerging among martial clans.
• **Religious Developments:** The period witnessed the decline of Buddhism in mainland India, the waning of Jainism in certain pockets, and the phenomenal rise of the Bhakti movement led by Nayanars (Shaivite saints) and Alvars (Vaishnavite saints).
• **Philosophy of Adi Shankara:** The great 8th-century philosopher who propagated *Advaita Vedanta* (non-dualism) and established four monastic centers (*mathas*) at Sringeri, Puri, Dwaraka, and Badrinath.
• **Philosophy of Ramanujacharya:** The 11th-century philosopher who championed *Vishishtadvaita* (qualified non-dualism) and emphasized personal devotion (*bhakti*) to Lord Vishnu as the path to salvation.
• **Temple as a Socio-Economic Institution:** Temples were not merely places of worship; they functioned as banks, schools, granaries, employers of artisans, and centers of classical dance and music.
• **Development of Classical Dance Forms:** Regional courts and temples gave birth to classical dance traditions like Bharatanatyam, Odissi, Kathakali, and Kuchipudi as forms of divine worship.
• **Literature in Regional Languages:** The era marked a shift from exclusive reliance on Sanskrit to the active growth of regional vernacular languages such as Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, Bengali, and early Hindi/Apabhramsha.
• **Scientific and Mathematical Contributions:** Scholars in regional courts continued the traditions of Aryabhata and Brahmagupta, producing advanced astronomical tables, mathematical treatises, and medical texts.

• **Geopolitical Interdependence:** Although these regional powers frequently fought one another for supremacy, they collectively preserved and enriched Indian civilization, art, and administrative systems.
• **Absence of Pan-Indian Unity:** The constant interstate rivalries and mutual distrust among regional kingdoms prevented them from forming a united front against external invaders from the Northwest.
• **Impact of Turkish Invasions:** The political vacuum created by the fragmentation of Northern India allowed Ghaznavid and Ghurid invaders to penetrate the subcontinent step by step.
• **Mahmud of Ghazni:** The ruler of Ghazni in Afghanistan who launched seventeen destructive raids into India between 1000 and 1027 CE, primarily targeting wealthy temple towns like Mathura, Kanauj, and Somnath.
• **Primary Motivation of Mahmud:** His primary goal was to plunder India's accumulated temple wealth to fund his Central Asian military campaigns rather than to establish an empire in India.
• **Al-Biruni:** The renowned scholar, astronomer, and historian who accompanied Mahmud of Ghazni to India and wrote *Kitab-ul-Hind*, an objective, comprehensive account of Indian science, philosophy, religion, and social life.
• **Muhammad Ghori:** Unlike Mahmud of Ghazni, Muhammad Ghori aimed to conquer territory and establish a permanent Islamic empire in Northern India, leading to direct military clashes with the Rajputs.
• **Fall of Northern Kingdoms:** Following the Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE), one by one, the regional kingdoms of northern and central India fell to the advancing armies of Ghori and his generals.
• **Qutb-ud-din Aibak:** General of Muhammad Ghori who was left in charge of Indian conquests, eventually laying the foundation of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206 CE.
• **Resilience of Southern Kingdoms:** While Northern India fell under Delhi Sultanate rule, the regional powers of the Deccan and South (such as the Yadavas, Hoysalas, Kakatiyas, and Pandyas) maintained their independence for another century before succumbing to later Khalji and Tughlaq expansions.
• **Continuity of Culture:** Despite political upheavals and changes in dynasties at the apex level, the foundational structures of Indian village life, agriculture, temple culture, and local governance remained remarkably stable.
• **Synthesis of Traditions:** The interaction between indigenous regional systems and incoming Islamic administrative and cultural influences laid the groundwork for the rich composite culture of medieval India.
• **Historical Significance of the Chapter:** Understanding the regional powers is crucial because they represent the bridge between the ancient classical empires and the medieval Sultanate-Mughal periods of Indian history.
• **Role of Epigraphy and Numismatics:** Much of our knowledge of these regional dynasties is derived from stone inscriptions, copper-plate charters, and coins that record land grants, tax remissions, and military triumphs.
• **Enduring Heritage:** The monuments, literature, social institutions, and political geography of modern India still bear deep imprints of the dynamic era of regional powers.

• **Key Term: Samanta:** Feudal lords or subordinate chiefs who held local administrative power and military obligations under a sovereign king during the early medieval period.
• **Key Term: Tripartite Struggle:** The prolonged three-way conflict among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas for the control of Kannauj between the 8th and 10th centuries CE.
• **Key Term: Agraharas:** Tax-free plots of land or entire villages granted by kings to Brahmanas, temples, and scholars to promote agriculture and religious merit.
• **Key Term: Nagara Style:** The distinctive style of Hindu temple architecture prevalent in Northern India, characterized by curved beehive-shaped towers (*shikharas*).
• **Key Term: Dravida Style:** The classical style of temple architecture prevalent in Southern India, distinguished by pyramidal towers (*vimanas*), massive gateway towers (*gopurams*), and pillared halls.
