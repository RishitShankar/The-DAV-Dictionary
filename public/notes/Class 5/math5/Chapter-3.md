• **Introduction to Chapter 3**: Welcome to the DAV Class 5 Primary Mathematics comprehensive study notes on "Multiples and Factors," designed to build a rock-solid foundation in number theory.
• **Definition of Multiples**: A **multiple** of a number is the product obtained when that number is multiplied by any whole number ($1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$).
• **Core Concept of Multiplication**: If we multiply a base number by successive counting numbers, the resulting products form the sequence of multiples for that specific number.
• **Example of Multiples**: The multiples of $4$ are calculated as $4 \times 1 = 4$, $4 \times 2 = 8$, $4 \times 3 = 12$, $4 \times 4 = 16$, and so on, continuing infinitely.
• **Infinite Nature of Multiples**: Every non-zero whole number has an **infinite number of multiples** because counting numbers never end.
• **Smallest Multiple Property**: The **smallest multiple** of any given non-zero number is the number itself, since multiplying a number by $1$ yields the number.
• **Comparison of Size**: Every multiple of a number (other than zero) is either greater than or equal to the number itself, never smaller.
• **Multiples and Zero**: The product of any number and zero is zero ($n \times 0 = 0$), making zero a universal multiple, though standard listing of multiples usually begins with the number multiplied by $1$.
• **Identifying Multiples**: To check if a number is a multiple of another, perform division; if the division leaves no remainder, it is a confirmed multiple.
• **Example Verification**: $48$ is a multiple of $6$ because $6 \times 8 = 48$ and $48 \div 6 = 8$ with a remainder of zero.
• **Multiples of 2**: **Even numbers** are all multiples of $2$, ending in digits $0, 2, 4, 6,$ or $8$.
• **Multiples of 5**: All multiples of $5$ always end in either the digit $0$ or the digit $5$.
• **Multiples of 10**: All multiples of $10$ always end strictly with the digit $0$.
• **Common Multiples**: A **common multiple** is a number that is a multiple of two or more given numbers simultaneously.
• **Finding Common Multiples**: To find common multiples of $3$ and $4$, list the multiples of both numbers and identify the numbers that appear in both lists ($12, 24, 36, \dots$).
• **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)**: The **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** of two or more numbers is the smallest non-zero common multiple shared by those numbers.

• **Transition to Factors**: While multiples involve scaling numbers up through multiplication, **factors** involve breaking numbers down through division or finding constituent parts.
• **Definition of a Factor**: A **factor** of a number is an exact divisor of that number; it divides the number completely without leaving any remainder.
• **Multiplication Link to Factors**: If two whole numbers multiply together to give a product, then each of those numbers is a factor of the product.
• **Example of Factors**: Since $6 \times 4 = 24$, both $6$ and $4$ are factors of $24$, and similarly, $24 \div 6 = 4$ with zero remainder.
• **Complete Set of Factors**: Finding all factors of a number requires listing every pair of whole numbers whose product equals the target number.
• **Factors of 12**: The factors of $12$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6,$ and $12$, because $1 \times 12 = 12$, $2 \times 6 = 12$, and $3 \times 4 = 12$.
• **Finite Nature of Factors**: Unlike multiples, the total number of factors for any given non-zero whole number is always **finite** and countable.
• **Smallest Factor Property**: The **smallest factor** of every non-zero whole number is always $1$, because $1$ divides every number evenly.
• **Greatest Factor Property**: The **greatest factor** of any number is the number itself, because a number cannot be divided evenly by anything greater than itself (excluding fractions or decimals).
• **Bounds of Factors**: Every factor of a number is either less than or equal to the number itself; no factor can exceed the number.
• **Factor Pairs**: Factors often occur in pairs; if $a \times b = c$, then $a$ and $b$ form a **factor pair** of the product $c$.
• **Symmetrical Factor Finding**: When finding factors, once the factor pairs meet in the middle (using square roots or sequential testing), all factors have been successfully discovered.
• **Number 1 as a Factor**: The number $1$ has only **one factor**, which is itself ($1$). This makes $1$ a unique number in number theory, neither prime nor composite.
• **Divisibility Rule Application**: Basic divisibility tests act as quick shortcuts to determine whether a small number is a factor of a larger number without performing full long division.
• **Distinction Between Multiple and Factor**: A **multiple** is larger or equal to the number (multiplication scaling up), whereas a **factor** is smaller or equal to the number (division scaling down).

• **Classification of Numbers**: Numbers can be classified into distinct categories based on the exact count of their factors, which is a core DAV curriculum learning objective.
• **Definition of Prime Numbers**: A **prime number** is a whole number greater than $1$ that has exactly two distinct factors: $1$ and the number itself.
• **Exclusivity of Primes**: Prime numbers cannot be formed by multiplying two smaller natural numbers other than $1$ and the number itself.
• **Example of Prime Numbers**: The number $7$ is prime because its only factors are $1$ and $7$. Other early primes include $2, 3, 5, 11, 13, 17,$ and $19$.
• **The Unique Even Prime**: The number **$2$ is the only even prime number**; all other even numbers are divisible by $2$ and therefore possess more than two factors.
• **Definition of Composite Numbers**: A **composite number** is a whole number greater than $1$ that has **more than two factors** (meaning it can be divided evenly by numbers other than $1$ and itself).
• **Example of Composite Numbers**: The number $4$ is composite because its factors are $1, 2,$ and $4$. The number $6$ is composite with factors $1, 2, 3,$ and $6$.
• **Composite Breakdown**: Every composite number can be broken down into products of prime numbers, a property known as prime factorization.
• **Status of Number 1**: The number **$1$ is neither prime nor composite** because it has only one factor ($1$), failing the requirement of having two factors (prime) or more than two factors (composite).
• **Status of Number 0**: The number **$0$ is neither prime nor composite**; it is a whole number with an infinite number of factors and unique arithmetic properties.
• **Sieve of Eratosthenes**: The **Sieve of Eratosthenes** is an ancient, efficient mathematical method used to find all prime numbers up to any given limit by systematically crossing out multiples of known primes.
• **Applying the Sieve**: To find primes up to $100$, keep $2$ and cross out all its multiples, then keep $3$ and cross out its remaining multiples, continuing this process for $5$ and $7$.
• **Primes Between 1 and 100**: There are exactly **$25$ prime numbers** between $1$ and $100$, a key factual retention point for DAV examinations.
• **Twin Primes**: **Twin primes** are pairs of prime numbers that have a difference of exactly $2$.
• **Examples of Twin Primes**: Famous twin prime pairs include $(3, 5)$, $(5, 7)$, $(11, 13)$, $(17, 19)$, and $(29, 31)$.

• **Introduction to Divisibility Rules**: **Divisibility rules** are systematic shortcuts used to test whether a given whole number can be divided exactly by another number without performing long division.
• **Divisibility Rule for 2**: A number is divisible by $2$ if its **ones digit (units digit) is an even number**, specifically $0, 2, 4, 6,$ or $8$.
• **Application for 2**: The number $486$ is divisible by $2$ because its units digit is $6$, whereas $487$ is not divisible by $2$ because $7$ is odd.
• **Divisibility Rule for 3**: A number is divisible by $3$ if the **sum of all its individual digits is a multiple of $3$** (or divisible by $3$).
• **Application for 3**: For the number $354$, add the digits: $3 + 5 + 4 = 12$. Since $12$ is a multiple of $3$, $354$ is fully divisible by $3$.
• **Divisibility Rule for 5**: A number is divisible by $5$ if its **ones digit is either $0$ or $5$**.
• **Application for 5**: Numbers like $785$ and $1,200$ are divisible by $5$ because their respective ending digits are $5$ and $0$.
• **Divisibility Rule for 10**: A number is divisible by $10$ if and only if its **ones digit is strictly $0$**.
• **Application for 10**: The number $4,590$ is divisible by $10$ because it ends in $0$, while $4,591$ leaves a remainder of $1$ when divided by $10$.
• **Divisibility Rule for 4**: A number is divisible by $4$ if the **number formed by its last two digits (tens and ones)** is divisible by $4$.
• **Application for 4**: In the number $7,324$, look at the last two digits ($24$). Since $24$ is divisible by $4$, the entire number $7,324$ is divisible by $4$.
• **Divisibility Rule for 6**: A number is divisible by $6$ if it is **simultaneously divisible by both $2$ and $3$**.
• **Application for 6**: To check $528$, first verify it ends in an even digit ($8$, so divisible by $2$), then sum the digits ($5 + 2 + 8 = 15$, divisible by $3$). Since both hold true, $528$ is divisible by $6$.
• **Divisibility Rule for 9**: A number is divisible by $9$ if the **sum of its digits is a multiple of $9$** (or divisible by $9$).
• **Application for 9**: For $7,290$, sum the digits: $7 + 2 + 9 + 0 = 18$. Since $18$ is divisible by $9$, the number $7,290$ is divisible by $9$.
• **Importance of Rules**: Mastering divisibility tests accelerates problem-solving in factor finding, prime factorization, and fraction simplification.

• **Prime Factorization Concept**: **Prime factorization** is the process of expressing a composite number as the product of its prime factors exclusively.
• **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**: Every composite number can be factored into prime numbers in only one way, regardless of the order in which the prime factors are multiplied.
• **Methods of Prime Factorization**: In Class 5 DAV mathematics, prime factorization is primarily performed using two systematic methods: the **Factor Tree Method** and the **Short Division Method**.
• **Factor Tree Method Overview**: The **Factor Tree Method** involves breaking down a composite number into a pair of factors, and then continuously breaking down the resulting factors until all ending branches are prime numbers.
• **Step-by-Step Factor Tree**: To find the prime factorization of $24$, start with $24$ splitting into $4$ and $6$. Then split $4$ into $2 \times 2$, and $6$ into $2 \times 3$.
• **Finalizing the Factor Tree**: Collect all the circled prime tree tips ($2, 2, 2,$ and $3$), yielding the prime factorization $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$.
• **Alternative Tree Branches**: Different initial factor pairs (e.g., starting $24$ with $3 \times 8$ instead of $4 \times 6$) will yield the exact same final set of prime factors.
• **Short Division Method Overview**: The **Short Division Method** (also called continuous division) involves dividing the composite number by the smallest possible prime number repeatedly until the quotient becomes $1$.
• **Step-by-Step Short Division**: To find the prime factorization of $36$, divide by the smallest prime $2$ to get $18$. Divide $18$ by $2$ to get $9$. Divide $9$ by $3$ to get $3$. Finally, divide $3$ by $3$ to get $1$.
• **Reading Short Division Results**: The divisors used on the left-hand side ($2, 2, 3, 3$) represent the prime factorization of $36$, written as $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3$.
• **Checking Work via Multiplication**: Always verify prime factorization results by multiplying the prime factors back together to ensure the product equals the original composite number.
• **Exponential Notation Preview**: While fully expanded multiplication (e.g., $2 \times 2 \times 3$) is standard in Class 5, recognizing repeated factors prepares students for powers and exponents.
• **Prime Factorization of Even Numbers**: The prime factorization of any even composite number will always begin by including the prime number $2$ as its first divisor.
• **Prime Factorization of Odd Numbers**: The prime factorization of an odd composite number will exclusively contain odd prime numbers ($3, 5, 7, 11,$ etc.), never $2$.
• **Utility in Higher Math**: Prime factorization is an indispensable tool required for efficiently calculating both the Highest Common Factor (HCF) and the Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) of larger numbers.

• **Introduction to HCF**: The **Highest Common Factor (HCF)**, also known as the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD), is a foundational concept in advanced number theory and fraction reduction.
• **Definition of HCF**: The **HCF** of two or more given numbers is the greatest whole number that divides each of the numbers exactly without leaving any remainder.
• **Method 1: Listing Factors**: The first method to find HCF is by **listing all the individual factors** of each given number and identifying the largest shared factor.
• **Example of Listing Factors**: To find the HCF of $12$ and $18$: list factors of $12$ ($1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$) and factors of $18$ ($1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$).
• **Identifying Common Factors**: The common factors shared by $12$ and $18$ are $1, 2, 3,$ and $6$. The greatest among these is **$6$**, making $6$ the HCF.
• **Method 2: Prime Factorization**: A more efficient method for larger numbers is finding the HCF using **Prime Factorization**.
• **Steps for Prime Factorization HCF**: First, find the prime factorization of each individual number, then identify the common prime factors shared across all sets.
• **Example of Prime Factorization HCF**: Find HCF of $24$ and $36$. Prime factorization of $24$ is $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$; prime factorization of $36$ is $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3$.
• **Multiplying Common Primes**: The shared prime factors between $24$ and $36$ are $2 \times 2 \times 3$. Multiplying these common primes gives $2 \times 2 \times 3 = 12$, so the HCF is $12$.
• **Method 3: Common Division Method**: The **Common Division Method** (or short division by prime factors) is another highly streamlined technique for calculating HCF.
• **Steps for Common Division**: Write the numbers in a row, divide them simultaneously by a common prime factor that divides all of them, and continue until no common prime factor greater than $1$ remains.
• **Co-prime Numbers Definition**: Two numbers are said to be **co-prime** (or relatively prime) if their **HCF is exactly $1$**.
• **Examples of Co-prime Numbers**: The numbers $8$ and $15$ are co-prime because their only common factor is $1$, even though neither $8$ nor $15$ is a prime number individually.
* **Consecutive Numbers Property**: Any two consecutive numbers (e.g., $15$ and $16$, or $24$ and $25$) are always co-prime, meaning their HCF is always $1$.
• **HCF and Number Magnitude**: The HCF of two or more numbers can never be greater than the smallest of the given numbers.

• **Introduction to LCM**: The **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** is the counterpart to the HCF, representing the smallest multiple shared by a set of numbers.
• **Definition of LCM**: The **LCM** of two or more given numbers is the smallest non-zero whole number that is a multiple of each of the numbers.
• **Method 1: Listing Multiples**: The introductory method for finding LCM is **listing the multiples** of each number until the first matching multiple appears.
• **Example of Listing Multiples**: To find the LCM of $4$ and $6$: list multiples of $4$ ($4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24$) and multiples of $6$ ($6, 12, 18, 24, 30$).
• **Identifying Common Multiples**: The common multiples in the lists are $12, 24, 36,$ etc. The lowest (smallest) among these is **$12$**, making $12$ the LCM of $4$ and $6$.
• **Method 2: Prime Factorization for LCM**: For larger numbers, finding LCM via **Prime Factorization** is much faster and more reliable than listing endless multiples.
• **Steps for Prime Factorization LCM**: Find the prime factorization of each number, then take the product of the highest power or maximum occurrences of each prime factor involved.
• **Example of Prime Factorization LCM**: Find LCM of $12$ and $18$. Prime factorization of $12$ is $2 \times 2 \times 3$; prime factorization of $18$ is $2 \times 3 \times 3$.
• **Selecting Maximum Factors**: For the prime $2$, it appears twice in $12$ and once in $18$ (take two $2$s). For the prime $3$, it appears once in $12$ and twice in $18$ (take two $3$s).
• **Calculating the LCM**: Multiply the selected maximum prime factors: $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 36$, making $36$ the LCM of $12$ and $18$.
• **Method 3: Common Division Method for LCM**: The **Common Division Method** is a compact table format used to find the LCM by continuous prime division.
• **Steps for Common Division LCM**: Write numbers in a row, divide by a prime number that divides at least one of the numbers, bring down undivided numbers, and continue until all quotients become $1$.
• **Final LCM Calculation**: Multiply all the prime divisors on the left-hand side of the common division table to get the final LCM.
• **Relationship Between Numbers and LCM**: The LCM of two or more numbers is always greater than or equal to the largest of the given numbers.
• **LCM of Co-prime Numbers**: If two numbers are co-prime, their **LCM is always equal to their direct product** (e.g., LCM of $4$ and $5$ is $4 \times 5 = 20$).

• **Crucial Mathematical Relationship**: There is a famous, fundamental mathematical relationship connecting two numbers, their HCF, and their LCM.
• **The Product Rule Formula**: For any two given whole numbers, **the product of the two numbers is always equal to the product of their HCF and their LCM**.
• **Mathematical Expression**: $\text{First Number} \times \text{Second Number} = \text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}$.
• **Verifying the Rule (Example)**: Let the two numbers be $12$ and $18$. Their product is $12 \times 18 = 216$.
• **Computing HCF and LCM for Verification**: For $12$ and $18$, we previously found that the $\text{HCF} = 6$ and the $\text{LCM} = 36$.
• **Multiplying HCF and LCM**: Multiplying their HCF and LCM yields $6 \times 36 = 216$.
• **Confirmation of Equality**: Since $216 = 216$, the product rule formula is successfully verified and proven for $12$ and $18$.
• **Finding Missing Values**: This formula allows students to find a missing value if any three of the four components (First Number, Second Number, HCF, LCM) are known.
• **Formula Rearrangement for HCF**: $\text{HCF} = \frac{\text{First Number} \times \text{Second Number}}{\text{LCM}}$.
• **Formula Rearrangement for LCM**: $\text{LCM} = \frac{\text{First Number} \times \text{Second Number}}{\text{HCF}}$.
• **Formula Rearrangement for a Number**: $\text{First Number} = \frac{\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}}{\text{Second Number}}$.
• **Limitation of the Rule**: Note that the special product formula ($\text{Product} = \text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}$) applies strictly to **pairs of numbers**, not sets of three or more numbers.
• **Application in Problem Solving**: DAV word problems frequently test this relationship by asking students to find the LCM when the HCF and the two numbers are provided.
• **Logical Sanity Check**: When solving DAV problems using this formula, ensure that the calculated LCM is always divisible by the calculated HCF.

• **Real-World Word Problems**: Chapter 3 concepts—multiples, factors, HCF, and LCM—are heavily applied to solve practical, real-world everyday problems.
• **Recognizing LCM Word Problems**: Word problems involving events that **repeat, recur, flash, ring, or sync together at regular intervals** almost always require calculating the **LCM**.
• **Classic LCM Scenario (Bells)**: If temple bells ring at intervals of $9, 12,$ and $15$ minutes, find when they will toll together next by calculating the LCM of $9, 12,$ and $15$.
• **Classic LCM Scenario (Running Tracks)**: If two runners complete a lap around a circular track in $18$ seconds and $24$ seconds respectively, they will meet at the starting line again at a time equal to the LCM of $18$ and $24$.
• **Recognizing HCF Word Problems**: Word problems involving **splitting items into equal groups, packing maximum items into identical boxes, cutting things into equal maximum lengths, or distributing items evenly without remainder** require calculating the **HCF**.
• **Classic HCF Scenario (Ribbon Cutting)**: If two ribbons of lengths $36\text{ cm}$ and $48\text{ cm}$ must be cut into pieces of equal maximum length without any waste, the length of each piece is the HCF of $36$ and $48$.
• **Classic HCF Scenario (Arranging Students)**: If $72$ boys and $90$ girls need to be arranged in maximum equal rows with each row having the exact same composition, find the number of rows using the HCF of $72$ and $90$.
• **Reading Comprehension in Math**: Successfully solving DAV word problems requires careful reading to determine whether the situation calls for grouping/dividing down (HCF) or scaling/repeating up (LCM).
• **Step-by-Step Problem Presentation**: DAV evaluation guidelines require students to write clear, descriptive statements for every line in word problems rather than just writing raw numbers.
• **Drafting Descriptive Statements**: Example statement: "Total number of bells = $3$", "Interval of first bell = $9$ minutes", followed by clear working out.
* **Checking Units**: Always ensure that all measurements given in a word problem are in the same units (e.g., converting meters to centimeters) before calculating HCF or LCM.
• **Avoiding Common Confusion**: Do not confuse "least" in LCM problems with subtraction; LCM asks for the *smallest common multiple*, which is always larger than or equal to the input numbers.
• **Avoiding Common Confusion**: Do not confuse "greatest" in HCF problems with addition; HCF asks for the *greatest common factor*, which is always smaller than or equal to the input numbers.
• **Checking Final Answers**: Always perform a quick logical check: does the HCF divide the original numbers evenly? Does the LCM divide evenly by the original numbers?
