• **Definition of Environment**: The term 'environment' is derived from the French word 'environer', which means 'to surround' or 'surroundings'.
• **Core Concept**: Environment refers to the sum total of all conditions, circumstances, and influences that surround and affect the life and development of an organism or a group of organisms.
• **Significance**: It encompasses all living and non-living things that interact with each other in a given space and time, forming the fundamental support system for all life forms on Earth.
• **Interdependence Principle**: No organism exists in isolation; every plant, animal, and human being depends continuously on various components of the environment for survival, growth, and reproduction.
• **Basic Dual Classification**: The environment is broadly classified into two major categories: Natural Environment and Human-Made Environment.
• **Natural Environment Definition**: It comprises all living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) occurrences that exist naturally on Earth without any direct human creation or intervention.
• **Biotic Components**: The living elements of the natural environment, which include plants (flora), animals (fauna), microorganisms, and human beings themselves.
• **Abiotic Components**: The non-living physical elements of the natural environment, such as land, water, air, sunlight, temperature, and minerals.
• **Human-Made Environment Definition**: It includes all structures, modifications, artifacts, and systems created, built, or altered by human beings using their intelligence, skills, and available technology.
• **Examples of Human-Made Environment**: Buildings, roads, bridges, factories, parks, monuments, railways, and various tools that modify the natural landscape to suit human needs.
• **Human Environment Definition**: It focuses specifically on individual human beings, their families, communities, and socio-cultural dimensions like economic systems, political institutions, and religious practices.
• **Dynamic Nature**: The environment is not static; it is constantly changing due to natural geological processes and continuous human interventions.
• **DAV Curriculum Focus**: The DAV Class 7 curriculum emphasizes understanding the delicate balance between human activities and natural environmental systems to foster sustainable living.

• **Natural Environment Scope**: The natural environment serves as the foundational physical matrix that supports all ecological interactions and life-sustaining processes on the planet.
• **Four Major Realms**: The natural environment is traditionally studied through its four distinct physical domains: Lithosphere, Hydrosphere, Atmosphere, and Biosphere.
• **Lithosphere Definition**: The solid outer crust of the Earth, composed of rocks and minerals, and covered by a thin layer of soil that sustains terrestrial life.
• **Etymological Root**: The word 'lithos' is derived from Greek, meaning 'stone' or 'rock', defining the hard, rocky shell of the globe.
• **Landforms of Lithosphere**: It includes diverse physical features such as mountains, plateaus, plains, valleys, deserts, and coastal regions.
• **Economic Importance of Lithosphere**: It provides land for human habitation, agriculture, forestry, and industrial development, besides being the primary source of metallic and non-metallic minerals.
• **Soil Formation**: The uppermost layer of the lithosphere is soil, formed over thousands of years through the weathering of rocks and the decomposition of organic matter.
• **Hydrosphere Definition**: The comprehensive water realm of the Earth, encompassing all forms of water found on, under, and above the surface.
• **Water Distribution**: Approximately 71% of the Earth's surface is covered by water, making the Earth uniquely known as the 'Blue Planet'.
• **Marine vs. Freshwater**: The vast majority of the hydrosphere (about 97.5%) is saline water contained in oceans and seas, while only a tiny fraction (2.5%) is fresh water.
• **Freshwater Reserves**: Freshwater is primarily locked in polar ice caps, glaciers, and deep underground aquifers, leaving a very small percentage available in rivers, lakes, and the atmosphere.
• **Hydrological Significance**: The hydrosphere is crucial for regulating global temperatures, driving weather patterns, and supplying the mandatory liquid water required by all living organisms.
• **Atmosphere Definition**: The gaseous envelope or blanket of air that surrounds the Earth, held in place by the planet's gravitational pull.
• **Composition of Air**: The atmosphere is a mechanical mixture of various gases, primarily nitrogen (about 78%) and oxygen (about 21%), with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, and trace gases.
• **Layered Structure**: The atmosphere is vertically stratified into distinct layers—troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere, and exosphere—based on temperature gradients and density.
• **Climatic Shield**: The atmosphere protects life on Earth by absorbing harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun and moderating temperature extremes between day and night.

• **Biosphere Definition**: The narrow zone of contact where the lithosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere interact, creating the unique conditions necessary for life to exist.
• **Global Ecosystem**: Often referred to as the 'zone of life', the biosphere spans from the deepest ocean floors to the highest mountain peaks where living organisms can survive.
• **Biotic Community**: The biosphere contains millions of species of plants, animals, fungi, and microscopic organisms, all intricately connected through food chains and food webs.
• **Energy Flow**: The sun acts as the primary external energy source for the biosphere, driving photosynthesis in green plants, which in turn feeds herbivores, carnivores, and decomposers.
• **Interlocking Spheres**: A change in any one of the four realms—lithosphere, hydrosphere, or atmosphere—inevitably triggers cascading effects across the biosphere.
• **Ecosystem Concept**: An ecosystem is a functional system formed by the interaction of all living organisms with each other and with the physical and chemical factors of the environment in which they live.
• **Ecosystem Scale**: Ecosystems can vary infinitely in scale, ranging from a small drop of water, a decaying log, or a single pond, to vast biomes like the Amazon rainforest or the Sahara Desert.
• **Trophic Levels**: Organisms within an ecosystem are categorized into producers (plants), consumers (herbivores and carnivores), and decomposers (bacteria and fungi).
• **Nutrient Cycling**: Ecosystems maintain ecological balance through the continuous cycling of essential nutrients like carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and phosphorus between living organisms and the physical environment.
• **Ecological Balance**: This refers to a stable state of equilibrium within a community of organisms where species coexist, interact, and reproduce sustainably without depleting resources.
• **Human Impact on Ecosystems**: Rapid industrialization, urbanization, and deforestation have severely disrupted natural ecosystems, leading to habitat destruction and loss of biodiversity.
• **Biodiversity Significance**: Biological diversity ensures ecosystem resilience, providing essential ecological services such as pollination, water purification, climate regulation, and soil fertility.
• **Food Chain Dynamics**: A linear sequence of organisms through which nutrients and energy pass as one organism eats another, starting with primary producers.
• **Food Web Complexity**: A complex network of interconnected food chains within a specific ecosystem, reflecting multiple feeding relationships and dietary adaptability among species.
• **Ecological Adaptation**: Living organisms undergo structural, physiological, and behavioral adaptations over generations to survive the specific abiotic conditions of their local environment.

• **Human Component of Environment**: Human beings are both active modifiers and integral components of the environment, possessing unique cognitive abilities to alter their surroundings.
• **Early Human Life**: Prehistoric humans lived in complete harmony with nature, directly depending on gathering, hunting, and scavenging for their daily survival without causing large-scale ecological damage.
• **Nomadic Lifestyle**: Early humans were nomads who moved from place to place in search of food, water, and shelter, leaving minimal permanent footprints on the natural landscape.
• **Major Turning Point (Neolithic Revolution)**: The transition from a nomadic lifestyle to settled agriculture marked the beginning of significant human modification of the natural environment.
• **Domestication of Animals**: Early humans began domesticating animals like dogs, sheep, goats, and cattle for milk, meat, labor, and companionship, altering animal populations and ecosystems.
• **Agricultural Development**: The discovery of farming allowed humans to produce surplus food, leading to permanent settlements, village communities, and the rise of early civilizations along river valleys.
• **Invention of the Wheel**: A monumental technological breakthrough that revolutionized transport, pottery-making, and trade, accelerating human mobility and cultural exchange.
• **Discovery of Fire**: Enabled humans to cook food, stay warm in cold climates, ward off wild predators, and eventually smelt metals to create advanced tools and weapons.
• **Metal Ages**: The sequential discovery and utilization of copper, bronze, and iron allowed humans to craft stronger tools, clear larger tracts of forests, and expand agricultural productivity.
• **Industrial Revolution**: The 18th-century shift from hand-production methods to machines, powered by coal, steam, and later electricity, exponentially increased human capacity to exploit natural resources.
• **Technological Advancements**: Modern science and technology have equipped humans with unprecedented power to manipulate physical geography, extract minerals, and travel globally in record time.
• **Urbanization Trends**: The massive migration of rural populations to cities has led to the expansion of metropolitan areas, transforming natural land covers into concrete jungles.
• **Economic Systems**: Modern human environments are driven by complex economic networks, including primary (agriculture/mining), secondary (manufacturing), and tertiary (services) sectors.
• **Socio-Cultural Dimensions**: Human environments include language, art, religion, traditions, laws, and educational institutions that shape human behavior, values, and community organization.
• **Resource Utilization**: Humans extract renewable and non-renewable resources from the lithosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere to fuel economic growth and maintain modern standards of living.

• **Human-Environment Interaction**: The two-way dynamic relationship where humans influence the environment through their actions, and the environment, in turn, shapes human lifestyles, cultures, and survival strategies.
• **Adaptation to Environment**: Human societies adapt their clothing, housing, food habits, and occupations to match the local climatic and topographical conditions of their region.
• **Modification of Environment**: Unlike other animals, humans do not merely adapt to their surroundings; they actively engineer, reshape, and modify the environment to maximize comfort and productivity.
• **Irrigation Systems**: Humans construct dams, canals, reservoirs, and tube wells to divert water from rivers to arid lands, enabling agriculture in otherwise dry regions.
• **Deforestation Consequences**: Large-scale clearing of forests for timber, agriculture, and urban expansion has destroyed vast wildlife habitats and reduced global carbon sequestration capacity.
• **Mining Operations**: Extraction of ores, coal, and petroleum leaves behind massive open pits, soil erosion, and toxic waste, severely damaging local terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems.
• **Environmental Degradation**: The deterioration of the environment through the depletion of natural resources such as air, water, and soil, and the destruction of ecosystems and wildlife extinction.
• **Pollution Definition**: The introduction of harmful contaminants, substances, or energy forms into the natural environment, causing adverse changes and health hazards to living organisms.
• **Air Pollution**: Contamination of the atmosphere by harmful gases (like carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide), particulate matter, and smoke emitted from factories, vehicles, and stubble burning.
• **Water Pollution**: Contamination of water bodies like rivers, lakes, and oceans through the discharge of untreated industrial effluents, agricultural pesticides, and domestic sewage.
• **Soil Pollution (Land Degradation)**: The decline in soil quality and fertility caused by excessive chemical fertilizers, pesticides, industrial waste dumping, and improper solid waste disposal.
• **Noise Pollution**: Excessive, disruptive sound levels generated by heavy traffic, industrial machinery, construction activities, and loudspeakers, causing stress and hearing impairment.
• **Global Warming**: The long-term heating of Earth's climate system observed since the pre-industrial period due to human activities, primarily fossil fuel burning, which increases heat-trapping greenhouse gases.
• **Greenhouse Effect**: A natural atmospheric process where gases like carbon dioxide and methane trap heat radiating from Earth's surface; intensified by human pollution, leading to global temperature rises.
• **Climate Change**: Significant, long-term shifts in global weather patterns and temperatures, resulting in melting polar ice, rising sea levels, severe droughts, and unpredictable extreme weather events.

• **Need for Environmental Conservation**: Conservation refers to the sustainable management, protection, and wise use of natural resources to ensure they are available for present and future generations.
• **Intergenerational Equity**: The ethical principle that current generations must manage and utilize environmental resources responsibly so that future generations are not deprived of a habitable planet.
• **Resource Classification**: Natural resources are broadly classified into renewable resources (which can replenish naturally, e.g., solar and wind energy) and non-renewable resources (which exist in finite quantities, e.g., coal and petroleum).
• **Overexploitation Dangers**: Unchecked consumption and wasteful usage of non-renewable fossil fuels and minerals risk total exhaustion, threatening modern energy grids and economic stability.
• **Sustainable Development Definition**: Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, balancing economic growth with ecological protection.
• **Core Pillars of Sustainability**: Sustainable development rests on three interconnected pillars: economic viability, social inclusion, and environmental protection.
• **Role of Individuals**: Every individual plays a vital role in environmental protection through conscious daily choices, such as reducing waste, conserving water, and minimizing energy consumption.
• **Reduce, Reuse, Recycle (3Rs Principle)**: A foundational waste management framework aimed at minimizing resource extraction and landfill accumulation.
• **Reduce Principle**: Consuming fewer resources, minimizing impulse purchases, turning off unnecessary lights, and preventing food and water wastage at the source.
• **Reuse Principle**: Finding secondary uses for items instead of discarding them prematurely, such as using glass jars for storage, donating old clothes, or repurposing containers.
• **Recycle Principle**: Processing used materials (like paper, glass, plastic, and aluminum) into new, usable products through industrial reprocessing, thereby saving energy and raw materials.
• **Afforestation and Reforestation**: Planting trees on barren lands (afforestation) and replanting trees in depleted forests (reforestation) to restore green cover, absorb carbon dioxide, and stabilize soils.
• **Wildlife Protection**: Establishing national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, and passing stringent anti-poaching laws to protect endangered species and preserve biodiversity.
• **Alternative Energy Sources**: Transitioning away from polluting fossil fuels toward clean, renewable energy sources such as solar power, wind energy, hydroelectricity, geothermal energy, and biomass.
• **Environmental Education**: Integrating environmental science and ecological awareness into school curricula to foster eco-conscious values among students from an early age.

• **Global Environmental Initiatives**: International cooperation is essential because environmental problems like global warming, acid rain, and ocean pollution do not respect national boundaries.
• **United Nations Role**: The UN plays a pivotal leadership role in organizing global summits, establishing environmental treaties, and formulating international guidelines for sustainable development.
• **Stockholm Conference (1972)**: The first major UN conference on international environmental issues, which placed environmental protection on the global agenda and led to the creation of UNEP.
• **UNEP (United Nations Environment Programme)**: The leading global environmental authority that sets the global environmental agenda, promotes sustainable implementation within the UN system, and advocates for environmental protection.
• **Brundtland Commission (1987)**: Formally known as the World Commission on Environment and Development, it popularized the concept of 'Sustainable Development' in its landmark report *Our Common Future*.
• **Earth Summit (Rio de Janeiro, 1992)**: A monumental UN conference that produced Agenda 21, a comprehensive action plan of global, national, and local actions by organizations of the UN system and governments.
• **Paris Agreement (2015)**: A legally binding international treaty on climate change adopted by nearly all nations, aiming to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius compared to pre-industrial levels.
• **World Environment Day**: Celebrated annually on June 5th worldwide to raise global awareness and encourage action for the protection of our environment.
• **Earth Hour**: A global grassroots movement uniting people to protect the planet by turning off non-essential electric lights for one hour as a symbol of commitment to the planet.
• **Chipko Movement**: A famous non-violent, forest conservation movement in India (1970s) where villagers, notably women, hugged trees to prevent them from being felled by commercial loggers.
• **Appiko Movement**: A decentralized environmental movement in southern India (Karnataka) inspired by the Chipko movement, aimed at saving the Western Ghats' tropical forests.
• **Silent Valley Movement**: A successful environmental protest in Kerala, India, in the late 1970s and 1980s to protect an ecologically rich tropical evergreen forest from being flooded by a hydroelectric project.
• **National Environmental Policies**: India has enacted robust legislative frameworks, such as the Wildlife Protection Act (1972), Water Act (1974), Air Act (1981), and Environment (Protection) Act (1986).
• **Judicial Activism in India**: The Supreme Court of India has played a proactive role in environmental jurisprudence, introducing landmark judgments on pollution control, wildlife conservation, and the 'Right to a Clean Environment' as part of the Right to Life (Article 21).
• **Eco-friendly Practices**: Adopting green habits like using public transport, carpooling, bicycling, walking, using cloth bags, avoiding single-use plastics, and harvesting rainwater.

• **Review of Lithosphere Concepts**: The lithosphere provides the foundational relief features that dictate human migration routes, agricultural patterns, and urban settlement layouts across continents.
• **Review of Hydrosphere Dynamics**: The hydrological cycle (water cycle)—driven by solar energy—continuously evaporates water from oceans, forms clouds, precipitates as rain or snow, and returns water via rivers back to the sea.
• **Atmospheric Circulation**: Global wind patterns, driven by unequal solar heating of the Earth's surface, distribute heat and moisture across latitudes, shaping global climatic zones.
• **Biomes of the Biosphere**: Distinct ecological communities adapted to specific climatic conditions, ranging from tropical rainforests and temperate grasslands to tundras and deserts.
• **Ecological Interdependence**: Every organism plays a specific ecological niche; removing even a single species can cause a trophic cascade that destabilizes an entire ecosystem.
• **Human-Made vs. Natural Balance**: Maintaining the balance between natural environments and human-made infrastructure is the ultimate challenge of modern urban and regional planning.
• **Technological Impact**: While technology has elevated human living standards, uncontrolled technological expansion has introduced synthetic pollutants (like microplastics and e-waste) that persist in the environment for centuries.
• **Urban Heat Islands**: Metropolitan areas that experience significantly warmer temperatures than surrounding rural areas due to human activities, heat-trapping concrete structures, and lack of vegetation.
• **Deforestation Impacts on Climate**: Loss of forests diminishes the Earth's ability to absorb carbon dioxide, directly accelerating global warming and altering local rainfall patterns.
• **Soil Erosion Mechanisms**: Removal of topsoil by wind and water agents, aggravated by overgrazing, deforestation, and faulty agricultural practices, leading to reduced agricultural productivity and desertification.
• **Water Scarcity Issues**: Over-extraction of groundwater, erratic monsoons, and water pollution have created severe freshwater shortages in many urban and rural regions around the world.
• **Role of Education in Conservation**: DAV schools emphasize values-based education, encouraging students to develop a deep reverence for nature in alignment with ancient Vedic traditions of respecting the five basic elements (Panchabhutas).
• **Panchabhutas Concept**: Ancient Indian philosophy recognizes the physical universe as being composed of five basic elements: Earth (Prithvi), Water (Jal), Fire (Agni), Air (Vayu), and Space (Akasha).
• **Vedic View of Environment**: Traditional Indian thought views nature as sacred and divine, advocating for peaceful coexistence with all living creatures rather than aggressive dominion over them.
• **Synthesis of Ancient Wisdom and Modern Science**: Combining traditional respect for nature with modern scientific conservation techniques provides the most effective pathway toward achieving long-term environmental sustainability.

• **Key Terms Summary - Environment**: The sum total of all living and non-living elements and their interactions that surround an organism.
• **Key Terms Summary - Biotic**: The living components of an environment, including plants, animals, and microorganisms.
• **Key Terms Summary - Abiotic**: The non-living physical components of an environment, including land, water, air, and sunlight.
• **Key Terms Summary - Lithosphere**: The solid rocky outer crust of the Earth.
• **Key Terms Summary - Hydrosphere**: The total water component of the Earth, including oceans, seas, rivers, lakes, and underground water.
• **Key Terms Summary - Atmosphere**: The gaseous envelope of air surrounding the Earth.
• **Key Terms Summary - Biosphere**: The narrow zone of contact where land, water, and air interact to support life.
• **Key Terms Summary - Ecosystem**: A community of living organisms interacting with each other and their non-living physical environment.
• **Key Terms Summary - Human-Made Environment**: Structures, artifacts, and systems created by humans using technology.
• **Key Terms Summary - Sustainable Development**: Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
• **Key Terms Summary - Biodiversity**: The variety of plant and animal life in the world or in a particular habitat.
• **Key Terms Summary - Global Warming**: The gradual increase in the overall temperature of Earth's atmosphere due to greenhouse gas emissions.
• **Key Terms Summary - Deforestation**: The permanent clearance or removal of forest trees on a large scale.
• **Key Terms Summary - Afforestation**: The process of planting trees on barren or uncultivated land to create a forest.
• **Key Terms Summary - Pollution**: The presence of harmful or poisonous substances in the natural environment.
