• **Subject and Grade**: Class 7, Science (The Living World: Science - DAV Public School Board).
• **Chapter Title**: Chapter 3 - Chemical Substances and Processes.
• **Core Focus**: Understanding matter, elements, compounds, mixtures, chemical symbols, formulas, and chemical changes.
• **Matter Definition**: Anything that has mass, occupies space, and can be perceived by our senses.
• **Composition of Matter**: Made up of extremely small, discrete particles called atoms and molecules.
• **Pure Substances**: Matter that has a fixed composition and specific, constant properties throughout.
• **Classification of Pure Substances**: Broadly divided into two distinct categories: Elements and Compounds.
• **Impure Substances**: Matter composed of two or more pure substances physically mixed in any ratio, known as mixtures.
• **Element Definition**: A pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by physical or chemical methods.
• **Basic Unit of Element**: Consists of identical atoms of the same kind.
• **Total Known Elements**: There are 118 known elements in the universe, occurring naturally and artificially.
• **Classification of Elements**: Elements are systematically classified into metals, non-metals, metalloids, and noble gases.
• **Metal Properties**: Generally lustrous, malleable, ductile, sonorous, and good conductors of heat and electricity.
• **Non-Metal Properties**: Poor conductors of heat and electricity, brittle in solid state, and lack luster.
• **Metalloids**: Elements that exhibit intermediate properties between those of typical metals and non-metals.
• **Noble Gases**: Elements with completely filled outermost electron shells, making them chemically inert and unreactive.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Compound Definition**: A pure substance composed of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion by mass.
• **Properties of Compounds**: The properties of a compound are entirely different from the properties of its constituent elements.
• **Fixed Ratio**: Water ($H_2O$) always contains hydrogen and oxygen combined in a fixed mass ratio of 1:8, regardless of its source.
• **Separation of Compounds**: The constituents of a compound cannot be separated by simple physical methods; chemical methods are required.
• **Energy Change**: Formation of a compound is invariably accompanied by a noticeable absorption or release of energy, usually heat or light.
• **Mixture Definition**: A physical combination of two or more substances in any arbitrary proportion where each substance retains its original properties.
• **Homogeneous Mixtures**: Mixtures that have a uniform composition throughout, such as a solution of salt in water.
• **Heterogeneous Mixtures**: Mixtures that do not have a uniform composition throughout, such as a mixture of sand and iron filings.
• **Distinction from Compounds**: Unlike compounds, the components of a mixture retain their individual chemical and physical properties.
• **Separation of Mixtures**: Components of mixtures can be easily separated using physical methods like filtration, evaporation, distillation, or magnetic separation.
• **Chemical Symbols Definition**: Short, standardized abbreviations used to represent the names of chemical elements.
• **J.J. Berzelius System**: Proposed the modern system of using the first letter, or the first two letters, of the English or Latin name of the element.
• **Single Letter Symbols**: Carbon is represented by 'C', Nitrogen by 'N', Oxygen by 'O', and Hydrogen by 'H'.
• **Two Letter Symbols**: Calcium is represented by 'Ca', Chlorine by 'Cl', Aluminium by 'Al', and Magnesium by 'Mg'.
• **Latin-Derived Symbols**: Sodium is represented by 'Na' (from Natrium), Potassium by 'K' (from Kalium), and Iron by 'Fe' (from Ferrum).
• **Significance of Symbols**: Not only represent the name of the element but also stand for one atom of that specific element.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Chemical Formula Definition**: A symbolic representation of a molecule of an element or a compound using chemical symbols and numerical subscripts.
• **Formula of Elemental Molecules**: Indicates the number of atoms present in a single molecule of an element, such as $O_2$ for oxygen gas.
• **Atomicity**: The total number of atoms contained within a single molecule of an element (e.g., Monoatomic, Diatomic, Polyatomic).
• **Monoatomic Elements**: Noble gases like Helium ($He$), Neon ($Ne$), and Argon ($Ar$) exist as single, independent atoms.
• **Diatomic Elements**: Elements like Hydrogen ($H_2$), Nitrogen ($N_2$), and Chlorine ($Cl_2$) exist naturally as molecules containing two atoms.
• **Triatomic and Polyatomic Elements**: Ozone exists as a triatomic molecule ($O_3$), while Phosphorus exists as a tetra-atomic molecule ($P_4$).
• **Formula of Compounds**: Expresses the exact types and relative numbers of atoms of each constituent element present in a compound.
• **Water Formula**: Represented as $H_2O$, indicating that two atoms of hydrogen are chemically combined with one atom of oxygen.
• **Carbon Dioxide Formula**: Represented as $CO_2$, showing one carbon atom combined with two oxygen atoms.
• **Sodium Chloride Formula**: Represented as $NaCl$, depicting a 1:1 ratio of sodium and chlorine ions in the ionic lattice.
• **Valency Definition**: The combining capacity of an element, representing the number of hydrogen atoms that can combine with or displace one atom of that element.
• **Monovalent Elements**: Elements with a valency of 1, such as Sodium ($Na^+$), Potassium ($K^+$), and Chlorine ($Cl^-$).
• **Divalent Elements**: Elements with a valency of 2, such as Magnesium ($Mg^{2+}$), Calcium ($Ca^{2+}$), and Oxygen ($O^{2-}$).
• **Trivalent Elements**: Elements with a valency of 3, such as Aluminium ($Al^{3+}$) and Iron(III) ($Fe^{3+}$).
• **Variable Valency**: Certain transition metals, like iron and copper, exhibit more than one valency (e.g., $Fe^{2+}$ and $Fe^{3+}$).
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Writing Chemical Formulas**: A systematic procedure involving the identification of constituent elements, their symbols, and their respective valencies.
• **Step 1 - Symbol and Valency**: Write the symbols of the constituent radicals or elements side-by-side, with the positive radical/metal on the left and the negative radical/non-metal on the right.
• **Step 2 - Cross-Over Method**: Write the respective valency numbers as subscripts at the lower right corner of the alternative elemental symbols.
• **Step 3 - Valency Exchange**: Interchange the valency numbers of the combining atoms as subscripts to determine the final formula.
• **Simplifying Subscripts**: If the subscript numbers have a common factor, divide throughout by that highest common factor to get the simplest molecular formula.
• **Formula of Magnesium Chloride**: Magnesium has valency 2 ($Mg$) and Chlorine has valency 1 ($Cl$); crossing over gives $MgCl_2$.
• **Formula of Aluminium Oxide**: Aluminium has valency 3 ($Al$) and Oxygen has valency 2 ($O$); crossing over yields $Al_2O_3$.
• **Formula of Calcium Hydroxide**: Calcium has valency 2 ($Ca$) and Hydroxide radical has valency 1 ($OH^-$); written as $Ca(OH)_2$.
• **Chemical Equations**: A concise mathematical statement using symbols and formulas to represent a chemical reaction.
• **Reactants**: The starting substances that undergo chemical change, conventionally written on the left-hand side of a chemical equation.
• **Products**: The new substances formed as a result of the chemical reaction, written on the right-hand side of a chemical equation.
• **Arrow Sign ($\rightarrow$)}: Points from the reactants to the products, signifying the direction of the ongoing chemical transformation.
• **Plus Sign ($+$)**: Used to separate multiple reactants or multiple products on either side of the chemical equation.
• **Word Equation**: A preliminary representation of a chemical reaction using full names of reactants and products instead of chemical symbols.
• **Skeletal Equation**: An unbalanced chemical equation that gives the correct symbols and formulas of reactants and products without satisfying mass conservation.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Law of Conservation of Mass**: States that mass can neither be created nor destroyed during any ordinary physical or chemical change.
• **Implication for Equations**: The total mass of all reactants must exactly equal the total mass of all products formed in a reaction.
• **Balanced Chemical Equation**: An equation in which the total number of atoms of each individual element is equal on both the reactant and product sides.
• **Need for Balancing**: Skeletal equations violate the law of conservation of mass unless adjusted to have equal atomic counts on both sides.
• **Step-by-Step Balancing**: Count the number of atoms of each element on both sides of the skeletal equation before altering any coefficients.
• **Coefficient Adjustment**: Place suitable whole-number coefficients before the chemical formulas to equalize the number of atoms, never changing the subscripts.
• **Balancing Water Formation**: $2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O$ ensures 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms on both sides.
• **Physical States in Equations**: To provide more information, physical states are denoted as $(s)$ for solid, $(l)$ for liquid, $(g)$ for gas, and $(aq)$ for aqueous solution.
• **Chemical Change Definition**: A permanent change in which one or more new substances with entirely different chemical properties and compositions are formed.
• **Irreversibility**: Most chemical changes are permanent and cannot be easily reversed by simple physical methods.
• **Energy Changes in Reactions**: Chemical changes are invariably accompanied by absorption or evolution of heat, light, sound, or electricity.
• **Characteristics of Chemical Changes**: Formation of new products, evolution of gas, change in color, change in temperature, and precipitation.
• **Physical Change Definition**: A temporary change affecting only physical properties like shape, size, state, or color, without forming any new substances.
• **Reversibility of Physical Changes**: Easily reversible by reversing the physical conditions, such as cooling or melting.
• **No New Substance**: The chemical composition of the substance remains completely unaltered throughout a physical process.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Types of Chemical Reactions**: Chemical reactions are broadly categorized into combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement reactions.
• **Combination Reaction (Synthesis)**: Two or more reactants combine chemically to form a single, more complex product ($A + B \rightarrow AB$).
• **Example of Combination**: Burning of magnesium ribbon in air: $2Mg_{(s)} + O_2_{(g)} \rightarrow 2MgO_{(s)}$.
• **Slaking of Lime**: Reaction of quicklime ($CaO$) with water ($H_2O$) to form slaked lime ($Ca(OH)_2$) releasing massive amounts of heat.
• **Exothermic Reactions**: Chemical processes that release thermal energy into the surroundings, resulting in a temperature increase.
• **Decomposition Reaction**: A single complex reactant breaks down into two or more simpler substances upon the application of heat, light, or electricity ($AB \rightarrow A + B$).
• **Thermal Decomposition**: Breaking down of limestone ($CaCO_3$) into quicklime ($CaO$) and carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) upon strong heating.
• **Endothermic Reactions**: Chemical processes that absorb thermal energy or light from their surroundings to proceed.
• **Electrolytic Decomposition**: Breakdown of acidified water into hydrogen and oxygen gases using an electric current (electrolysis).
• **Displacement Reaction**: A chemical reaction where a more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its aqueous salt solution ($A + BC \rightarrow AC + B$).
• **Iron and Copper Sulfate**: When an iron nail is dipped in a blue copper sulfate solution, iron displaces copper, turning the solution green and depositing brown copper.
• **Reactivity Series**: A vertical arrangement of metals in order of their decreasing chemical reactivity.
• **Position of Hydrogen**: Metals placed above hydrogen in the reactivity series can displace hydrogen from dilute acids, whereas metals below cannot.
• **Double Displacement Reaction**: A reaction where two ionic compounds exchange their positive and negative ions to form two new compounds ($AB + CD \rightarrow AD + CB$).
• **Precipitation Reactions**: A common type of double displacement reaction characterized by the formation of an insoluble solid called a precipitate.

• **Oxidation Definition**: A chemical process involving the addition of oxygen, the removal of hydrogen, or the loss of electrons by a substance.
• **Reduction Definition**: A chemical process involving the addition of hydrogen, the removal of oxygen, or the gain of electrons by a substance.
• **Redox Reactions**: Chemical reactions in which oxidation and reduction take place simultaneously; one reactant gets oxidized while the other gets reduced.
• **Oxidizing Agent**: The substance that oxidizes another substance by providing oxygen or accepting electrons, and gets reduced in the process.
• **Reducing Agent**: The substance that reduces another substance by removing oxygen or supplying electrons, and gets oxidized in the process.
• **Combustion**: A high-temperature exothermic chemical reaction between a fuel (usually a hydrocarbon) and an oxidant, producing oxidized products, heat, and light.
• **Requirements for Combustion**: Presence of a combustible fuel, presence of a supporter of combustion (oxygen), and attainment of the ignition temperature.
• **Ignition Temperature**: The minimum lowest temperature at which a combustible substance catches fire and starts burning.
• **Rapid Combustion**: A type of combustion where a substance burns rapidly and produces a large amount of heat and light in a short span, like LPG burning.
• **Spontaneous Combustion**: A type of combustion that occurs without any external application of heat, caused by internal self-heating (e.g., phosphorus in air).
• **Explosion**: A sudden, violent chemical reaction accompanied by the rapid evolution of heat, light, sound, and a large volume of expanding gases.
• **Flame Definition**: A luminous, burning zone of gas where combustion of a gaseous or vaporized fuel takes place.
• **Non-Luminous Zone**: The outermost zone of a complete combustion flame where the temperature is highest and complete oxidation occurs.
• **Luminous Zone**: The middle zone of incomplete combustion where unburnt carbon particles glow, producing a bright yellow light.
• **Dark Zone**: The innermost zone surrounding the wick, consisting of unburnt fuel vapors with the lowest temperature.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Corrosion Definition**: The gradual eating away and deterioration of metals due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with atmospheric moisture, oxygen, and acids.
• **Rusting of Iron**: A specific type of corrosion where iron reacts with atmospheric oxygen and moisture to form hydrated iron(III) oxide ($Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O$).
• **Chemical Formula of Rust**: Hydrated ferric oxide, represented as $Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O$, which appears as a flaky, reddish-brown coating.
• **Conditions Necessary for Rusting**: Simultaneous presence of both oxygen gas ($O_2$) and moisture or water vapor ($H_2O$).
• **Prevention of Corrosion**: Protecting metal surfaces from coming into direct contact with atmospheric moisture and oxygen.
• **Painting and Greasing**: Applying a layer of paint, grease, or oil on iron surfaces to physically block air and water.
• **Galvanization**: The process of coating iron or steel sheets with a thin, protective layer of molten zinc to prevent rusting.
• **Electroplating**: Depositing a layer of a corrosion-resistant metal, such as chromium or nickel, onto iron objects using electricity.
• **Alloying**: Mixing iron with other metals like chromium and nickel to produce stainless steel, which does not rust easily.
• **Effects of Corrosion**: Causes massive structural damage to bridges, iron pillars, automobiles, and machinery, leading to heavy economic losses globally.
• **Atmospheric Oxidation**: Various metals like copper and silver also corrode over time, developing colored coatings like green patina on copper and black tarnish on silver.
• **Green Coating on Copper**: Formed due to the conversion of copper into basic copper carbonate ($CuCO_3 \cdot Cu(OH)_2$) upon exposure to moist air.
• **Black Tarnishing of Silver**: Occurs when silver reacts with trace amounts of hydrogen sulfide ($H_2S$) gas present in polluted air to form silver sulfide ($Ag_2S$).
• **Food Spoilage and Rancidity**: Oxidation of fats and oils present in food items results in unpleasant smells and tastes, known as rancidity.
• **Prevention of Rancidity**: Storing food in airtight containers, adding antioxidants, or flushing food packets (like potato chips) with inert nitrogen gas.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Acidic Oxides**: Oxides formed when non-metals react with oxygen, which dissolve in water to form acidic solutions that turn blue litmus red.
• **Example of Acidic Oxide**: Sulfur dioxide ($SO_2$) dissolves in water to form sulfurous acid ($H_2SO_3$).
• **Basic Oxides**: Oxides formed when metals react with oxygen, which dissolve in water to form basic or alkaline solutions that turn red litmus blue.
• **Example of Basic Oxide**: Magnesium oxide ($MgO$) dissolves in water to form magnesium hydroxide ($Mg(OH)_2$).
• **Amphoteric Oxides**: Metallic oxides that show both acidic and basic behavior, reacting with both acids and bases to form salt and water (e.g., $ZnO$, $Al_2O_3$).
• **Neutral Oxides**: Oxides that show neither acidic nor basic properties, such as carbon monoxide ($CO$), nitrous oxide ($N_2O$), and water ($H_2O$).
• **Acids in Daily Life**: Substances containing acidic compounds, like acetic acid in vinegar, citric acid in citrus fruits, and lactic acid in curd.
• **Bases in Daily Life**: Chemical substances that are bitter to touch and soapy, such as sodium hydroxide ($NaOH$) and calcium hydroxide ($Ca(OH)_2$).
• **Neutralization Reaction**: A chemical reaction between an acid and a base resulting in the formation of salt and water along with the release of heat.
• **General Neutralization Equation**: $Acid + Base \rightarrow Salt + Water + Heat$.
• **Antacids in Medicine**: Mild bases like milk of magnesia ($Mg(OH)_2$) used to neutralize excess hydrochloric acid in the stomach and cure acidity.
• **Soil Treatment**: Acidic soils caused by acid rain or excessive fertilizer use are treated with basic substances like quicklime ($CaO$) or slaked lime ($Ca(OH)_2$).
• **Factory Waste Treatment**: Industrial effluents containing strong acids must be neutralized with basic materials before being safely discharged into water bodies.
• **Ant Sting Treatment**: Formic acid injected by ants can be neutralized by rubbing a weak base like baking soda or calamine lotion on the skin.
• **Wasp Sting Treatment**: Wasp stings are alkaline in nature and can be neutralized safely by applying a mild household acid like vinegar.

• **Summary of Matter**: Matter exists in pure forms (elements and compounds) and impure forms (mixtures), forming the structural foundation of the universe.
• **Importance of Symbols**: Chemical symbols and formulas provide a universal, standardized shorthand language understood by scientists worldwide.
• **Conservation Principle**: Chemical equations obey the law of conservation of mass and must always be balanced to reflect actual scientific measurements.
• **Versatility of Reactions**: Chemical reactions drive natural processes and industrial innovations through combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement mechanisms.
• **Redox Significance**: Oxidation and reduction processes are crucial for energy production, respiration, combustion, and preventing metallic corrosion.
• **Combatting Corrosion**: Understanding the chemical nature of rusting and corrosion helps in developing advanced protective coatings like galvanization and alloying.
• **Everyday Chemistry**: Neutralization reactions play a vital role in agriculture, medicine, and environmental protection by maintaining chemical balance.
• **Significance of Energy**: Energy changes accompanying physical and chemical processes dictate whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic.
• **Purity of Substances**: Separation techniques allow scientists to isolate pure elements and compounds from complex natural mixtures.
• **Role of Catalysts**: Certain substances alter the rate of chemical reactions without themselves undergoing any permanent chemical change during the process.
• **Atomic Theory Integration**: Dalton's atomic theory provides the theoretical backbone for understanding why atoms combine in fixed whole-number ratios.
• **Environmental Chemistry**: Analyzing combustion products and atmospheric pollutants helps mitigate harmful greenhouse effects and acid rain.
• **Academic Rigor**: Mastering chemical nomenclature, valency calculations, and equation balancing forms the core competency of middle-school chemistry.
