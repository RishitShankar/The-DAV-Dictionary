• **The Reading Process**: Divided into three distinct cognitive phases: Pre-reading, During-reading (While-reading), and Post-reading.
• **Pre-reading Strategies**: Preparing the mind before touching the text by previewing titles, headings, illustrations, and chapter summaries.
• **Activating Prior Knowledge**: Brainstorming what one already knows about a topic to create a mental framework for new information.
• **Predicting Outcomes**: Formulating educated guesses about what the text will discuss based on visual cues and structural headings.
• **Setting Objectives**: Establishing a clear purpose or question that the reader intends to answer by the end of the text.
• **During-reading Strategies**: Monitoring comprehension in real-time and adjusting reading speed based on the complexity of the material.
• **Mental Imagery**: Visualizing scenes, characters, or concepts described in the text to enhance retention and experiential understanding.
• **Self-Monitoring**: Pausing periodically to ask, "Does this make sense?" and employing fix-up strategies when comprehension breaks down.
• **Annotation Techniques**: Underlining key phrases, circling unknown words, and jotting down marginal notes to maintain active focus.
• **Identifying Main Ideas**: Locating the central theme or core message that the author wishes to convey in a specific section.
• **Distinguishing Details**: Separating major supporting arguments from minor, illustrative examples or background trivia.
• **Post-reading Strategies**: Reflecting upon, summarizing, and synthesizing the information gathered after finishing the text.
• **Consolidation**: Reviewing notes, answering mental recap questions, and discussing the reading with peers to solidify memory pathways.
• **Critical Evaluation**: Assessing the credibility, tone, and objectivity of the author rather than accepting the text blindly.
• **Application**: Transferring the knowledge gained from the text into practical scenarios or linking it to real-world experiences.
• **Metacognition**: Developing an awareness of one's own thought processes while reading to continuously improve comprehension efficiency.

• **Main Idea and Supporting Details**: The foundational structural relationship in non-fiction and informational reading passages.
• **Main Idea Definition**: The primary point, central concept, or overarching message that the author communicates in a paragraph or passage.
• **Locating the Main Idea**: Often found in the opening sentence (topic sentence) or the concluding sentence of a paragraph.
• **Implied Main Ideas**: Situations where the author does not explicitly state the main idea, requiring the reader to synthesize clues.
• **Supporting Details Definition**: Facts, statistics, examples, anecdotes, and descriptions that back up, explain, or prove the main idea.
• **The Table Analogy**: Visualizing the main idea as the tabletop and the supporting details as the legs that hold it up.
• **Identifying Supporting Facts**: Asking "What evidence does the author provide to prove this point?" during the reading process.
• **Irrelevant Details**: Recognizing information that strays from the core topic, which helps in summarizing and filtering text.
• **Paragraph Mapping**: Drawing mental or physical graphic organizers to map the hierarchy of main ideas versus supporting details.
• **Topic Identification vs. Main Idea**: Differentiating between the *topic* (what the text is about in a few words) and the *main idea* (what the author says *about* the topic).
• **Paragraph Focus**: Recognizing how paragraphs transition from one main idea to another to maintain logical flow.
• **Signpost Words**: Recognizing transitional words (furthermore, however, similarly) that signal shifts in supporting details or main arguments.
• **Summarization Link**: Understanding that an accurate summary is simply the main idea combined with the most crucial supporting details.
• **Exercise Application**: Applying main-idea extraction techniques to unseen passages provided in the DAV English Practice Book.
• **Analytical Rigor**: Training the Class 6 intellect to look beyond surface-level storytelling to grasp underlying informational structures.
• **Reading Efficiency**: Using main-idea identification to speed up reading without sacrificing comprehension quality.

• **Context Clues**: Unlocking the meanings of unfamiliar words using the surrounding text without immediately reaching for a dictionary.
• **Definition Clues**: Instances where the author directly defines a difficult word right within the sentence or the immediately following clause.
• **Synonym Clues**: The presence of a familiar word with a similar meaning close to the unfamiliar word, often separated by commas or dashes.
• **Antonym Clues**: The use of contrast words (unlike, however, on the other hand) that reveal the opposite meaning of an unknown term.
• **Example Clues**: Specific illustrations or instances provided by the author that give hints about the category or nature of the unknown word.
• **Inference Clues**: Relying on general context, logic, and background experience to deduce the meaning of a word from the overall situation.
• **Root Words and Affixes**: Recognizing prefixes and suffixes as structural context clues that modify word meanings from within.
• **Grammatical Clues**: Using the part of speech (noun, verb, adjective) of the unknown word to narrow down its potential meaning.
• **Contextual Practice**: Analyzing sentences where a nonsense word is used and figuring out its definition based purely on context.
• **Vocabulary Expansion**: Building active vocabulary systematically by recording and reviewing words decoded through context clues.
• **Reading Fluency Impact**: Preventing frequent stops for dictionary lookups, thereby maintaining the natural rhythm and flow of reading.
• **Self-Reliance**: Fostering academic independence in students by teaching them to solve vocabulary puzzles autonomously.
• **DAV Curriculum Standard**: Emphasizing word-attack skills as a core component of intermediate English language examinations.
• **Figurative Context**: Understanding when words are used metaphorically rather than literally based on the emotional or descriptive context.
• **Multiple Meanings**: Differentiating between various dictionary definitions of a word by analyzing which one fits the specific context.
• **Active Application**: Practicing context-clue identification exercises from the DAV English Practice Book chapter units.

• **Fact vs. Opinion**: A critical analytical distinction required for high-level reading comprehension and media literacy.
• **Definition of a Fact**: A statement that can be proven true or false through objective evidence, data, observation, or documentation.
• **Characteristics of Facts**: Universal, verifiable, unchanging regardless of personal feelings, and grounded in empirical reality.
• **Definition of an Opinion**: A statement expressing a personal belief, judgment, feeling, value, or attitude that cannot be proven objectively true.
• **Characteristics of Opinions**: Subjective, debatable, vary from person to person, and influenced by personal biases or perspectives.
• **Signal Words for Opinions**: Words and phrases such as *I think*, *I believe*, *should*, *best*, *worst*, *seem*, and *probably*.
• **Signal Words for Facts**: Objective markers such as *statistics show*, *records indicate*, *research proves*, and specific dates or measurements.
• **Blended Sentences**: Identifying complex sentences where a factual base is coupled with an authorial opinion or value judgment.
• **Authorial Bias**: Recognizing when an author's personal opinions subtly shape the presentation of facts in a non-fiction text.
• **Objectivity in Reading**: Approaching informative texts with a neutral mindset to separate verifiable truth from persuasive commentary.
• **Critical Questioning**: Asking "Can this be proven in a laboratory, historical document, or encyclopedia?" when evaluating statements.
• **Newspaper and Article Analysis**: Applying fact-versus-opinion filters to journalistic writings, editorials, and advertisements.
• **DAV Academic Value**: Cultivating objective thinkers who can discern truth from subjective claims in an information-heavy world.
• **Argumentative Texts**: Understanding how writers use a foundation of facts to support their overarching opinions or arguments.
• **Emotional Language**: Spotting emotionally charged adjectives that signal the presence of an opinion rather than a pure fact.
• **Comprehension Enhancement**: Preventing misunderstandings by accurately categorizing the nature of statements encountered in reading passages.

• **Drawing Inferences**: The advanced cognitive skill of "reading between the lines" to extract unstated meanings from a text.
• **Definition of Inference**: Reaching a logical conclusion based on available evidence, clues, and prior knowledge rather than explicit statements.
• **The Detective Analogy**: Viewing the reader as a detective who pieces together clues left by the author to solve a comprehension puzzle.
• **Formula for Inference**: Text Clues + Prior Knowledge / Background Experience = Logical Inference.
• **Explicit vs. Implicit**: Differentiating between what the author states directly (explicit) and what the author hints at indirectly (implicit).
• **Character Motivations**: Inferring *why* a character behaves in a certain way based on their actions, dialogue, and emotional reactions.
• **Setting and Mood**: Inferring the time, place, and emotional atmosphere of a story from sensory details and descriptive language.
• **Cause and Effect Deduction**: Figuring out unstated causes or predicting unstated outcomes based on the sequence of events.
• **Tone Recognition**: Inferring the author's attitude (humorous, serious, sarcastic, sympathetic) through word choice and style.
• **Avoiding Over-Inferences**: Ensuring that inferences are strictly supported by text evidence rather than wild, unsupported assumptions.
• **Reading Poetry and Fiction**: Recognizing that inference is the primary vehicle for understanding poems, short stories, and narratives.
• **Logical Consistency**: Checking if an inferred conclusion holds up against all other facts and details presented in the passage.
• **Inference in Informational Text**: Deducing the implications of scientific discoveries or historical events described in non-fiction readings.
• **Class 6 Progression**: Stepping beyond literal comprehension to master abstract, analytical reading tasks in DAV assessments.
• **Practice Drills**: Utilizing short scenario passages to train the brain in rapid, accurate deduction and logical reasoning.
• **Cognitive Maturity**: Enhancing emotional intelligence and situational awareness through the practice of literary inference.

• **Summarization Techniques**: The art and science of condensing a longer text into its essential points without losing its core meaning.
• **Definition of a Summary**: A brief, concise restatement of the most important ideas and details of a passage in the reader's own words.
• **Rule of Omission**: Leaving out minor details, repetitive information, interesting side-stories, and personal opinions when summarizing.
• **The 5 Ws and 1 H Framework**: Structuring a summary around Who, What, Where, When, Why, and How of the text.
• **Paraphrasing vs. Summarizing**: Differentiating between rewriting a specific passage in one's own words (paraphrasing) and condensing an entire text (summarizing).
• **Conciseness Principle**: Ensuring that the summary is significantly shorter than the original source material.
• **Objective Voice**: Maintaining neutrality and avoiding the injection of personal commentary or critique into the summary.
• **Sequential Integrity**: Retaining the logical order of events or arguments as presented in the original text.
• **Keyword Integration**: Utilizing the most critical DAV vocabulary words from the passage within the summary structure.
• **Paragraph Summaries**: Practicing the condensation of single paragraphs before moving on to full multi-paragraph passages.
• **Retelling vs. Summarizing**: Moving beyond a clumsy chronological retelling of every plot point to focus strictly on structural highlights.
• **DAV Exam Application**: Mastery of summary writing questions commonly found in English practice papers and comprehension tests.
• **Memory Retention**: Using summaries as a potent study aid for recalling large blocks of text during examination preparation.
• **Editing and Refining**: Reviewing a draft summary to trim unnecessary words and sharpen the core message.
• **Synthesis Skill**: Combining multiple supporting points into a single, cohesive concluding sentence.
• **Active Comprehension Check**: Acknowledging that if one can successfully summarize a text, one has truly understood it.

• **Sequential Ordering and Chronology**: Understanding the chronological arrangement of events and procedures in instructional and narrative texts.
• **Sequential Signpost Words**: Recognizing transitional markers such as *first*, *next*, *then*, *afterwards*, *meanwhile*, *finally*, and *subsequently*.
• **Narrative Sequence**: Following the plot arc of a story from exposition through rising action, climax, falling action, to resolution.
• **Procedural Sequence**: Following step-by-step instructions in recipes, manuals, science experiments, and craft guides.
• **Flashbacks and Time Shifts**: Recognizing when an author intentionally disrupts chronological order to reveal past events.
• **Timeline Construction**: Drawing chronological timelines as a visual tool to map historical or biographical reading passages.
• **Cause-and-Effect Sequencing**: Understanding that causes must chronologically precede their effects in logical text structures.
• **DAV Practice Exercises**: Reordering scrambled sentences into a logically coherent paragraph based on sequential clues.
• **Following Directions**: Applying sequential reading skills to accurately execute multi-step academic and practical instructions.
• **Predicting Next Steps**: Anticipating what action will logically occur next in a procedural or narrative text.
• **Flowchart Mapping**: Creating flowcharts to represent processes, cycles (like the water cycle), and historical timelines.
• **Transitions of Time**: Noting phrases like *three years later* or *the following morning* that signal time jumps in reading material.

• **Retelling Accuracy**: Ensuring that oral or written retellings maintain the exact chronological sequence of the original narrative.
