• **Chapter Overview**: Welcome to the DAV Class 7 Secondary Mathematics Chapter 3 study notes on "Rational Numbers as Decimals", designed to build a rigorous foundation in fractional and decimal real number representations.
• **Core Definition of Rational Numbers**: A rational number is formally defined as any number that can be expressed in the form $p/q$, where $p$ and $q$ are both integers, and the denominator $q$ is strictly non-zero ($q \neq 0$).
• **Role of Decimals**: Decimals serve as an alternative, highly standardized base-10 positional notation system to represent both whole numbers and fractions without requiring explicit numerator-denominator structures.
• **Link Between Fractions and Decimals**: Every fraction or rational number possesses a decimal equivalent, which is obtained by performing standard division of the numerator by the denominator.
• **Importance of Place Value**: The decimal number system relies strictly on powers of 10, where digits to the left of the decimal point represent positive powers of 10 and digits to the right represent negative powers of 10.
• **Tenths Place Value**: The first digit immediately to the right of the decimal point represents the "tenths" place, corresponding to fractional values of $1/10$ or $0.1$.
• **Hundredths Place Value**: The second digit to the right of the decimal point designates the "hundredths" place, which corresponds to fractional parts of $1/100$ or $0.01$.
• **Thousandths Place Value**: The third digit right of the decimal point indicates the "thousandths" place, representing fractional components equal to $1/1000$ or $0.001$.
• **Positional Expansion**: Any decimal number can be expanded algebraically using its place values, such as $45.67 = (4 \times 10) + (5 \times 1) + (6 \times 0.1) + (7 \times 0.01)$.
• **Terminating Decimals Definition**: A terminating decimal is a decimal number that contains a finite number of digits after the decimal point, meaning the division process eventually leaves a remainder of zero.
• **Non-Terminating Decimals**: A non-terminating decimal is one in which the digits after the decimal point continue infinitely without ever terminating or reaching a zero remainder.
• **Recurring Decimals**: Also known as repeating decimals, these are non-terminating decimals where a specific digit or a block of digits repeats endlessly in a regular pattern.
• **Vinculum Notation**: The recurring pattern in repeating decimals is denoted by placing a horizontal bar (called a vinculum) directly over the repeating digits, such as $0.\bar{3}$ or $2.1\bar{45}$.
• **Rational to Decimal Conversion Core Rule**: To convert any rational number $p/q$ into its decimal form, perform long division by dividing the numerator $p$ by the denominator $q$.
• **Division Mechanism**: During the conversion division, if the remainder becomes zero at any stage, the resulting decimal representation is strictly a terminating decimal.

• **Prime Factorization Property**: A rational number in its simplest form $p/q$ yields a terminating decimal if and only if the prime factorization of the denominator $q$ contains only powers of 2, powers of 5, or a combination of both.
• **Exclusion of Other Primes**: If the prime factorization of the simplified denominator $q$ contains any prime number other than 2 or 5 (such as 3, 7, 11, etc.), the resulting decimal expansion is guaranteed to be non-terminating and repeating.
• **Denominator Power of 10 Trick**: An alternative conversion method involves multiplying both the numerator and denominator by an appropriate integer so that the denominator becomes a power of 10 ($10, 100, 1000$, etc.).
• **Example of Power of 10 Method**: For the fraction $3/5$, multiplying both numerator and denominator by 2 yields $6/10$, which immediately converts to the terminating decimal $0.6$.
• **Conversion of Mixed Numbers**: To convert a mixed fraction into a decimal, either convert it first to an improper fraction and divide, or keep the whole number part intact and convert only the fractional part.
• **Handling Whole Numbers in Mixed Fractions**: For a mixed number like $7 \frac{3}{4}$, the whole number 7 remains to the left of the decimal point, and the fraction $3/4$ converts to $0.75$, resulting in $7.75$.
• **Sign Preservation**: When converting negative rational numbers, compute the decimal value of the absolute positive fraction first, and then simply prepend a negative sign to the final decimal output.
• **Example of Negative Conversion**: The negative rational number $-5/8$ is converted by evaluating $5 \div 8 = 0.625$ and applying the sign to get $-0.625$.
• **Zero Numerator Rule**: If the numerator of a rational number is zero (e.g., $0/13$), its decimal representation is simply $0.0$ or $0$, since zero divided by any non-zero integer is zero.
• **Unit Denominator Rule**: Any rational number with a denominator of 1 (e.g., $47/1$) has a decimal equivalent equal to its whole number numerator ($47.0$ or $47$).
• **Precision and Rounding**: In practical applications of rational decimals, numbers are often rounded to a specified number of decimal places using standard mathematical rounding rules.
• **Rounding Rule (Less than 5)**: If the digit in the place value immediately after the target rounding position is between 0 and 4, the target digit remains unchanged and all subsequent digits are dropped.
• **Rounding Rule (5 or Greater)**: If the digit in the place value immediately after the target rounding position is 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9, the target digit is incremented by 1 and subsequent digits are dropped.
• **Significance of Simplification**: Always reduce a rational number to its lowest terms (simplest form) before analyzing its denominator's prime factors to determine its decimal classification.
• **Trap of Unsimplified Fractions**: An unsimplified fraction like $3/15$ has a denominator with a prime factor of 3, but after simplification to $1/5$, the denominator is 5, confirming it is a terminating decimal.

• **Inverse Operation (Decimal to Rational)**: Converting decimals back into rational number forms ($p/q$) is the direct inverse process of converting fractions to decimals.
• **Conversion of Terminating Decimals**: To convert a terminating decimal to a rational number, write the decimal number without the decimal point as the numerator.
• **Denominator for Terminating Decimals**: The denominator for a converted terminating decimal is always 1 followed by as many zeros as there are digits to the right of the decimal point.
• **Example of Terminating Conversion**: The terminating decimal $0.45$ has two decimal places, so it converts to $45/100$, which simplifies by dividing numerator and denominator by 5 to $9/20$.
• **Decimal Places Count**: The number of zeros in the denominator of a converted terminating decimal precisely matches the count of decimal digits in the original number.
• **Simplification After Conversion**: Always simplify the resulting fraction to its irreducible lowest terms by dividing the numerator and denominator by their highest common factor (HCF).
• **Converting Pure Recurring Decimals**: Pure recurring decimals (where all digits after the decimal point repeat) require a specific algebraic manipulation method to convert into rational form.
• **Step 1 of Recurring Conversion**: Let a variable, say $x$, equal the pure recurring decimal, for instance, let $x = 0.\bar{6} = 0.6666...$ (Equation 1).
• **Step 2 of Recurring Conversion**: Count the number of repeating digits under the vinculum; if there is 1 repeating digit, multiply both sides of the equation by $10^1 = 10$.
• **Step 3 of Recurring Conversion**: Multiplying $x = 0.6666...$ by 10 yields $10x = 6.6666...$ (Equation 2).
• **Step 4 of Recurring Conversion**: Subtract Equation 1 ($x = 0.6666...$) from Equation 2 ($10x = 6.6666...$) to eliminate the infinite repeating decimal tails.
• **Step 5 of Recurring Conversion**: The subtraction yields $9x = 6$, which solves algebraically to $x = 6/9$, simplifying further to the rational number $2/3$.
• **Two Repeating Digits Rule**: If a decimal has two repeating digits under the bar, such as $0.\bar{45}$, multiply by $10^2 = 100$ to shift the decimal point two places.
• **General Multiplier for Recurring Decimals**: For a repeating block of length $n$ digits, multiply the decimal expression by $10^n$ before performing the subtraction step.
• **Mixed Recurring Decimals**: Mixed recurring decimals contain non-repeating digits immediately following the decimal point, followed by repeating digits (e.g., $0.2\bar{3}$).
• **Handling Mixed Recurring Decimals**: Convert mixed recurring decimals by first multiplying to shift the non-repeating part across the decimal point, then applying standard algebraic subtraction.

• **Comparison of Decimals**: Comparing two decimals involves evaluating their whole number parts first, and if they are equal, comparing the digits at corresponding place values from left to right.
• **Step-by-Step Decimal Comparison**: To compare $4.72$ and $4.75$, observe that whole parts ($4 = 4$) and tenths ($7 = 7$) are equal, but in the hundredths place, $5 > 2$, making $4.75 > 4.72$.
• **Padding with Zeros**: Decimals can be easily compared by appending trailing zeros to ensure both numbers have an equal number of decimal places without altering their mathematical value.
• **Ordering Decimals**: Ascending order arranges decimals from smallest to largest, while descending order arranges them from largest to smallest using systematic place-value comparison.
• **Addition of Decimals**: To add two or more decimal numbers, align their decimal points vertically in a column and add the digits according to standard column addition rules.
• **Zero Padding for Addition**: If decimals have differing numbers of decimal places during addition, fill the empty spaces with trailing zeros so that all numbers align uniformly in column layout.
• **Carrying Over in Addition**: Standard carry-over rules apply in decimal addition; when a column sum is 10 or greater, carry the excess value to the immediate next place value to the left.
• **Placement of Sum Decimal Point**: In the final sum, align the decimal point in the exact same vertical column position as the addends above it.
• **Subtraction of Decimals**: Subtracting decimals requires aligning the decimal points vertically, padding with zeros if necessary, and borrowing from neighboring columns when the top digit is smaller.
• **Multiplication of Decimals**: To multiply two decimals, temporarily ignore the decimal points and multiply the numbers as if they were standard whole numbers.
• **Decimal Places Count in Multiplication**: Count the total number of decimal places combined in both multiplier and multiplicand factors.
• **Placing the Decimal in Product**: In the final product of multiplication, start from the rightmost digit and move the decimal point leftward by a number of spaces equal to the combined total decimal places.
• **Example of Multiplication**: Multiplying $0.4$ (1 decimal place) by $0.2$ (1 decimal place) gives $4 \times 2 = 8$, with $1+1=2$ decimal places, resulting in $0.08$.
• **Multiplication by Powers of 10**: Multiplying any decimal number by $10, 100, 1000$ shifts the decimal point to the right by 1, 2, or 3 places respectively.
• **Division of Decimals**: Dividing a decimal by another decimal involves converting the divisor into a whole number by multiplying both dividend and divisor by an appropriate power of 10.
----PAGE-BREAK---
• **Divisor Conversion Principle**: Multiplying both dividend and divisor by the same power of 10 preserves the exact ratio and value of the division problem while eliminating the decimal point in the divisor.
• **Division Example**: To evaluate $7.5 \div 1.5$, multiply both by 10 to transform the problem into $75 \div 15$, which equals the whole number $5$.
• **Division by Powers of 10**: Dividing any decimal number by $10, 100, 1000$ shifts the decimal point to the left by 1, 2, or 3 places respectively.
• **Properties of Rational Decimals**: The set of rational numbers in decimal form satisfies the closure property under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.
• **Closure Under Division**: The set of non-zero rational numbers is closed under division, meaning dividing one rational decimal by another non-zero rational decimal always yields another rational number.
• **Commutative Property**: Addition and multiplication of decimals are commutative, meaning $a + b = b + a$ and $a \times b = b \times a$ hold true for all decimals.
• **Associative Property**: Addition and multiplication of decimals are associative, allowing regrouping of terms without altering the final result: $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$.
• **Distributive Property**: Multiplication distributes over addition for decimals, expressed algebraically as $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$.
• **Additive Identity**: Zero ($0$ or $0.0$) is the additive identity for decimals, as adding zero to any decimal leaves the number completely unchanged.
• **Multiplicative Identity**: One ($1$ or $1.0$) is the multiplicative identity for decimals, as multiplying any decimal by 1 results in the exact same decimal number.
• **Additive Inverse**: The additive inverse of any decimal number $x$ is $-x$, such that their sum equals the additive identity ($x + (-x) = 0$).
• **Multiplicative Inverse (Reciprocal)**: The multiplicative inverse of a non-zero decimal $x$ is $1/x$, such that their product equals the multiplicative identity ($x \times (1/x) = 1$).
• **Density Property**: Rational numbers, including their decimal representations, possess the density property, meaning between any two distinct rational decimals, there exist infinitely many other rational numbers.
• **Finding Numbers Between Decimals**: To find a rational number between two decimals $a$ and $b$, one can calculate their arithmetic mean: $(a + b) / 2$.
• **Metric System and Decimals**: Decimals are the foundation of metric measurements, allowing seamless conversion between units of length, mass, and capacity (e.g., centimeters to meters).

• **Length Conversions**: Converting smaller metric units of length to larger units utilizes decimals, such as converting centimeters to meters by dividing by 100 ($125 \text{ cm} = 1.25 \text{ m}$).
• **Mass Conversions**: Mass measurements rely heavily on decimals, where grams are converted to kilograms by dividing by 1000 ($450 \text{ g} = 0.450 \text{ kg}$).
• **Capacity Conversions**: Volume and capacity units use decimal notation for liters and milliliters, such as converting milliliters to liters by dividing by 1000 ($75 \text{ mL} = 0.075 \text{ L}$).
• **Currency and Money**: Financial calculations are naturally expressed in two-decimal-place numbers representing dollars, rupees, or other base currencies and their fractional subunit cents or paise.
• **Word Problems Application**: Real-world word problems involving rational decimals require translating verbal statements into mathematical expressions involving decimal operations.
• **Rate and Cost Problems**: Calculating total cost involves multiplying the quantity of items (which may be decimals, like weight in kilograms) by the unit price per decimal unit.
• **Speed, Distance, and Time**: Speed calculations frequently yield terminating or recurring decimals when dividing distance by time ($Speed = Distance / Time$).
• **Average Calculations**: Finding the average of a set of decimal numbers involves summing all the values and dividing the total sum by the count of items in the set.
• **Percentage to Decimal Conversion**: Percentages are converted to decimals by removing the percent sign and dividing the numerical value by 100 (shifting the decimal point two places to the left).
• **Example of Percentage Conversion**: A percentage value of $45\%$ converts to a decimal by evaluating $45 \div 100 = 0.45$.
• **Decimal to Percentage Conversion**: Conversely, any decimal number is converted to a percentage by multiplying the decimal value by $100$ and appending the percent ($\%$) sign.
• **Example of Decimal to Percentage**: The decimal $0.82$ converts to a percentage by calculating $0.82 \times 100 = 82\%$.
• **Ratio to Decimal Conversion**: Ratios expressed in the form $a:b$ are first written as fractions $a/b$ and then converted to decimals via standard division.
• **Proportions with Decimals**: Solving proportions involving decimals relies on cross-multiplication, where the product of extremes equals the product of means.
• **Estimation of Decimals**: Estimation techniques in decimals involve rounding numbers to the nearest whole number or tenth before performing rapid mental arithmetic.

• **Significance of Leading Zeros**: Leading zeros between the decimal point and the first non-zero digit determine the place value magnitude (e.g., $0.05$ is ten times larger than $0.005$).
• **Number Line Representation**: Rational numbers in decimal form can be precisely located and plotted on a standard horizontal number line.
• **Locating Tenths on Number Line**: To plot a decimal like $3.4$ on a number line, divide the interval between integer 3 and integer 4 into 10 equal subdivisions representing tenths.
• **Magnification Technique**: Plotting numbers with multiple decimal places (like $3.42$) utilizes successive magnification, zooming in on the interval between $3.4$ and $3.5$ and subdividing it into hundredths.
• **Density on Number Line**: The number line is densely packed with rational decimals, meaning no matter how much one zooms in, there are always uncounted rational points available.
• **Standard Form of Rational Decimals**: A rational number in decimal form is in standard form when it is fully simplified and written without redundant zeros or formatting errors.
• **Comparison with Irrational Numbers**: Unlike rational decimals (which either terminate or recur), irrational numbers have decimal expansions that are infinitely non-terminating and non-recurring (e.g., $\pi$ or $\sqrt{2}$).
• **Venn Diagram Hierarchy**: In the real number system hierarchy, integers are a subset of rational numbers, and all rational numbers are characterized by terminating or recurring decimal expansions.

• **Advanced Concept: Pure vs. Mixed Recurring Deep Dive**: Pure recurring decimals have repeating blocks starting immediately after the decimal point, whereas mixed recurring decimals feature intervening non-repeating digits.
• **General Formula for Pure Recurring**: Any pure recurring decimal of the form $0.\bar{a}$ where $a$ represents an $n$-digit integer is identically equal to the fraction $a / (10^n - 1)$.
• **Application of Pure Formula**: Using the general formula, $0.\bar{7} = 7 / (10^1 - 1) = 7/9$, and $0.\bar{35} = 35 / (10^2 - 1) = 35/99$.
• **General Formula for Mixed Recurring**: A mixed recurring decimal of the form $0.a\bar{b}$ converts directly to the rational number given by $(ab - a) / (900...00 \text{ with zeros for repeating and nines for non-repeating})$ or via systematic algebra.
• **Algebraic Method for Mixed Example**: For $x = 0.2\bar{3} = 0.2333...$, multiply by 10 to shift the non-repeating part: $10x = 2.3333...$ (Eq 1).
• **Second Shift for Mixed Example**: Multiply Equation 1 by 10 again to align the repeating tails: $100x = 23.3333...$ (Eq 2).
• **Subtraction for Mixed Example**: Subtracting Equation 1 from Equation 2 gives $90x = 21$, which solves to $x = 21/90$, simplifying to $7/30$.
• **Consistency Check**: Verifying $7 \div 30$ via long division confirms it produces the exact mixed recurring decimal $0.2333...$ or $0.2\bar{3}$.
• **Non-Terminating Non-Recurring Distinction**: It is critical to continually contrast recurring decimals (which are rational numbers) with non-terminating non-recurring decimals (which are irrational and outside the scope of rational numbers).
• **Terminating Decimal Length Theorem**: The maximum number of digits in the non-repeating decimal part of a fraction $p/q$ in lowest terms is determined by the maximum exponent of 2 or 5 in the prime factorization of $q$.
• **Period Length in Recurring Decimals**: When a fraction $p/q$ produces a recurring decimal, the length of the repeating period is at most $q - 1$ digits long.
