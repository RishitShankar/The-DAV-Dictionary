• **Chapter Overview**: **Nutrition in Living Organisms—Plants** forms the foundational first chapter of Class 7 science under the DAV curriculum, introducing students to how plants acquire and utilize food.
• **Definition of Nutrition**: **Nutrition** is defined as the comprehensive process by which living organisms take in food and utilize it to obtain energy, promote body growth, repair worn-out tissues, and maintain life processes.
• **Necessity of Food**: All living organisms require food to survive because food acts as the primary fuel that drives cellular activities, sustains metabolic functions, and supports overall survival.
• **Energy Source for Life**: The Sun serves as the ultimate source of energy for all living organisms on Earth, as solar energy is trapped and converted into chemical energy by green plants.
• **Modes of Nutrition**: Nutrition in living organisms is broadly classified into two main modes: **Autotrophic Nutrition** and **Heterotrophic Nutrition**.
• **Autotrophic Nutrition Terminology**: The word 'autotrophic' is derived from Greek words where 'autos' means self and 'trophe' means nourishment, signifying self-feeding.
• **Autotrophs Defined**: **Autotrophs** are organisms, primarily green plants, that manufacture their own organic food from simple inorganic raw materials present in their external environment.
• **Heterotrophic Nutrition Terminology**: The term 'heterotrophic' stems from 'heteros' meaning other and 'trophe' meaning nourishment, indicating dependence on others for food.
• **Heterotrophs Defined**: **Heterotrophs** are organisms that cannot synthesize their own food and must directly or indirectly rely on plants or other organisms for their nutritional needs.
• **Photosynthesis Introduction**: **Photosynthesis** is the intricate physicochemical process by which green plants utilize solar energy to synthesize organic food substances from carbon dioxide and water.
• **Site of Photosynthesis**: The cellular sites of photosynthesis are specialized green organelles known as **chloroplasts**, which are abundantly present inside the leaf cells.
• **Chlorophyll Pigment**: Chlorophyll is the vital, light-absorbing green pigment embedded within chloroplasts that traps specific wavelengths of sunlight required for driving the photosynthetic reaction.
• **Leaf as the Food Factory**: Leaves are structurally adapted to act as the primary food factories of the plant due to their broad surface area and internal cellular specialization.
• **Raw Materials Requirement**: The essential raw materials required by green plants to execute photosynthesis are carbon dioxide gas and liquid water.
• **Water Acquisition Mechanism**: Water is absorbed from the soil by the extensive root system and transported upward to the leaves via specialized tubular conducting tissues called xylem vessels.
• **Carbon Dioxide Acquisition**: Carbon dioxide gas from the surrounding atmosphere enters the internal tissues of the leaf primarily through microscopic pores called stomata.

• **Stomata Structure**: **Stomata** are microscopic aperture-like pores predominantly located on the underside of leaves, facilitating gaseous exchange between the plant interior and the atmosphere.
• **Guard Cells Function**: Each stoma is flanked by a pair of specialized bean-shaped cells known as **guard cells**, which regulate the opening and closing of the stomatal pore.
• **Mechanism of Stomatal Opening**: Guard cells swell up by absorbing water to open the stomatal pore during daylight, and shrink to close the pore when water is scarce.
• **Role of Sunlight**: Sunlight provides the photon energy required to break chemical bonds and drive the conversion of carbon dioxide and water into glucose.
• **Photosynthetic Chemical Equation**: The overall summarized chemical equation for photosynthesis involves carbon dioxide reacting with water in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll to yield glucose and oxygen gas.
• **Primary Product of Photosynthesis**: **Glucose** is the initial simple carbohydrate synthesized during photosynthesis, serving as the immediate source of chemical energy for the plant.
• **Storage Form of Food**: Plants convert excess glucose into complex polymeric carbohydrates called **starch**, which is stored in various plant organs as a reserve food supply.
• **Byproduct Release**: Oxygen gas is released as a vital byproduct of photosynthesis into the atmosphere, sustaining aerobic respiration in humans, animals, and plants themselves.
• **Interdependence of Life**: Photosynthesis bridges the biotic and abiotic worlds, maintaining the delicate balance of carbon dioxide and oxygen in the Earth's atmosphere.
• **Cellular Respiration Link**: The glucose produced during photosynthesis is broken down during respiration inside plant and animal cells to release usable energy in the form of ATP.
• **Synthesis of Non-Carbohydrates**: Besides carbohydrates, plants synthesize complex biomolecules such as proteins and fats using glucose derivatives combined with minerals absorbed from the soil.
• **Role of Nitrogen in Plants**: Nitrogen is an essential mineral nutrient required by plants for synthesizing proteins, nucleic acids, and other vital cellular components.
• **Atmospheric Nitrogen Limitation**: Although the atmosphere contains nearly 78 percent nitrogen gas, plants cannot absorb atmospheric nitrogen directly in its gaseous form.
• **Soil Nitrogen Conversion**: Plants absorb nitrogen primarily in the form of soluble inorganic nitrates and nitrites dissolved in soil water, or through microbial fixation.
• **Rhizobium Bacteria Symbiosis**: **Rhizobium** bacteria residing in the root nodules of leguminous plants fix atmospheric nitrogen gas and convert it into usable nitrogenous compounds for the plant.
• **Fertilizer Enrichment**: Farmers frequently enrich agricultural soils with nitrogen-, phosphorus-, and potassium-rich fertilizers to compensate for nutrient depletion caused by crop harvesting.

• **Beyond Autotrophy**: While the vast majority of plants are autotrophic, certain unique plant species exhibit alternative modes of nutrition to survive challenging environmental conditions.
• **Heterotrophic Plants Classification**: Heterotrophic plants are systematically categorized into several distinct nutritional strategies, namely parasitic, insectivorous, saprophytic, and symbiotic plants.
• **Parasitic Plants Definition**: **Parasitic plants** are those non-green or partially green organisms that live on or inside other living plants, known as hosts, to derive their nourishment.
• **Host Dependence Mechanism**: Parasites extract pre-formed water, minerals, and organic nutrients directly from the vascular tissues of their living host plants.
• **Total Parasites**: **Total parasites** are completely devoid of chlorophyll and depend entirely on their host for all nutritional requirements, rendering them incapable of independent survival.
• **Cuscuta (Dodder) Example**: **Cuscuta** (commonly known as Amarbel or dodder) is a classic parasitic plant characterized by its yellowish, tubular, twining stems that climb over host plants.
• **Haustoria Structure**: Cuscuta develops specialized sucking roots called **haustoria** that penetrate deep into the host's vascular tissues to siphon off nutrients and water.
• **Partial Parasites**: **Partial parasites** possess green leaves and perform photosynthesis to manufacture some of their food, but rely on the host for water and mineral nutrients.
• **Mistletoe Example**: Mistletoe is a well-known partial parasite that attaches itself to the branches of trees to draw water and dissolved minerals while producing its own carbohydrates.
• **Insectivorous Plants Overview**: **Insectivorous plants** are green, autotrophic plants that grow in nitrogen-deficient soils and supplement their diet by trapping and digesting insects.
• **Nutritional Adaptation**: The carnivorous habit in insectivorous plants is an evolutionary adaptation specifically evolved to fulfill their vital nitrogen requirements.
• **Pitcher Plant Structure**: The **pitcher plant** modifies its leaf lamina into a hollow, pitcher-shaped vessel filled with downward-pointing hairs and digestive fluids.
• **Trapping Mechanism in Pitcher Plants**: When an unsuspecting insect lands on the slippery rim of the pitcher, it slips inside, gets trapped by downward-pointing hairs, and drowns in the digestive liquid.
• **Digestion in Pitcher Plants**: Specialized digestive enzymes secreted inside the pitcher break down the soft tissues of the trapped insect, releasing soluble nitrogenous compounds that the plant absorbs.
• **Venus Flytrap Example**: The **Venus flytrap** features specialized hinge-like leaves with sensitive trigger hairs that snap shut rapidly when an insect brushes against them, trapping the prey.
• **Sundew (Drosera) Example**: The **sundew** plant captures insects using sticky, glistening glandular tentacles that secrete a viscous, dew-like trapping fluid loaded with digestive enzymes.

• **Saprophytic Plants Definition**: **Saprophytic plants** (or saprotrophs) are non-green organisms that obtain their nutrition by feeding on dead, decaying, and rotting organic matter.
• **Extracellular Digestion**: Saprotrophs secrete digestive juices directly onto dead organic matter, breaking down complex organic molecules externally into simpler soluble forms before absorbing them.
• **Fungi as Saprotrophs**: Common examples of saprophytic organisms include various species of fungi, such as bread molds, mushrooms, and yeasts, which play critical ecological roles.
• **Indian Pipe (Monotropa) Example**: **Monotropa** (Indian pipe or corpse plant) is a rare, white, non-green saprophytic flowering plant that derives nutrients from decaying forest humus with the help of fungi.
• **Coral Root Orchid Example**: Coral root orchids are other fascinating examples of flowering saprophytes that lack chlorophyll and rely on decomposed organic matter in forest soil.
• **Ecological Role of Saprotrophs**: Saprophytic organisms act as nature's primary recyclers by decomposing dead plants and animals, thereby returning essential nutrients back to the soil.
• **Symbiotic Relationship Definition**: **Symbiosis** is a close biological association between two different organisms where both partners derive mutual benefits, enhancing their chances of survival.
• **Lichens as Symbiotic Associations**: A **lichen** represents a classic, highly integrated symbiotic association between an autotrophic alga and a heterotrophic fungus.
• **Algal Partner Role in Lichens**: The algal partner in a lichen, known as phycobiont, contains chlorophyll and manufactures food via photosynthesis for both itself and its fungal partner.
• **Fungal Partner Role in Lichens**: The fungal partner, known as mycobiont, provides physical shelter, absorbs water, and anchors the lichen firmly to rocks or tree bark.
• **Lichens as Pollution Indicators**: Lichens are extremely sensitive to sulfur dioxide pollution and serve as reliable biological indicators of air quality in urban and industrial environments.
• **Mycorrhiza Definition**: **Mycorrhiza** is another prominent symbiotic association formed between certain soil fungi and the roots of higher vascular trees.
• **Nutrient Exchange in Mycorrhiza**: The fungal hyphae vastly increase the root surface area for absorbing water and essential minerals like phosphorus, while receiving manufactured carbohydrates from the tree.
• **Root Nodules Symbiosis**: The association between leguminous plants and Rhizobium bacteria is also symbiotic, where bacteria obtain shelter and plant sugars in exchange for fixed nitrogen.
• **Nutrient Replenishment in Soil**: Symbiotic relationships and natural decomposition cycles ensure that soil nutrients are continually replenished, maintaining long-term agricultural fertility.

• **Detailed Leaf Anatomy**: Leaves possess a highly specialized internal architecture optimized for capturing maximum sunlight and facilitating efficient gaseous exchange.
• **Cuticle Layer**: The upper and lower surfaces of a leaf are covered by a waxy, waterproof layer called the **cuticle**, which minimizes excessive loss of water via transpiration.
• **Epidermis Layers**: The **upper epidermis** and **lower epidermis** form protective, tightly packed cellular layers enclosing the inner photosynthetic tissues of the leaf.
• **Mesophyll Tissue**: The central region of the leaf, known as **mesophyll**, is bifurcated into palisade parenchyma and spongy parenchyma cells packed with chloroplasts.
• **Palisade Cells**: Palisade cells are elongated, columnar cells located beneath the upper epidermis, densely packed with chloroplasts to carry out the bulk of photosynthesis.
• **Spongy Cells**: Spongy cells are loosely arranged irregular cells with prominent intercellular air spaces that facilitate rapid diffusion of carbon dioxide and oxygen within the leaf.
• **Vascular Bundles in Leaves**: Leaf veins represent vascular bundles containing **xylem** tubes for water transport and **phloem** tubes for distributing synthesized sugars.
• **Transpiration Process**: **Transpiration** is the biological process by which plants lose water vapor into the atmosphere primarily through open stomatal pores on their leaves.
• **Cooling Effect of Transpiration**: Transpiration creates a continuous upward suction pull, known as transpiration pull, which draws water and minerals from roots to the topmost leaves.
• **Factors Affecting Photosynthesis**: The rate of photosynthesis is influenced by several environmental factors, including light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, temperature, and water availability.
• **Light Intensity Impact**: As light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases proportionally up to a certain saturation point where other factors become limiting.
• **Temperature Dependence**: Photosynthesis relies on enzymatic reactions that operate within an optimal temperature range, typically between 20°C and 35°C, dropping sharply outside these bounds.
• **Carbon Dioxide Concentration**: Elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations can enhance photosynthetic rates in certain plant species until enzyme active sites become saturated.
• **Water Stress Effects**: Severe water scarcity causes stomata to close tightly, drastically cutting off carbon dioxide supply and halting the photosynthetic process.
• **Importance of Chlorophyll Concentration**: Leaves with chlorosis or reduced chlorophyll content exhibit diminished photosynthetic capacity, affecting overall plant growth and biomass accumulation.
• **Solar Energy Conversion Efficiency**: Green plants convert only a small percentage of incident solar energy into chemical energy, yet this fraction sustains virtually all life on Earth.

• **Evolutionary Adaptations in Nutrition**: Over geological time scales, plants have evolved remarkable morphological and physiological adaptations to secure nutrition in extreme habitats.
• **Xerophytic Adaptations**: Desert plants, or **xerophytes**, possess modified leaves like spines, thick fleshy stems, and sunken stomata to conserve water while performing specialized photosynthesis.
• **Crassulacean Acid Metabolism**: Certain arid-land plants utilize a specialized pathway called **CAM photosynthesis** to fix carbon dioxide at night, minimizing daytime water loss through open stomata.
• **Epiphytic Plants**: **Epiphytes** are non-parasitic plants that grow harmlessly upon the branches of taller trees solely to obtain better access to sunlight in dense tropical rainforests.
• **Velamen Roots**: Epiphytic orchids possess specialized spongy aerial roots covered by a multi-layered tissue called **velamen**, which rapidly absorbs moisture directly from humid air and rain.
• **Aquatic Plant Adaptations**: Submerged and floating aquatic plants exhibit unique structural features, such as large internal air chambers called **aerenchyma**, to maintain buoyancy and gas exchange.
• **Rootless Aquatic Plants**: Certain free-floating aquatic plants, like *Utricularia* (bladderwort), possess specialized submersed traps to capture tiny aquatic animals, supplementing mineral nutrition.
• **Hemi-parasites Details**: Hemi-parasites like sandal trees form haustoria on the roots of neighboring plants while retaining functional green leaves to synthesize organic carbon.
• **Myco-heterotrophy**: Certain non-green plants, known as **myco-heterotrophs**, completely exploit mycorrhizal fungi connected to neighboring green trees, indirectly parasitizing forest canopy plants.
• **Bacterial Nitrogen Fixation Chemistry**: Biological nitrogen fixation is catalyzed by the specialized enzyme **nitrogenase** found in Rhizobium and free-living cyanobacteria.
• **Ammonia Production**: Nitrogenase breaks the exceptionally strong triple covalent bond of atmospheric nitrogen gas, converting it into ammonia, which is rapidly assimilated into amino acids.
• **Leghaemoglobin Pigment**: Root nodules of leguminous plants contain a pink-red oxygen-scavenging pigment called **leghaemoglobin** to protect the sensitive nitrogenase enzyme from oxygen poisoning.
• **Nitrifying Bacteria Role**: Soil bacteria such as *Nitrosomonas* convert ammonia into nitrites, and *Nitrobacter* subsequently converts nitrites into nitrates for optimal plant absorption.
• **Denitrifying Bacteria**: Certain denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soils convert soil nitrates back into atmospheric nitrogen gas, completing the global biogeochemical nitrogen cycle.
• **Mineral Nutrient Classification**: Plant mineral nutrients are classified into macronutrients, required in large quantities, and micronutrients, required in trace amounts for enzymatic functions.
• **Macronutrients Examples**: Vital macronutrients essential for plant metabolism include nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and sulfur.

• **Micronutrients Examples**: Crucial micronutrients required in trace quantities include iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, and chlorine.
• **Chlorosis Symptom**: **Chlorosis** is a pathological condition characterized by the yellowing of plant leaves due to a deficiency in essential mineral nutrients like magnesium, iron, or nitrogen.
• **Necrosis Symptom**: **Necrosis** refers to the localized death of plant tissue, often resulting from acute mineral deficiencies, pathogen attacks, or environmental stress.
• **Stunted Growth Deficiency**: Deficiencies in phosphorus or nitrogen frequently manifest as stunted vegetative growth, delayed flowering, and poor crop yields in agricultural systems.
• **Agricultural Soil Management**: Continuous farming depletes natural soil nutrients, necessitating systematic crop rotation, green manuring, and organic composting to sustain fertility.
• **Crop Rotation Practice**: **Crop rotation** involves alternating cereal crops with leguminous crops in successive seasons to naturally restore soil nitrogen content via symbiotic fixation.
• **Organic Manure Benefits**: Application of organic manure improves soil texture, enhances water retention capacity, and provides a balanced array of micronutrients for plant growth.
• **Inorganic Fertilizers Impact**: While chemical fertilizers provide rapid nutrient boosts, excessive or unscientific application can lead to soil acidification, water pollution, and disruption of microbial life.
• **Biological Pest Control**: Integrated pest management utilizes natural predators and bio-pesticides to protect crops without damaging beneficial soil microorganisms and symbiotic fungi.
• **Historical Experiments on Photosynthesis**: Seminal experiments by historical scientists like Jan Baptista van Helmont, Joseph Priestley, and Jan Ingenhousz unraveled the mechanisms of photosynthesis.
• **Van Helmont's Willow Tree Experiment**: Van Helmont demonstrated that the mass of a growing tree comes primarily from water rather than soil, laying the groundwork for plant nutrition studies.
• **Priestley's Bell Jar Experiment**: Joseph Priestley proved in 1770 that plants restore oxygen to the air enclosed within a sealed bell jar, reversing the foul effects of animal respiration.
• **Ingenhousz's Sunlight Discovery**: Jan Ingenhousz established that sunlight is absolutely essential for the release of oxygen by green plants, noting that only green parts perform this function.
• **Julius von Sachs' Discovery**: Julius von Sachs proved in 1854 that glucose produced during photosynthesis is subsequently polymerized and stored as starch granules inside chloroplasts.
• **Engelmann's Action Spectrum**: T.W. Engelmann illuminated a filamentous green alga with a spectrum of light, demonstrating that oxygen-seeking bacteria congregate in regions receiving red and blue light.
• **Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors**: F.H. Blackman formulated the law of limiting factors, stating that the rate of a physiological process is limited by the pace of the slowest factor.

• **Cellular Ultrastructure of Chloroplasts**: A detailed examination of chloroplast ultrastructure reveals stacked membrane systems known as **thylakoids** grouped into structures called **grana**.
• **Stroma Matrix**: The fluid-filled interior matrix of the chloroplast, known as the **stroma**, contains enzymes, ribosomes, and chloroplast DNA necessary for carbon fixation reactions.
• **Light-Dependent Reactions**: Photosynthesis occurs in two distinct stages: the **light-dependent reactions** (occurring in grana) and the **light-independent reactions** (occurring in stroma).
• **Photolysis of Water**: During light-dependent reactions, solar energy absorbed by chlorophyll drives the splitting of water molecules, a process known as **photolysis**, releasing oxygen gas.
• **ATP and NADPH Generation**: Light reactions convert solar energy into chemical energy currency in the form of **ATP** and reducing power in the form of **NADPH**.
• **Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)**: The **Calvin cycle** utilizes the ATP and NADPH generated in light reactions to fix atmospheric carbon dioxide into glucose without requiring direct sunlight.
• **Carbon Fixation Enzyme**: The crucial enzyme facilitating carbon dioxide fixation in the Calvin cycle is **RuBisCO** (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase), the most abundant protein on Earth.
• **Photorespiration Phenomenon**: Under high temperatures and dry conditions, RuBisCO can bind oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, leading to a wasteful metabolic pathway known as **photorespiration**.
• **C4 and CAM Adaptations**: Specialized plants in tropical environments have evolved C4 and CAM pathways to concentrate carbon dioxide and circumvent the inefficiencies of photorespiration.
• **Radioactive Isotope Tracers**: The use of radioactive carbon isotopes like Carbon-14 by Melvin Calvin enabled scientists to map the precise biochemical pathway of carbon fixation.
• **Plant Pigment Diversity**: Beyond chlorophyll $a$ and $b$, plants contain accessory pigments like **carotenoids** and **xanthophylls** that absorb different light wavelengths and protect chloroplasts from photo-oxidation.
• **Anthocyanin Pigments**: Water-soluble red, purple, or blue pigments called **anthocyanins** found in vacuoles protect leaves from intense ultraviolet radiation and herbivore grazing.
• **Herbivory Defense Mechanisms**: Many plants synthesize toxic secondary metabolites, such as alkaloids, tannins, and terpenoids, to deter herbivores and insect pests from consuming their tissues.
• **Phytohormones Role in Growth**: Plant growth and nutrient allocation are regulated by chemical messengers called **phytohormones**, including auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid, and ethylene.
• **Nutrient Translocation**: Synthesized organic food is actively transported from source tissues (mature leaves) to sink tissues (roots, fruits, and growing shoots) via the **phloem**.
• **Pressure Flow Hypothesis**: The mechanism of phloem transport is explained by the **pressure flow hypothesis**, where high osmotic pressure at the source forces sugar-rich sap through sieve tubes.

• **Global Significance of Photosynthesis**: Photosynthesis is arguably the most critical biochemical pathway on Earth, responsible for sustaining nearly all planetary biomass and atmospheric oxygen.
• **Primary Productivity**: Gross primary productivity represents the total rate of organic matter synthesis by photosynthesis, forming the foundational base of all global trophic food webs.
• **Fossil Fuels Connection**: Coal, petroleum, and natural gas represent stored solar energy captured by ancient prehistoric plants and animals through millions of years of geological burial.
• **Carbon Sequestration by Plants**: Forests act as massive biological carbon sinks, mitigating global climate change by absorbing colossal amounts of carbon dioxide from the industrial atmosphere.
• **Deforestation Consequences**: Rapid deforestation disrupts the global carbon cycle, diminishes atmospheric oxygen renewal, accelerates soil erosion, and threatens biodiversity worldwide.
• **Reforestation Initiatives**: Large-scale tree-planting and afforestation campaigns are essential strategies employed globally to restore degraded ecosystems and enhance natural carbon sequestration.
• **Urban Forestry Impact**: Urban parks and roadside green belts improve local microclimates, reduce ambient temperatures, filter air pollutants, and enhance community well-being.
• **Agricultural Biotechnology Innovations**: Genetic engineering aims to enhance photosynthetic efficiency in staple crops like rice and wheat to boost food security for a growing global population.
• **Artificial Photosynthesis Research**: Scientists are actively developing artificial photosynthetic systems and solar-to-fuel technologies to mimic natural plant processes for clean, renewable energy generation.
• **Phytoremediation Technology**: Certain specialized plants, known as **hyperaccumulators**, are utilized in phytoremediation to clean up heavy metal-contaminated soils and industrial wastewater.
• **Biofuels Production**: Crops such as sugarcane, corn, and oil palms are cultivated to produce renewable biofuels, offering alternative energy sources to fossil-derived fuels.
• **Plant Tissue Culture Techniques**: Plant biotechnology utilizes tissue culture to rapidly propagate disease-free elite crop varieties and endangered medicinal plant species under sterile conditions.
• **Nutritional Adaptations in Extreme Environments**: High-altitude alpine plants synthesize protective flavonoid compounds and develop dwarf habits to survive intense ultraviolet radiation and freezing temperatures.
• **Halophytic Adaptations**: Halophytes growing in saline coastal marshes and mangrove swamps possess specialized salt glands to excrete excess salt and pneumatophores for root aeration.
• **Epiparasitism Phenomenon**: Some unusual parasitic plants, such as *Conopholis* (squawroot), parasitize the fungal mycelium attached to tree roots rather than attacking the tree directly.
• **Summary of Plant Nutrition**: Understanding nutrition in plants reinforces the fundamental ecological truth that all living organisms are interconnected through complex nutritional networks anchored by autotrophs.
