• **Introduction to Three-Digit Numbers**: Numbers beyond 99 are known as three-digit numbers, extending from 100 to 999.
• **The Concept of Hundreds**: When we add 1 to 99, we get 100, which is the smallest three-digit number.
• **Place Value Structure**: Every three-digit number is composed of three distinct positions: Hundreds, Tens, and Ones.
• **The Hundreds Place**: The third position from the right represents groups of 100, denoted by the letter 'H'.
• **The Tens Place**: The middle position represents groups of 10, denoted by the letter 'T'.
• **The Ones Place**: The rightmost position represents individual units, denoted by the letter 'O'.
• **Base-10 System**: Our number system is based on groups of ten, where 10 ones make 1 ten, and 10 tens make 1 hundred.
• **Representation of 100**: 100 is written as 1 in the Hundreds place, 0 in the Tens place, and 0 in the Ones place.
• **Reading Numbers**: A three-digit number is read by stating the hundreds value first, followed by the tens and ones combined.
• **Example 101**: This is read as "One hundred and one," consisting of 1 hundred, 0 tens, and 1 one.
• **Example 110**: This is read as "One hundred and ten," consisting of 1 hundred, 1 ten, and 0 ones.
• **Example 111**: This is read as "One hundred and eleven," consisting of 1 hundred, 1 ten, and 1 one.
• **Standard Form**: Writing a number using digits (e.g., 245) is known as the standard form or numeral form.
• **Word Form**: Writing a number using English words (e.g., Two hundred forty-five) is known as the number name.
• **Abacus Representation**: An abacus uses beads on rods to represent the value of digits in the Hundreds, Tens, and Ones columns.
• **Empty Rods**: If a rod on an abacus has no beads, it represents the digit 0 in that specific place value.

• **Expanded Form**: This is the process of breaking a number into the sum of its place values (e.g., 342 = 300 + 40 + 2).
• **Significance of Expanded Form**: It helps in understanding the actual value each digit contributes to the total number.
• **Place Value vs. Face Value**: The **Face Value** of a digit is the digit itself, regardless of its position in the number.
• **Place Value Definition**: The **Place Value** of a digit is determined by the position it occupies (H, T, or O).
• **Example of Place Value**: In the number 567, the digit 5 has a face value of 5, but a place value of 500.
• **Zero's Place Value**: The digit 0 always has a place value of 0, no matter which position it occupies.
• **Hundreds Column**: The digit in the hundreds column represents the number of 100s (e.g., 4 in the hundreds place is 400).
• **Tens Column**: The digit in the tens column represents the number of 10s (e.g., 4 in the tens place is 40).
• **Ones Column**: The digit in the ones column represents the number of 1s (e.g., 4 in the ones place is 4).
• **Decomposition**: Breaking down a number like 789 into 7 hundreds, 8 tens, and 9 ones is essential for mental arithmetic.
• **Addition of Place Values**: Adding the expanded parts (300 + 20 + 5) always results in the original number (325).
• **Visualizing Hundreds**: A flat square block in base-10 blocks represents 100 units.
• **Visualizing Tens**: A long rod in base-10 blocks represents 10 units.
• **Visualizing Ones**: A single small cube in base-10 blocks represents 1 unit.
• **Consistency**: The place value system remains consistent for all numbers up to 999.
• **Mathematical Notation**: Always ensure the H, T, and O headers are aligned correctly when writing numbers vertically.

• **Number Sequences**: Counting forward from a given number involves adding 1 to the ones place repeatedly.
• **Counting by 1s**: When the ones place reaches 9, the next count resets the ones to 0 and increases the tens place by 1.
• **Carrying Over**: When the tens place reaches 9, the next count resets the tens to 0 and increases the hundreds place by 1.
• **Counting by 10s**: This involves keeping the hundreds and ones digits constant while increasing the tens digit by 1.
• **Example of Counting by 10s**: 215, 225, 235, 245, 255, and so on.
• **Counting by 100s**: This involves keeping the tens and ones digits constant while increasing the hundreds digit by 1.
• **Example of Counting by 100s**: 120, 220, 320, 420, 520, 620, 720, 820, 920.
• **Skip Counting**: A technique used to count numbers faster by skipping a fixed amount (e.g., skip counting by 2s, 5s, or 10s).
• **Pattern Recognition**: Identifying the rule in a sequence (e.g., +1, +10, or +100) is crucial for completing number patterns.
• **Backward Counting**: Counting in reverse order from a larger number to a smaller number (e.g., 500, 499, 498).
• **Decreasing Sequences**: In backward counting, the value of the digits decreases according to the rule provided.
• **Number Line**: A visual tool consisting of a straight line with numbers marked at equal intervals to show order and magnitude.
• **Using the Number Line**: Moving to the right on a number line represents increasing values; moving to the left represents decreasing values.
• **Intervals**: The distance between two consecutive marks on a number line represents the unit of counting.
• **Application**: Number lines are highly effective for comparing two numbers to see which is larger.

• **Comparison of Numbers**: The process of determining which of two numbers is greater, smaller, or if they are equal.
• **Rule 1 (Hundreds)**: If the hundreds digits are different, the number with the larger hundreds digit is the greater number.
• **Rule 2 (Tens)**: If the hundreds digits are the same, compare the tens digits; the number with the larger tens digit is greater.
• **Rule 3 (Ones)**: If both hundreds and tens digits are the same, compare the ones digits; the number with the larger ones digit is greater.
• **Equality**: Two numbers are equal if they have the same digit in the hundreds, tens, and ones places.
• **Greater Than Symbol (>)**: Used to indicate that the number on the left is larger than the number on the right.
• **Less Than Symbol (<)**: Used to indicate that the number on the left is smaller than the number on the right.
• **Equal To Symbol (=)**: Used to indicate that two numbers have the exact same value.
• **Alligator Analogy**: The open mouth of the symbol (>) always faces the larger number, mimicking an alligator eating the bigger portion.
• **Comparing 3-digit and 2-digit numbers**: Any 3-digit number is always greater than any 2-digit number.
• **Comparing 3-digit and 1-digit numbers**: Any 3-digit number is always greater than any 1-digit number.
• **Step-by-Step Comparison**: Always start comparing from the leftmost digit (the Hundreds place).
• **Consistency in Comparison**: Never start comparing from the ones place, as the hundreds place holds the most weight.
• **Practice of Signs**: Mastery of the symbols >, <, and = is fundamental for algebraic foundations.
• **Logical Deduction**: If 456 > 450, it is because 6 (in the ones place) is greater than 0 (in the ones place).

• **Ordering Numbers**: Arranging a set of numbers in a specific sequence based on their values.
• **Ascending Order**: Arranging numbers from the smallest to the largest (also known as increasing order).
• **Descending Order**: Arranging numbers from the largest to the smallest (also known as decreasing order).
• **Strategy for Ascending Order**: Find the smallest number first, then the next smallest, and continue until the largest is reached.
• **Strategy for Descending Order**: Find the largest number first, then the next largest, and continue until the smallest is reached.
• **Identifying Extremes**: In any set of numbers, the first step is to identify the absolute smallest and absolute largest values.
• **Sorting Process**: Use the comparison rules (Hundreds, then Tens, then Ones) to sort the middle numbers.
• **Visual Aids**: Writing numbers on cards and physically moving them helps in understanding the concept of ordering.
• **Number Sets**: Ordering is usually performed on a set of 3 to 5 numbers in Class 2 mathematics.
• **Verification**: After ordering, read the sequence aloud to ensure it follows the intended logical flow (increasing or decreasing).
• **Common Mistakes**: Ensure that numbers with the same hundreds digit are carefully compared by their tens digit.
• **Real-world Application**: Ordering is used in daily tasks like arranging books by page count or organizing items by price.
• **Consistency**: Once the order is established, verify that no numbers from the original set were omitted.
• **Comparison Check**: If a set is 234, 199, 567, the ascending order is 199, 234, 567.
• **Comparison Check**: If a set is 234, 199, 567, the descending order is 567, 234, 199.

• **Successor**: The number that comes immediately after a given number.
• **Calculating Successor**: To find the successor, add 1 to the given number (e.g., Successor of 450 is 451).
• **Predecessor**: The number that comes immediately before a given number.
• **Calculating Predecessor**: To find the predecessor, subtract 1 from the given number (e.g., Predecessor of 450 is 449).
• **Boundary Conditions**: The successor of 999 is 1000, which is the first four-digit number.
• **Boundary Conditions**: The predecessor of 100 is 99, which is the largest two-digit number.
• **Relationship**: The number itself lies exactly between its predecessor and its successor.
• **Number Between**: Given two numbers, the "number between" is the value that falls in the middle of the sequence.
• **Example of Between**: In the sequence 344, 345, 346, the number 345 is the number between 344 and 346.
• **Logical Flow**: Successors and predecessors are essential for understanding the continuity of the number system.
• **Mental Math**: Quickly identifying the successor or predecessor helps in faster mental calculation.
• **Sequence Completion**: Often used in exercises to complete a chain of three consecutive numbers.
• **Number Line Utility**: On a number line, the successor is the mark to the immediate right of the number.
• **Number Line Utility**: On a number line, the predecessor is the mark to the immediate left of the number.
• **Zero's Role**: While 0 is not a 3-digit number, it is the predecessor of 1, establishing the base of the number line.

• **Even Numbers**: Numbers that can be grouped into pairs without any remainder.
• **Identifying Even Numbers**: A number is even if its ones digit is 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8.
• **Examples of Even Numbers**: 102, 254, 386, 490, 998.
• **Odd Numbers**: Numbers that cannot be grouped into pairs perfectly, leaving a remainder of 1.
• **Identifying Odd Numbers**: A number is odd if its ones digit is 1, 3, 5, 7, or 9.
• **Examples of Odd Numbers**: 101, 253, 385, 497, 999.
• **The Ones Digit Rule**: The hundreds and tens digits do not affect whether a number is even or odd; only the ones digit matters.
• **Alternating Pattern**: Even and odd numbers always alternate in the number sequence (e.g., 101 [Odd], 102 [Even], 103 [Odd]).
• **Addition Property**: Adding two even numbers always results in an even number.
• **Addition Property**: Adding two odd numbers always results in an even number.
• **Addition Property**: Adding an even and an odd number always results in an odd number.
• **Visualizing Pairs**: Even numbers represent a complete set of pairs; odd numbers have one "leftover" unit.
• **Counting by 2s**: Counting by 2s starting from an even number will always yield even numbers.
• **Counting by 2s**: Counting by 2s starting from an odd number will always yield odd numbers.
• **Real-world Context**: Even and odd numbers are used in house numbering systems on opposite sides of a street.

• **Ordinal Numbers**: Numbers that indicate the position or order of an object in a sequence (e.g., 1st, 2nd, 3rd).
• **Cardinal vs. Ordinal**: Cardinal numbers (1, 2, 3) tell "how many," while ordinal numbers (1st, 2nd, 3rd) tell "which position."
• **First Ten Ordinals**: 1st (First), 2nd (Second), 3rd (Third), 4th (Fourth), 5th (Fifth), 6th (Sixth), 7th (Seventh), 8th (Eighth), 9th (Ninth), 10th (Tenth).
• **Suffixes**: Most ordinal numbers are formed by adding "-th" to the cardinal number (e.g., 4 becomes 4th).
• **Exceptions**: "First," "Second," and "Third" are unique and do not follow the "-th" rule.
• **Usage**: Ordinal numbers are used to describe rank, dates, or the order of events in a story.
• **Sequence Application**: In a race, the person who finishes first is in the 1st position.
• **Calendar Application**: Dates are expressed using ordinal numbers (e.g., "The 5th of May").
• **Classroom Application**: Describing the position of a student in a row (e.g., "He is sitting in the 10th seat").
• **Writing Ordinals**: Always include the suffix (st, nd, rd, th) when writing ordinal numbers in short form.
• **Spelling**: Pay close attention to spelling changes, such as "Fifth" (not Fiveth) and "Ninth" (not Nineth).
• **Ordinal Sequence**: Ordinal numbers are always relative to a starting point (the 1st position).
• **Directionality**: Ordinal positions are usually counted from left to right or top to bottom.
• **Logical Order**: Understanding ordinals helps in organizing data and following step-by-step instructions.
• **Comparison**: Ordinal numbers are not used for mathematical operations like addition or subtraction, but for labeling.

• **Number Patterns**: A series of numbers that follow a specific rule or logic.
• **Increasing Patterns**: Patterns where the numbers grow larger by a constant amount (e.g., +2, +5, +10).
• **Decreasing Patterns**: Patterns where the numbers grow smaller by a constant amount (e.g., -2, -5, -10).
• **Rule Identification**: The first step in solving a pattern is to find the difference between two consecutive numbers.
• **Constant Difference**: If the difference between all consecutive numbers is the same, the pattern is consistent.
• **Complex Patterns**: Patterns can involve alternating rules, such as +2, then +3, then +2, then +3.
• **Visual Patterns**: Patterns can also be represented by shapes or objects increasing or decreasing in count.
• **Number Series**: A sequence of numbers that requires the next three or four terms to be filled in.
• **Problem Solving**: To find the next number, apply the identified rule to the last known number in the series.
• **Verification**: Check if the rule works for every pair of numbers in the given sequence.
• **Skip Counting as a Pattern**: Skip counting by 2s, 5s, or 10s is a fundamental type of number pattern.
• **Pattern Growth**: Patterns can grow rapidly if the rule involves multiplication, though this is advanced for Class 2.
• **Logical Thinking**: Developing the ability to spot patterns is a core skill in mathematical reasoning.
• **Creative Patterns**: Students can create their own patterns to test their understanding of number sequences.
• **Pattern Completion**: Always ensure the final answer fits the established logic of the entire sequence.
