• **Chapter Introduction:** Welcome to Chapter 14 of Class 6 Secondary Mathematics under the DAV Dictionary curriculum, dedicated entirely to the fundamental geometric art and science of **Constructions**.
• **DAV Academic Objective:** The primary objective of this chapter is to transition students from rough sketches of geometric figures to precise, mathematically accurate drawings using standard mathematical instruments.
• **Definition of Geometrical Construction:** **Construction** refers to the drawing of geometric figures such as line segments, angles, perpendiculars, and circles with absolute precision using only approved instruments without relying on visual estimation.
• **Role of Geometry Box:** Every accurate geometric construction depends upon the correct handling, maintenance, and precision of the instruments found inside a standard **geometry box** or mathematical instrument box.
• **Instrument 1 - The Ruler (Scale):** A **ruler** is a straight-edged instrument graduated in centimeters and millimeters, primarily used for drawing straight lines and measuring the exact lengths of line segments.
• **Instrument 2 - The Compass:** A **compass** is a V-shaped drawing instrument featuring one metal pointed leg and one pencil-holding leg, used primarily for drawing circles, arcs, and transferring exact lengths.
• **Instrument 3 - The Divider:** A **divider** looks very similar to a compass but features two metal pointed legs, and its sole purpose is to compare distances and measure line segment lengths without scale markings.
• **Instrument 4 - The Set-Squares:** **Set-squares** are a pair of triangular plastic or wooden templates—one with angles $30^\circ\text{-}60^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ and the other with $45^\circ\text{-}45^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$—used for drawing parallel and perpendicular lines.
• **Instrument 5 - The Protractor:** A **protractor** is a semi-circular transparent plastic instrument graduated from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$ used for measuring existing angles and drawing angles of specified degree measures.
• **Importance of Sharp Pencils:** For all construction work in DAV mathematics, students must use a **sharp, hard lead pencil** (preferably 2H or HB) to ensure that intersection points are fine, precise, and unambiguous.
• **Accuracy in Marking:** Every geometric point marked during construction must be labeled clearly with capital letters (e.g., $A, B, C$) to avoid confusion during step-by-step verification.
• **Role of Arcs:** **Arcs**—curved paths traced by the pencil leg of a compass—form the backbone of geometric constructions, allowing mathematicians to find equidistant points without using measuring scales.
• **Intersection of Arcs:** The precise point where two construction arcs intersect represents a unique spatial coordinate that satisfies specific geometric conditions.
• **Elimination of Freehand Drawing:** In this chapter, freehand sketching is strictly prohibited; all diagrams must reflect strict adherence to geometric theorems and instrumental precision.
• **Foundation for Higher Geometry:** Mastering these basic constructions in Class 6 builds the cognitive and motor foundation required for advanced Euclidean proofs and constructions in higher secondary grades.

• **Concept of a Line Segment:** A **line segment** is a distinct portion of a straight line bounded by two distinct end points, possessing a fixed, measurable length.
• **Need for Construction:** While a line segment can be drawn by simply joining two points using a ruler, specific geometric problems require drawing a line segment whose length is exactly equal to a given line segment.
• **Method 1 - Using a Ruler:** The most basic method to construct a line segment of a given length is to align the zero mark of the **ruler** with a starting point and mark the endpoint at the required numerical graduation.
• **Method 2 - Using Compass and Ruler:** A more robust geometric method involves drawing an arbitrary ray using a ruler, measuring the given source segment using a compass, and transferring that exact opening onto the ray.
• **Step 1 of Compass Transfer:** Draw a straight line or ray of arbitrary length using a ruler and label its starting endpoint as point $A$.
• **Step 2 of Compass Transfer:** Place the metal pointed tip of the compass on one end of the given source line segment (say $X$) and adjust the pencil leg until it rests precisely on the other end ($Y$).
• **Step 3 of Compass Transfer:** Without disturbing the hinge of the compass, lift it carefully and place the metal pointed tip on point $A$ of the newly drawn ray.
• **Step 4 of Compass Transfer:** Swing a sharp pencil arc to intersect the ray at a new point, which can be labeled as point $B$.
• **Result of Segment Transfer:** The resulting line segment $AB$ is guaranteed to be equal in length to the original source line segment $XY$.
• **Verification of Length:** Students can verify the precision of the construction by measuring the newly constructed segment $AB$ against the original segment using a divider.
• **Constructing the Sum of Segments:** To construct a line segment whose length equals the sum of two given segments $AB$ and $CD$, construct $AB$ first and then consecutively extend it by transferring segment $CD$ along the same straight path.
• **Constructing the Difference of Segments:** To construct a line segment equal to the difference between a longer segment and a shorter segment, transfer the shorter segment backward from the endpoint of the longer segment.
• **Precision Tip:** Always ensure that the pencil lead in the compass is firmly tightened and sharpened to a chisel or conical point to prevent cumulative measurement errors.
• **Role of the Divider in Checking:** The **divider** can be used to pick up the length of one segment and test if it matches another by checking if both metal tips coincide perfectly with the endpoints.
• **DAV Classroom Practice:** Teachers emphasize that construction lines (arcs and construction rays) must never be erased, as they serve as proof of the geometric method used.

• **Introduction to Circles:** A **circle** is the locus of all points in a plane that maintain a constant, fixed distance from a single fixed point called the center.
• **Definition of Radius:** The **radius** of a circle is the fixed distance from the center to any point lying on the boundary of the circle.
• **Fundamental Tool for Circles:** The **compass** is the specialized instrument dedicated to the mechanical drawing of circles and circular arcs.
• **Step 1 of Circle Construction:** Mark a clear point on the paper using a sharp pencil and label this point as $O$, representing the **center** of the circle.
• **Step 2 of Circle Construction:** Use a ruler to measure the required radius length (e.g., $4\text{ cm}$) by placing the metal pointer of the compass at the $0\text{ cm}$ mark and adjusting the pencil lead until it touches the $4\text{ cm}$ graduation mark.
• **Step 3 of Circle Construction:** Fix the metal pointed leg firmly on the center point $O$ without letting it slip or punch an excessively large hole in the paper.
• **Step 4 of Circle Construction:** Rotate the compass smoothly by holding its upper knob between the thumb and index finger, sweeping the pencil leg in a continuous $360^\circ$ motion.
• **Concentric Circles:** **Concentric circles** are two or more circles that share the exact same center point but possess different radii lengths.
• **Constructing Concentric Circles:** To draw concentric circles of radii $3\text{ cm}$ and $5\text{ cm}$, keep the metal pointer fixed at center $O$, draw the first circle with a $3\text{ cm}$ radius opening, and then expand the compass to $5\text{ cm}$ to draw the outer circle from the same center.
• **Intersecting Circles:** Two circles of equal or different radii can be constructed such that their boundaries cross each other at exactly two distinct points, creating symmetrical intersection points.
• **Radius vs. Diameter in Construction:** If a construction problem specifies a diameter (e.g., $6\text{ cm}$), students must mentally or mathematically divide it by two to find the radius ($3\text{ cm}$) before setting the compass.
• **Arc of a Circle:** An **arc** is any connected section of the circumference of a circle, frequently used in geometric constructions to locate specific target points.
• **Semicircle Construction:** A semicircle can be constructed by drawing a full circle and then drawing a straight diameter line through the center to bisect the circular boundary.
• **Symmetry in Circles:** Every point on a constructed circle is equidistant from its center, showcasing the fundamental rotational symmetry inherent in circular geometry.
• **Common Error Prevention:** A common student error is measuring the compass opening against the air instead of flat against a rigid ruler resting on the desk; this leads to radius inaccuracies.

• **Concept of Perpendicularity:** Two lines, rays, or line segments are said to be **perpendicular** when they intersect each other at a right angle, forming a $90^\circ$ angle at the intersection point.
• **Perpendicular Bisector Definition:** The **perpendicular bisector** of a given line segment is a line that divides the segment into two equal halves (bisects it) and meets it at a $90^\circ$ right angle.
• **Importance of Perpendicular Bisector Construction:** Constructing perpendicular bisectors using a compass and ruler is one of the most powerful and frequently used techniques in classical geometry.
• **Step 1 of Perpendicular Bisector:** Draw a given line segment $AB$ of any desired length (e.g., $7\text{ cm}$) using a ruler.
• **Step 2 of Perpendicular Bisector:** Place the metal pointed tip of the compass at endpoint $A$.
• **Step 3 of Perpendicular Bisector:** Adjust the compass radius so that it is clearly **more than half** the estimated length of segment $AB$. This is a critical mathematical condition for the arcs to intersect.
• **Step 4 of Perpendicular Bisector:** With the compass set to this radius (greater than half of $AB$), draw two long arcs—one above the line segment $AB$ and one below the line segment $AB$.
• **Step 5 of Perpendicular Bisector:** Keeping the exact same compass radius setting, move the metal pointed tip to endpoint $B$.
• **Step 6 of Perpendicular Bisector:** Draw two new arcs—one above $AB$ and one below $AB$—so that they intersect the previously drawn arcs.
• **Labeling Intersection Points:** Label the upper intersection point of the arcs as $P$ and the lower intersection point of the arcs as $Q$.
• **Step 7 of Perpendicular Bisector:** Use a ruler to join point $P$ and point $Q$ with a straight line, extending it to intersect the original segment $AB$ at a point, which can be labeled as $M$.
• **Verification of Bisector:** The line $PQ$ is the required perpendicular bisector of segment $AB$, such that angle $AMP$ measures $90^\circ$ and length $AM$ equals length $MB$.
• **Why "More Than Half" is Required:** If the compass radius is set to less than half of $AB$, the arcs drawn from $A$ and $B$ will never meet or intersect each other in the upper and lower half-planes.
• **Symmetry of the Bisector:** Every single point lying on the constructed perpendicular bisector $PQ$ is equidistant from the two endpoints $A$ and $B$.
• **DAV Examination Tip:** Students must show all construction arcs clearly; papers evaluated without visible intersecting arcs for perpendicular bisectors lose marks according to DAV board marking schemes.

• **Concept of Angles:** An **angle** is formed when two distinct rays share a common initial endpoint, referred to as the vertex of the angle.
• **Standard Instrument for Angles:** While protractors are used to measure angles, classical geometric constructions require constructing standard angles ($60^\circ, 120^\circ, 90^\circ, 45^\circ$, etc.) using **only a ruler and a compass**.
• **Concept of $60^\circ$ Angle:** An angle measuring exactly $60^\circ$ is the foundational building block for constructing multiple other angles because it forms an equilateral triangle geometry.
• **Step 1 for $60^\circ$ Angle:** Draw a ray $OA$ of any convenient length using a ruler, with $O$ acting as the starting vertex.
• **Step 2 for $60^\circ$ Angle:** Place the metal pointed tip of the compass at the vertex $O$ and, with any convenient radius, draw a wide circular arc that cuts the ray $OA$ at some point, labeling this intersection point as $B$.
• **Step 3 for $60^\circ$ Angle:** Without changing the compass radius setting, lift the compass and place its metal pointer firmly on intersection point $B$.
• **Step 4 for $60^\circ$ Angle:** Swing a pencil arc that intersects the first arc at a new point, labeling this new intersection point as $C$.
• **Step 5 for $60^\circ$ Angle:** Use a ruler to draw a straight ray starting from vertex $O$ and passing directly through intersection point $C$, extending it outward.
• **Result of $60^\circ$ Construction:** The angle formed between ray $OC$ and ray $OA$ (i.e., $\angle AOC$) is mathematically guaranteed to measure exactly $60^\circ$.
• **Concept of $120^\circ$ Angle:** An angle measuring $120^\circ$ represents two consecutive $60^\circ$ arc units measured along the same circular path from the baseline ray.
• **Constructing $120^\circ$ Angle:** From the previous $60^\circ$ construction, keeping the same compass radius, place the metal pointer on point $C$ and swing another arc further along the main circle to create a third intersection point $D$.
• **Result of $120^\circ$ Construction:** Joining vertex $O$ through point $D$ creates an angle $\angle AOD$ measuring exactly $120^\circ$ ($60^\circ + 60^\circ$).
• **Equilateral Triangle Connection:** Connecting points $O, B,$ and $C$ with straight line segments forms an equilateral triangle where all interior angles equal $60^\circ$.
• **Arc Radius Independence:** The initial radius chosen to draw the arc from vertex $O$ does not affect the final angle measure, provided the compass width remains strictly constant during the subsequent arc-cutting steps.
• **Precision Check:** Using a protractor to check a constructed $60^\circ$ angle is a recommended self-verification habit for students before finalizing their DAV geometry examination sheets.

• **Concept of Angle Bisector:** An **angle bisector** is a ray that divides a given angle into two equal, identical smaller angles, each measuring exactly half of the original angle.
• **Significance of Angle Bisectors:** Angle bisector construction is essential for creating compound angles and achieving angular symmetry without relying on protractor degree scales.
• **Step 1 of Angle Bisector:** Draw any given angle $\angle POQ$ using a protractor or as the result of a prior construction (e.g., an arbitrary acute angle).
• **Step 2 of Angle Bisector:** Place the metal pointed tip of the compass at the vertex $O$ of the angle.
• **Step 3 of Angle Bisector:** With any convenient radius, draw an arc that cuts both arms of the angle—ray $OP$ and ray $OQ$—at two distinct points.
• **Step 4 of Angle Bisector:** Label the intersection point on ray $OP$ as point $X$ and the intersection point on ray $OQ$ as point $Y$.
• **Step 5 of Angle Bisector:** Place the metal pointed tip of the compass on point $X$, and with a convenient radius (greater than half the distance between $X$ and $Y$), draw an arc in the interior of the angle.
• **Step 6 of Angle Bisector:** Without changing the compass radius setting, place the metal pointed tip on point $Y$ and draw another arc to intersect the previously drawn arc in the interior space.
• **Step 7 of Angle Bisector:** Label the intersection point of these two interior arcs as point $Z$.
• **Step 8 of Angle Bisector:** Use a ruler to draw a straight ray starting from vertex $O$ and passing directly through the intersection point $Z$.
• **Result of Bisector Construction:** Ray $OZ$ is the required angle bisector of $\angle POQ$, dividing it such that $\angle POZ = \angle ZOQ$.
• **Constructing $30^\circ$ Angle:** By constructing a standard $60^\circ$ angle first and then applying the angle bisector method to it, students can accurately construct an angle measuring exactly $30^\circ$.
• **Constructing $90^\circ$ Angle:** A $90^\circ$ angle is constructed by bisecting the $180^\circ$ straight angle or by constructing a perpendicular at a point on a line.
• **Constructing $45^\circ$ Angle:** By constructing a $90^\circ$ right angle first and then bisecting it using the angle bisector method, students obtain an angle measuring exactly $45^\circ$.
• **Nested Constructions:** DAV examination problems frequently test nested constructions, such as constructing a $60^\circ$ angle, bisecting it to get $30^\circ$, and further bisecting that to get a $15^\circ$ angle.

• **Concept of a Right Angle ($90^\circ$):** A **right angle** is an angle measuring exactly $90^\circ$, representing perpendicular intersection between two straight lines or rays.
• **Method 1 for $90^\circ$ Construction (Perpendicular at a Point):** To construct a $90^\circ$ angle at a specific point on a line, treat the point as the center of a semi-circular reference arc.
• **Step 1 of $90^\circ$ Construction:** Draw a line $AB$ and mark a point $P$ on it where the perpendicular and $90^\circ$ angle are required to be erected.
• **Step 2 of $90^\circ$ Construction:** With point $P$ as the center and any convenient radius, draw a semi-circular arc that cuts line $AB$ at two points on either side of $P$; label these points $X$ and $Y$.
• **Step 3 of $90^\circ$ Construction:** With point $X$ as center and a radius greater than half the distance $XY$, draw an arc above the semi-circle.
• **Step 4 of $90^\circ$ Construction:** With point $Y$ as center and the exact same radius, draw another arc to intersect the first arc at a point, labeling it $C$.
• **Step 5 of $90^\circ$ Construction:** Join point $P$ and point $C$ with a straight line using a ruler.
• **Result of $90^\circ$ Construction:** The line segment $PC$ is perpendicular to line $AB$ at point $P$, forming a $90^\circ$ angle ($\angle APC = 90^\circ$).
• **Method 2 for $90^\circ$ Construction (Using $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$):** Construct a $60^\circ$ angle and a $120^\circ$ angle from the same vertex, then construct the angle bisector between the $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ marks.
• **Mathematical Proof of Method 2:** The midpoint between $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ on a circular arc is calculated as $\frac{60^\circ + 120^\circ}{2} = \frac{180^\circ}{2} = 90^\circ$.
• **Constructing $150^\circ$ Angle:** An angle of $150^\circ$ can be constructed by finding the angle bisector between the $120^\circ$ mark and a $180^\circ$ straight line mark ($120^\circ + 30^\circ = 150^\circ$).
• **Constructing $75^\circ$ Angle:** A $75^\circ$ angle is constructed by finding the angle bisector between the $60^\circ$ mark and the $90^\circ$ mark ($60^\circ + 15^\circ = 75^\circ$).
• **Constructing $105^\circ$ Angle:** A $105^\circ$ angle is constructed by bisecting the region between the $90^\circ$ perpendicular line and the $120^\circ$ arc mark ($90^\circ + 15^\circ = 105^\circ$).
• **Importance of Compass Setting Stability:** Students must ensure that when bisecting angular regions, the compass radius is not accidentally adjusted between placing it on the two reference points.
• **DAV Standards of Neatness:** Intersecting arcs for perpendicular and angle constructions must be clean, distinct, and drawn with single-stroke pencil movements.

• **Concept of Parallel Lines:** **Parallel lines** are two or more straight lines in a plane that never intersect or meet, no matter how far they are extended in either direction, maintaining a constant perpendicular distance between them.
• **Fundamental Property of Parallel Lines:** If two parallel lines are cut by a transversal, their corresponding angles and alternate interior angles are equal.
• **Instrumental Requirements:** Constructing a line parallel to a given line through a point not on the line requires either a ruler and set-squares or a ruler and compass.
• **Method 1 - Using Compass (Alternate Interior Angles):** This classical geometric method uses the property that if alternate interior angles formed by a transversal are equal, the lines are parallel.
• **Step 1 of Parallel Construction:** Draw a given line $l$ and mark a point $P$ outside the line $l$ through which the parallel line must pass.
• **Step 2 of Parallel Construction:** Choose any point $A$ on line $l$ and join $A$ to point $P$ using a ruler to act as a transversal line segment.
• **Step 3 of Parallel Construction:** With point $A$ as the center and any convenient radius, draw an arc cutting line $l$ at point $B$ and line segment $AP$ at point $C$.
• **Step 4 of Parallel Construction:** With point $P$ as the center and the exact same radius used in Step 3, draw a corresponding arc cutting line segment $AP$ at a new point $D$.
• **Step 5 of Parallel Construction:** Measure the exact opening length between point $B$ and point $C$ using the compass.
• **Step 6 of Parallel Construction:** Place the metal pointer of the compass on point $D$ and draw an arc to intersect the arc drawn in Step 4 at a new point, labeling it $E$.
• **Step 7 of Parallel Construction:** Use a ruler to draw a straight line passing through point $P$ and point $E$, extending it in both directions.
• **Result of Parallel Construction:** The newly drawn line through $P$ is strictly parallel to the original line $l$.
• **Method 2 - Using Set-Squares:** Place the edge of a set-square along the given line $l$, place a ruler firmly against another edge of the set-square, and slide the set-square along the ruler until its edge reaches point $P$ to draw the parallel line.
• **Perpendicular Distance Check:** Students can verify parallelism by measuring the perpendicular distance from multiple points on the new line down to the original line $l$ using a set-square.

• **Comprehensive Review of Constructions:** Mastery of Chapter 14 requires understanding the symbiotic relationship between basic elements: points, lines, rays, arcs, circles, and angles.
• **Summary of Standard Angle Table:** DAV students must be fluent in constructing the following standard angles without a protractor: $15^\circ, 30^\circ, 45^\circ, 60^\circ, 75^\circ, 90^\circ, 105^\circ, 120^\circ, 135^\circ, 150^\circ$, and $180^\circ$.
• **Classification of Triangles by Construction:** Triangles can be constructed using various sets of given parameters, such as Side-Side-Side (SSS), Side-Angle-Side (SAS), and Angle-Side-Angle (ASA).
• **Constructing SSS Triangles:** To construct a triangle given three side lengths (e.g., $5\text{ cm}, 6\text{ cm}, 7\text{ cm}$), draw the longest base segment first, then use a compass set to the remaining two lengths to find the third vertex where their arcs intersect.
• **Constructing SAS Triangles:** To construct a triangle given two sides and the included angle (e.g., $AB = 6\text{ cm}, \angle A = 60^\circ, AC = 5\text{ cm}$), draw base $AB$, construct the specified angle at vertex $A$, and cut an arc of $5\text{ cm}$ along the angle ray to locate vertex $C$.
• **Constructing ASA Triangles:** To construct a triangle given two angles and the included side (e.g., $\angle B = 60^\circ, BC = 7\text{ cm}, \angle C = 45^\circ$), draw base $BC$, construct $\angle B$ at one end and $\angle C$ at the other end, and extend their rays until they intersect at vertex $A$.
• **Constructing Right Triangles (RHS):** A right-angled triangle can be constructed when the hypotenuse and one side are given (Right-angle Hypotenuse Side criterion).
• **Importance of Rough Sketches:** Before beginning any complex geometric construction in DAV exams, students must draw a freehand **rough sketch** labeled with given measurements to plan the step-by-step construction sequence.
• **Troubleshooting Compass Slip:** If a compass slips during arc drawing, students must re-anchor the metal point firmly in the exact same paper indentation to maintain structural integrity.
• **Avoiding Pencil Lead Wobble:** A loose compass screw causes the pencil leg to shift during rotation; tightening the central hinge nut is a mandatory pre-construction check.
• **Erasure Protocol:** Construction arcs should never be erased; if a mistake occurs, it is better to erase only the incorrect lines while keeping valid construction steps visible and clear.
• **Precision Standards:** Geometric constructions in the DAV curriculum are judged on a tolerance of $\pm1\text{ mm}$ for lengths and $\pm1^\circ$ for angle degrees.
• **Real-World Applications:** Geometric constructions form the core of architectural blueprints, engineering drafting, cartography, and computer-aided design (CAD) software algorithms.
• **Final Chapter Summary:** Chapter 14 equips students with the instrumental dexterity and spatial reasoning required to translate theoretical geometry into tangible, accurate visual reality.
