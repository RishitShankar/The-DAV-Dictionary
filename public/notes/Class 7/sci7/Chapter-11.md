• **Introduction to Chapter 11**: Welcome to the DAV Academic Study Notes for Class 7 Science, Chapter 11, focusing on "Electric Charges at Rest", also known as electrostatics, which deals with stationary electrical phenomena.
• **Scope of Electrostatics**: This chapter explores the fascinating world of static electricity, investigating how objects acquire electric charges, how these charges interact, and the natural consequences of electric charges at rest.
• **Historical Roots**: The study of static electricity traces back to ancient Greece, where philosophers observed that amber, when rubbed against animal fur, acquired the mysterious property of attracting light objects like dust, feathers, and dry leaves.
• **Etymology of Electricity**: The English word "electricity" is derived from the Greek word for amber, which is *electron*, highlighting the foundational role amber played in the historical discovery of electrical phenomena.
• **Thales of Miletus**: Around 600 BCE, the Greek philosopher Thales of Miletus was among the first to document the attractive properties of rubbed amber, marking the dawn of humankind's formal inquiry into electrical forces.
• **Definition of Electric Charge**: **Electric Charge** is an intrinsic, fundamental property of matter carried by elementary particles like protons and electrons that causes them to experience a force when placed in an electric field.
• **State of Rest**: In this chapter, the focus is exclusively on static charges—charges that reside on the surface of an object and remain stationary, unlike current electricity where charges flow through a conductor.
• **Everyday Encounters with Static Electricity**: Static electricity is commonly experienced in daily life, such as the crackling sound and sparks when taking off a woolen sweater in winter, or hair standing on end after removing a plastic comb.
• **Triboelectric Effect**: The fundamental mechanism behind static electricity is the **Triboelectric Effect**, a type of contact electrification in which certain materials become electrically charged after they come into contact with another different material through friction.
• **Neutral Nature of Matter**: Under normal, uncharged conditions, all matter in the universe is electrically neutral because every atom contains an equal number of positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons.
• **Subatomic Architecture**: An atom consists of a dense central nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons, surrounded by shells of orbiting negatively charged electrons.
• **Role of Electrons**: While protons are tightly bound inside the atomic nucleus and cannot be easily dislodged, the outermost valence electrons are loosely held and can be transferred from one object to another via friction.
• **Gaining Negative Charge**: When an object gains extra electrons through rubbing or physical contact, it develops an excess of negative charge, making the overall body negatively charged.
• **Losing Negative Charge**: When an object loses some of its electrons to another material during friction, it is left with a deficit of electrons, resulting in a net positive charge due to the remaining uncompensated protons.
• **Conservation of Charge**: The charging process does not create or destroy charges; it merely redistributes electrons from one object to another, illustrating the universal conservation of electric charge.

• **Two Kinds of Charges**: Experimental evidence proves there are only two fundamental types of electric charges in the universe: **Positive Charge** and **Negative Charge**, a nomenclature originally coined by Benjamin Franklin.
• **Benjamin Franklin's Contribution**: The 18th-century scientist and statesman Benjamin Franklin proposed the single-fluid theory of electricity, designating the two opposing states of electrification as positive (+) and negative (-).
• **Law of Charges (Fundamental Rule)**: The primary law of electrostatics states that **like charges repel** each other, while **unlike charges attract** each other with a measurable electrostatic force.
• **Behavior of Like Charges**: Two objects possessing the same type of charge—either both positive or both negative—will exert a mutual repulsive force, pushing each other away.
• **Behavior of Unlike Charges**: Two objects possessing opposite types of charges—one positive and one negative—will exert a mutual attractive force, pulling each other closer together.
• **Detection of Charge**: To determine whether an object is charged, and the nature of its charge, scientists and students use a specialized scientific instrument known as an **electroscope**.
• **Principle of the Electroscope**: An electroscope operates entirely on the electrostatic principle that like charges repel each other, causing movable metal leaves or foils to diverge when a charge is applied.
• **Gold-Leaf Electroscope**: The classic **Gold-Leaf Electroscope** consists of a vertical metal rod housed in a protective glass flask, with two thin strips of gold or aluminum foil suspended at the bottom end.
• **Capped Metal Rod**: The top of the metal rod in an electroscope features a smooth brass disk or cap, which serves as the primary contact point for transferring charges from test objects.
• **Insulating Stopper**: The metal rod of an electroscope passes through a non-conducting rubber or cork stopper at the neck of the glass container to prevent any acquired charge from leaking away into the earth.
• **Housing and Earthing**: The glass enclosure of the electroscope protects the delicate metal leaves from external air currents, and sometimes features a grounded metal foil lining to neutralize stray electrostatic fields.
• **Charging by Contact (Conduction)**: An electroscope can be charged by touching a charged body directly to the top brass cap, allowing electrons to flow directly into or out of the instrument's metal components.
• **Divergence of Leaves**: When a charged object touches the brass cap, the charge travels down the rod and distributes equally to both gold leaves; because both leaves acquire the exact same charge, they repel and diverge.
• **Degree of Divergence**: The angle of divergence (how far apart the leaves move) is directly proportional to the magnitude of the electric charge imparted to the electroscope.
• **Testing Neutral Objects**: If a neutral object is touched to the brass cap of an uncharged electroscope, nothing happens because no net charge is available to be transferred to the leaves.

• **Methods of Charging**: Objects can be electrically charged through various physical processes, with the three primary methods being **Friction**, **Conduction**, and **Induction**.
• **Charging by Friction**: **Friction** (or triboelectrification) involves rubbing two different insulating materials together; the mechanical work done overcomes surface adhesion and transfers electrons from one material to the other.
• **Examples of Friction Charging**: Rubbing a plastic scale with dry hair, rubbing an ebonite rod with fur, or rubbing a glass rod with silk cloth are classic textbook examples of charging by friction.
• **Charging by Conduction**: **Conduction** is the process of charging an uncharged neutral conductor by bringing it into direct physical contact with an already charged object, resulting in a sharing of charges.
• **Direction of Flow in Conduction**: During conduction, electrons flow between the objects until an electrical equilibrium (equal distribution of potential) is reached between the contacting bodies.
• **Charging by Induction**: **Induction** is a revolutionary method of charging a neutral object *without any direct physical contact* with the charging body, relying instead on electrostatic field forces.
• **Mechanism of Induction**: When a charged body is brought close to (but not touching) a neutral conductor, it attracts opposite charges to the near side and repels like charges to the far side of the conductor.
• **Temporary vs. Permanent Induction**: While the separation of charges within a neutral body during induction is temporary as long as the inducing charge is near, it can be made permanent using a grounding technique.
• **The Process of Earthing**: **Earthing** (or grounding) is the act of connecting a charged or induced object to the Earth with a conductor, allowing excess electrons to flow safely into the vast, neutral reservoir of the Earth.
• **Step-by-Step Permanent Induction (Step 1)**: Bring a negatively charged plastic rod close to an isolated neutral metal sphere mounted on an insulating stand; positive charges accumulate on the near side, and negative charges move to the far side.
• **Step-by-Step Permanent Induction (Step 2)**: While holding the charged rod in place, touch the far side of the metal sphere with a finger to provide a grounding path for the repelled electrons to escape into the Earth.
• **Step-by-Step Permanent Induction (Step 3)**: Remove the finger (breaking the ground connection), trapping the electron deficit, and then remove the charged rod; the metal sphere is now left with a permanent net positive charge.
• **Polarization of Insulators**: Even non-conducting materials (insulators or dielectrics) experience a microscopic internal charge redistribution called **polarization** when exposed to an external electric field.
• **Attractive Force on Neutral Objects**: The phenomenon of induction explains why a charged comb can attract small, uncharged neutral bits of paper: the comb induces an opposite charge on the near side of the paper bits, causing attraction.
• **Role of Humidity**: Static electricity experiments work best on dry, cold winter days because high humidity in the air causes moisture to form a conductive film on objects, allowing static charges to leak away instantly into the atmosphere.

• **Conductors and Insulators**: Materials in our environment are broadly classified into two major categories based on their ability to allow electric charges to pass through them: **Conductors** and **Insulators**.
• **Definition of Conductors**: **Conductors** are materials that permit electric charges (electrons) to move freely and easily through their internal atomic structure due to the presence of abundant free electrons.
• **Examples of Conductors**: All metals (such as copper, aluminum, iron, silver, and gold), tap water, graphite, acids, and the human body are excellent examples of electrical conductors.
• **Definition of Insulators**: **Insulators** (also known as non-conductors or dielectrics) are materials that tightly bind their electrons and do not allow electric charges to flow freely through them.
• **Examples of Insulators**: Rubber, plastic, glass, dry wood, ebonite, amber, silk, wool, porcelain, and pure distilled water are standard examples of electrical insulators.
• **Practical Uses of Insulators**: Insulators are critically important for safety and are used to coat metal wires, make screwdriver handles, and create insulating gloves worn by electricians to prevent electric shocks.
• **Semiconductors (Brief Mention)**: Certain materials, like silicon and germanium, have electrical properties intermediate between conductors and insulators and are known as **semiconductors**, forming the backbone of modern electronics.
• **Static Charge Retention on Insulators**: Because insulators do not allow charges to move, any static charge placed on an insulator stays right where it was created, making insulators ideal for studying static electricity.
• **Discharge of Conductors**: If a conductor is charged by friction (like holding a metal rod with an insulating handle), any excess charge is instantly conducted away through the human body and the Earth unless the conductor is completely isolated.
• **Electrostatic Series**: Scientists use an **Electrostatic Series** list to predict which material will acquire a positive or negative charge when rubbed against another, ranking materials from most positive to most negative.
• **Position of Glass in the Series**: Glass rubbed with silk tends to lose electrons easily, placing it near the positive end of the electrostatic series.
• **Position of Plastic/Ebonite in the Series**: Plastic, rubber, and ebonite tend to gain electrons when rubbed with fur or wool, placing them near the negative end of the electrostatic series.
• **Triboelectric Series Application**: The further apart two materials are on the triboelectric series, the greater the magnitude of the static charge generated between them through friction.
• **Friction and Surface Area**: The efficiency of generating static electricity via friction depends heavily on surface roughness, contact pressure, and the cleanliness of the rubbing surfaces.
• **Safety Warning on Static Sparks**: High accumulations of static charge on non-conductive surfaces can suddenly discharge in the form of a visible spark, posing fire hazards in environments with flammable vapors.

• **Atmospheric Electricity**: Moving beyond laboratory demonstrations, static electricity plays a monumental, awe-inspiring role in nature on a planetary scale through weather phenomena like thunderstorms.
• **Origin of Atmospheric Charges**: During the violent turbulence of a thunderstorm, strong updrafts and downdrafts of wind carry millions of water droplets, ice crystals, and hailstones crashing into each other inside towering cumulonimbus clouds.
• **Cloud Electrification Process**: Through continuous friction and collision between ice particles and supercooled water droplets within thunderclouds, massive quantities of static electricity are generated and separated.
• **Polarity Distribution in Thunderclouds**: As a result of this colossal triboelectric charging, the upper regions of a thundercloud typically accumulate a massive net positive charge, while the lower regions accumulate a dense negative charge.
• **Induction on the Earth's Surface**: The massive negative charge gathering in the lower base of a thundercloud acts as a giant induction source, repelling negative charges deep into the Earth and inducing a strong positive charge on the surface features below.
• **Definition of Lightning**: **Lightning** is a gigantic, highly luminous electrostatic discharge that occurs either between two oppositely charged clouds or between a negatively charged cloud and the positively charged Earth's surface.
• **The Physics of Lightning Strike**: When the electric potential difference between the cloud and the ground becomes so immense that it overcomes the insulating breakdown strength of the intervening air, a colossal electrical spark—lightning—breaks through.
• **Path of Least Resistance**: Lightning travels through the atmosphere along paths of ionized air molecules that offer the least resistance, often appearing as jagged, branching streaks spanning miles across the sky.
• **Magnitude of Lightning Energy**: A single typical lightning stroke carries an unimaginably massive electrical current of up to 30,000 to 100,000 amperes and generates temperatures hotter than the surface of the Sun (around 30,000 degrees Celsius).
• **Definition of Thunder**: **Thunder** is the loud, booming, rumbling acoustic shockwave that accompanies lightning, caused by the sudden, violent thermal expansion of air superheated by the lightning channel.
• **Speed of Light vs. Sound**: We see lightning almost instantaneously because light travels at approximately 300,000 kilometers per second, whereas thunder reaches our ears much later because sound travels at a sluggish 343 meters per second.
• **Calculating Storm Distance**: By counting the seconds between seeing a flash of lightning and hearing the corresponding thunder and dividing by 3 (or multiplying by 340 meters), one can accurately estimate the distance of the thunderstorm in kilometers.
• **Dangers of Lightning**: Lightning strikes possess devastating destructive power, capable of shattering massive trees, causing catastrophic fires, knocking out electrical power grids, and causing severe injury or death to humans and animals caught in the open.
• **Historical Misconceptions**: In ancient times, lightning was viewed with supernatural dread as an expression of divine wrath, but pioneering scientific investigations by Benjamin Franklin and others proved it is purely an electrical phenomenon.

• **Lightning Safety Protocols**: Understanding the science of electric charges at rest helps formulate life-saving safety rules to protect oneself and property from the lethal hazards of lightning strikes during thunderstorms.
• **Seeking Shelter Indoors**: The single most effective safety rule during a thunderstorm is to seek immediate shelter inside a fully enclosed, permanent building with plumbing and wiring rather than staying out in the open.
• **Unsafe Outdoor Locations**: During an active electrical storm, one must strictly avoid open fields, tall isolated trees, hilltops, athletic sports grounds, metal fences, wire clotheslines, and bodies of water.
• **The Danger of Trees**: Standing under a tall, isolated tree is extremely dangerous because trees project high into the air, acting as natural lightning rods that attract descending electrical discharges.
• **Vehicular Safety**: If caught outdoors while traveling in a car or bus, remain inside the vehicle with the windows rolled up; the metal frame of the vehicle acts as a protective **Faraday Cage**, safely channeling lightning around the occupants.
• **Faraday Cage Principle**: A **Faraday Cage** is an enclosure formed by conducting material that blocks external static electric fields; any excess charge on a Faraday cage resides entirely on its outer surface, leaving the interior space completely safe.
• **Indoor Precautions (Electrical Appliances)**: While inside a building during a storm, unplug all sensitive electronic appliances (televisions, computers, refrigerators) because lightning can cause massive power surges along electrical supply lines.
• **Avoiding Plumbing and Landlines**: Stay away from running water, metal plumbing pipes, and corded landline telephones during a thunderstorm, as electricity from a lightning strike can travel through metal pipes and telephone wires.
• **Lightning Crouch Position**: If no shelter is available and your hair begins to stand on end (a terrifying sign that a lightning strike is imminent), squat low to the ground in a ball, tuck your head, place your hands over your ears, and keep your feet close together to minimize ground contact.
• **Avoiding Group Clustering**: When caught outdoors in a group during a thunderstorm, individuals should spread out rather than huddling together, reducing the risk of multiple casualties if lightning strikes nearby.
• **First Aid for Lightning Victims**: A person struck by lightning does not carry an electrical charge and can be touched safely; immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and calling emergency medical services can save the victim's life.
• **Indoor Safety with Open Windows**: Keep away from open doors and windows during severe thunderstorms, as wind-blown ionized air or a side flash could breach the indoor space.
• **Role of School Curriculum**: Teaching lightning safety in Class 7 Science empowers young students with crucial survival knowledge that can prevent accidents and save lives in disaster-prone regions during monsoon seasons.
• **Community Awareness**: Promoting public awareness campaigns about the dangers of static electricity and lightning safety in rural and urban communities drastically reduces annual fatalities and property damage.

• **Lightning Protection Devices**: To safeguard towering skyscrapers, historical monuments, residential homes, and industrial plants from catastrophic lightning strikes, specialized protective devices are installed.
• **Invention of the Lightning Conductor**: The **Lightning Conductor** (or lightning rod) was brilliantly invented in 1752 by the American scientist, inventor, and philosopher Benjamin Franklin.
• **Primary Purpose of a Lightning Conductor**: A lightning conductor is a metal rod designed to protect buildings from lightning damage by safely intercepting high-voltage electrical strikes and channeling the current harmlessly into the deep Earth.
• **Anatomy of a Lightning Conductor (Top Terminal)**: The uppermost component of a lightning conductor system features a heavy-duty, corrosion-resistant pointed metal rod (usually made of copper or aluminum) mounted securely at the highest point of the building.
• **Anatomy of a Lightning Conductor (Down Conductor)**: A thick, heavy copper cable or strip called the **down conductor** runs securely down the exterior wall of the building, connecting the top pointed rod directly to the ground system.
• **Anatomy of a Lightning Conductor (Earthing Pit)**: At the base of the building, the down conductor is deeply welded to a large copper plate or pipe buried far underground in a moist pit filled with charcoal and salt to ensure ultra-low electrical resistance.
• **Mechanism of Action (Discharge by Point)**: Pointed metal conductors exhibit a phenomenon known as **action of points**, where electric charges concentrate intensely at sharp tips, quietly discharging static electricity into the surrounding air to neutralize approaching storm clouds.
• **Mechanism of Action (Direct Strike Path)**: If a lightning stroke does occur, the sharp upper terminal intercepts the high-voltage electrical arc, instantly capturing the massive current and channeling it down the thick cable, bypassing the building's flammable structure entirely.
• **Importance of Low Resistance**: The entire lightning protection system must maintain an extremely low electrical resistance path to the Earth; any break or corrosion in the down conductor can cause the lightning to arc into the building, causing devastating fires.
• **Protection of Tall Structures**: Iconic structures like the Qutub Minar, the Empire State Building, and tall skyscrapers rely entirely on robust lightning conductor networks to survive severe meteorological storms unscathed.
• **Installation Guidelines**: Lightning conductors must be installed by certified professionals following strict engineering standards (such as Bureau of Indian Standards - BIS specifications) to guarantee total structural safety.
• **Maintenance and Inspection**: Lightning protection systems require regular periodic inspections and electrical resistance testing to ensure that ground connections remain intact and corrosion-free over years of weathering.
• **Misconceptions About Lightning Rods**: Some people incorrectly believe lightning rods *attract* lightning from miles away; in reality, they merely provide a safe, predetermined pathway for lightning that was already striking the immediate vicinity.
• **Historical Significance in Architecture**: The widespread adoption of Benjamin Franklin's lightning rod in the 18th and 19th centuries revolutionized architectural engineering, saving countless historical cathedrals, wooden homes, and human lives across the globe.
