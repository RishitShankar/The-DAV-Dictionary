• **Srinivasa Ramanujan (1887–1920):** An Indian mathematician who, despite having almost no formal training in pure mathematics, made substantial contributions to mathematical analysis, number theory, infinite series, and continued fractions.
• **Birth Details:** He was born on December 22, 1887, in Erode, Madras Presidency (now Tamil Nadu), in his maternal grandparents' residence.
• **Family Background:** He was born into a humble Tamil Brahmin family; his father, Kuppuswamy Srinivasa Iyengar, worked as a clerk in a sari shop, and his mother, Komalatammal, was a housewife who sang at a local temple.
• **Early Childhood in Kumbakonam:** Ramanujan spent most of his childhood in the town of Kumbakonam, which later became the site of his early mathematical explorations.
• **Prodigious Memory:** From a very young age, he demonstrated an exceptional memory and the ability to recall complex mathematical formulas and values of constants to many decimal places.
• **Early Schooling:** He attended Kangayan Primary School and later entered Town Higher Secondary School, where he first encountered formal mathematics.
• **Mastery of Trigonometry:** By the age of 13, he had completely mastered a book on advanced trigonometry written by S.L. Loney, discovering sophisticated theorems on his own.
• **The Turning Point (1903):** At age 16, he obtained a library copy of "A Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics" by G.S. Carr, which contained over 5,000 theorems.
• **Carr’s Synopsis Influence:** This book is credited with awakening Ramanujan’s genius, as he worked through the theorems without the aid of formal proofs, developing his own unique methods of derivation.
• **Mathematical Intuition:** Ramanujan claimed that his family goddess, Namagiri Thayar of Namakkal, provided him with mathematical insights and formulas in his dreams.
• **Academic Struggles:** Despite his brilliance in mathematics, he failed his Fellow of Arts exams at Government Arts College, Kumbakonam, because he neglected all other subjects like English, History, and Physiology.
• **Loss of Scholarship:** Due to his failure in non-mathematical subjects, he lost his scholarship, leading to a period of extreme poverty and wandering.
• **Pachaiyappa’s College:** He later attempted to study at Pachaiyappa’s College in Madras but again failed his exams, focusing solely on his independent mathematical research.
• **The "Notebooks" of Ramanujan:** During his years of unemployment, he recorded his mathematical discoveries in three large leather-bound notebooks, which are now legendary in the world of mathematics.
• **Independent Research:** Between 1906 and 1912, he lived in poverty, often on the verge of starvation, yet he continued to fill his notebooks with thousands of original theorems.
• **Marriage:** In 1909, he married Janakiammal, a young girl chosen by his mother, which increased his pressure to find stable employment to support his family.
• **Search for Patrons:** He began showing his notebooks to influential people in Madras, seeking a clerical job that would allow him enough free time to pursue mathematics.
• **V. Ramaswamy Aiyer:** The founder of the Indian Mathematical Society, Aiyer was one of the first to recognize Ramanujan's genius and helped him get his work published.

• **Journal of the Indian Mathematical Society:** Ramanujan’s first formal publication appeared in this journal in 1911, titled "Some Properties of Bernoulli's Numbers."
• **Madras Port Trust:** In 1912, Ramanujan secured a position as an accounting clerk at the Madras Port Trust, earning a small salary of 30 rupees per month.
• **Sir Francis Spring:** The Chairman of the Madras Port Trust, along with S. Narayana Iyer, encouraged Ramanujan to reach out to British mathematicians to validate his work.
• **Correspondence with British Academics:** Ramanujan initially wrote to several professors at University College London and Cambridge, but most ignored his letters or returned them without comment.
• **The Letter to G.H. Hardy:** In January 1913, Ramanujan wrote a historic letter to G.H. Hardy, a famous mathematician at Trinity College, Cambridge, containing 120 mathematical theorems.
• **Hardy’s Initial Reaction:** Hardy first thought the letter was a fraud or a "curiosity," but after studying the complex formulas with his colleague J.E. Littlewood, he realized they were written by a mathematician of the highest class.
• **Hardy’s Assessment:** Hardy famously remarked that some of Ramanujan's formulas "defeated me completely; I had never seen anything the least like them before."
• **Invitation to Cambridge:** Hardy immediately took steps to bring Ramanujan to England, recognizing that his talent was being wasted in isolation.
• **Religious Constraints:** Initially, Ramanujan refused to travel overseas due to religious beliefs (crossing the "black seas" was considered a taboo for orthodox Brahmins).
• **The Vision of Namagiri:** Ramanujan’s mother eventually gave her permission for his travel after claiming the goddess Namagiri appeared in a dream, commanding her not to stand in the way of her son’s mission.
• **Arrival in England (1914):** Ramanujan arrived in London in April 1914 and soon moved to Trinity College, Cambridge, to begin his collaboration with Hardy.
• **The Hardy-Ramanujan Collaboration:** This partnership is considered one of the most productive and famous in the history of mathematics, blending Ramanujan’s intuition with Hardy’s rigor.
• **Bridging the Gap:** Hardy faced the challenge of teaching Ramanujan modern mathematical "proofs" without stifling his natural creative flow and unique insights.
• **Dietary Challenges:** As a strict vegetarian, Ramanujan struggled to find proper food in England, especially during the food shortages of World War I, often cooking for himself.
• **Academic Recognition:** Despite lacking a formal undergraduate degree, Ramanujan was awarded a Bachelor of Arts by Research (later renamed a PhD) from Cambridge in 1916.
• **The Highly Composite Numbers:** His dissertation focused on "Highly Composite Numbers," which are integers with more divisors than any smaller positive integer.
• **Fellow of the Royal Society (1918):** Ramanujan became one of the youngest Fellows in the history of the Royal Society and the first Indian to be elected for his mathematical contributions.
• **Fellow of Trinity College:** In the same year, he was elected a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, becoming the first Indian to receive this prestigious honor.

• **Number Theory:** Ramanujan’s primary area of expertise was number theory, the branch of pure mathematics concerned with the properties and relationships of numbers.
• **Infinite Series for Pi (π):** Ramanujan developed several incredibly fast-converging infinite series for calculating the value of Pi, which are still used in modern computer algorithms.
• **Ramanujan’s Pi Formula:** One of his formulas for 1/π is so efficient that each term in the series adds roughly eight decimal places of accuracy to the calculation.
• **Partition Function p(n):** Ramanujan made groundbreaking discoveries regarding the number of ways a positive integer 'n' can be expressed as a sum of positive integers.
• **The Partition Formula:** Along with G.H. Hardy, he developed the "Hardy-Ramanujan Asymptotic Formula" to estimate the number of partitions for very large numbers.
• **Ramanujan’s Congruences:** He discovered striking properties of partitions, such as the fact that the number of partitions of an integer ending in 4 or 9 is always divisible by 5.
• **The Hardy-Ramanujan Number (1729):** This is the most famous anecdote involving Ramanujan; it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways.
• **The 1729 Story:** When Hardy visited Ramanujan in the hospital and mentioned his taxi number (1729) was "dull," Ramanujan instantly replied that it was a very interesting number: $1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 9^3 + 10^3$.
• **Taxicab Numbers:** Following the 1729 discovery, mathematicians defined "Taxicab numbers" as the smallest number that can be expressed as the sum of two cubes in 'n' distinct ways.
• **Mock Theta Functions:** In the final year of his life, Ramanujan discovered a new class of functions called "Mock Theta Functions," which he described in his last letter to Hardy.
• **Modern Use of Mock Theta Functions:** These functions, once a mystery, are now used in the study of black holes and entropy in modern physics.
• **Ramanujan Prime:** A specific type of prime number that relates to the prime-counting function, named in his honor.
• **Ramanujan Theta Function:** A function used to summarize many different types of theta functions into a single identity, widely used in mathematical physics.
• **Continued Fractions:** Ramanujan was a master of continued fractions, producing identities that were far more complex than anything previously known in Europe.
• **The Rogers-Ramanujan Identities:** These are two famous identities in the theory of partitions and continued fractions that Ramanujan rediscovered independently.
• **Ramanujan’s Sum:** A trigonometric sum that depends on the divisors of an integer, used extensively in the proof of Vinogradov's theorem.
• **Master Theorem:** A technique he developed for providing an analytic expression for the Mellin transform of a function.
• **Bernoulli Numbers:** Ramanujan’s early work on Bernoulli numbers provided new ways to calculate these sequences, which appear in many areas of mathematics.

• **Health Decline:** The cold climate of England, combined with his strict vegetarian diet and the stress of intense mathematical work, severely affected Ramanujan’s health.
• **Tuberculosis Diagnosis:** He was diagnosed with tuberculosis and severe vitamin deficiency (specifically Vitamin B12) during his stay in England.
• **Hepatic Amoebiasis:** Modern medical analysis suggests that Ramanujan might have actually suffered from hepatic amoebiasis, a parasitic liver infection common in India at the time.
• **Hospitalization in England:** He spent a significant portion of his final years in England in various nursing homes and sanatoriums, often feeling lonely and isolated.
• **Return to India (1919):** Due to his failing health, Ramanujan returned to India in 1919, hoping the warmer climate and familiar food would help him recover.
• **Final Year of Research:** Even while bedridden and in great pain during his final year in Madras, he continued to produce high-level mathematics at a frantic pace.
• **The "Lost Notebook":** Upon his death, a final notebook containing 138 pages of formulas was left behind; it was "lost" for decades before being rediscovered in 1976.
• **George Andrews:** The mathematician who rediscovered the "Lost Notebook" in the Trinity College library, sparking a new wave of research into Ramanujan’s final thoughts.
• **Death (1920):** Srinivasa Ramanujan passed away on April 26, 1920, at the young age of 32, in Chetput, Madras.
• **Janakiammal’s Legacy:** His wife, Janakiammal, lived until 1994 and spent much of her life preserving his memory and supporting the education of young mathematicians.
• **National Mathematics Day:** In 2012, Prime Minister Manmohan Singh declared December 22nd (Ramanujan’s birthday) as National Mathematics Day in India.
• **Year of Mathematics:** The year 2012 was also celebrated as the National Mathematics Year in India to mark the 125th anniversary of his birth.
• **Ramanujan IT City:** A major information technology business park in Chennai is named "Ramanujan IT City" in his honor.
• **SASTRA Ramanujan Prize:** An annual award given to young mathematicians (under age 32) who have made outstanding contributions to areas influenced by Ramanujan.
• **ICTP Ramanujan Prize:** A prestigious award given to mathematicians from developing countries by the International Centre for Theoretical Physics.
• **The Ramanujan Journal:** A peer-reviewed scientific journal established in 1997 to publish work in all areas of mathematics influenced by Ramanujan.
• **Stamp Commemoration:** The Government of India has issued several commemorative postage stamps featuring Ramanujan’s portrait to honor his global impact.
• **Museum in Kumbakonam:** His childhood home in Kumbakonam has been preserved as a museum and a monument by the SASTRA University.

• **Mathematical Style:** Ramanujan’s style was unique because he often stated results without providing the intermediate steps or proofs, which he saw intuitively.
• **The Role of Intuition:** He believed that a mathematical formula had no meaning unless it expressed a "thought of God," highlighting his spiritual approach to science.
• **Hardy’s Scale of Genius:** G.H. Hardy created a personal scale of mathematical genius from 0 to 100; he gave himself a 25, Littlewood a 30, Hilbert an 80, and Ramanujan a 100.
• **Impact on String Theory:** Some of Ramanujan’s functions, particularly those involving modular forms, are essential in the development of String Theory in theoretical physics.
• **Crystallography:** His work on certain mathematical series has found applications in the study of crystals and their atomic structures.
• **Computer Science Applications:** Ramanujan’s algorithms for Pi are the basis for many modern computer programs used to test the processing power of supercomputers.
• **The Man Who Knew Infinity:** This is the title of the famous biography of Ramanujan written by Robert Kanigel, which was later adapted into a major motion picture.
• **Dev Patel’s Portrayal:** In the 2015 film "The Man Who Knew Infinity," actor Dev Patel played the role of Ramanujan, bringing his story to a global audience.
• **Ramanujan’s Influence on Indian Education:** His life story serves as a primary inspiration for the DAV Board and other educational institutions to promote STEM education in India.
• **The Concept of "Ramanujan’s Lost Notebook":** This notebook contained over 600 formulas that he wrote during the last year of his life, many of which took mathematicians decades to prove.
• **Modular Forms:** Ramanujan was a pioneer in the study of modular forms, which are complex functions that possess a high degree of symmetry.
• **Ramanujan Conjecture:** A famous conjecture he made regarding the size of the tau-function, which was finally proven in 1974 by Pierre Deligne (who won a Fields Medal for it).
• **Circle Method:** A technique developed by Hardy and Ramanujan to solve problems in additive number theory, such as Waring's problem.
• **Highly Composite Numbers Paper:** This paper was over 50 pages long and is considered one of the most significant works in the history of the London Mathematical Society.
• **Ramanujan’s Religious Identity:** He was a devout Hindu and attributed his mathematical discoveries to his faith, often saying "An equation for me has no meaning unless it represents a thought of God."
• **The "Genius" Label:** Ramanujan is often compared to other "natural geniuses" like Leonhard Euler and Carl Friedrich Gauss due to his innate ability to see patterns in numbers.
• **Global Legacy:** Today, Ramanujan is recognized not just as an Indian hero, but as one of the greatest mathematical minds in human history.
• **The DAV Perspective:** For Class 7 students, Ramanujan represents the power of perseverance, self-study, and the ability to achieve greatness despite limited resources.

• **Early Mathematical Interests:** Even before he had access to advanced books, Ramanujan would ask his teachers about "the limits of the universe" and the properties of zero.
• **Zero and Infinity:** He was fascinated by the concept of zero divided by zero, arguing that it could represent anything, which showed his early philosophical approach to math.
• **The Town Higher Secondary School:** Here, Ramanujan was recognized as a "prodigy" by his teachers, and he often helped senior students with their college-level math problems.
• **Self-Correction:** Ramanujan often corrected his own formulas in his notebooks as his understanding of mathematical rigor improved through his correspondence with Hardy.
• **The "Synopsis" Method:** Because Carr’s book only provided results and not proofs, Ramanujan grew up believing that "proof" was simply a matter of verifying that a formula worked.
• **The First Letter’s Content:** The 1913 letter to Hardy included formulas for infinite series, improper integrals, and the distribution of prime numbers.
• **Hardy’s Collaboration Style:** Hardy provided the "European" rigor of proof, while Ramanujan provided the "Indian" intuition and creative spark.
• **The Trinity College Atmosphere:** Ramanujan was initially a lonely figure at Cambridge, often seen walking in his traditional Indian attire before switching to Western clothes.
• **The Impact of World War I:** The war broke out shortly after Ramanujan arrived in England, making it difficult for him to receive supplies from India and affecting his mental well-being.
• **Ramanujan’s Work Ethic:** He was known to work for 24 to 30 hours at a stretch, then sleep for 12 hours, ignoring regular meal times and sleep cycles.
• **The "Ramanujan Graph":** In graph theory, a Ramanujan graph is a regular graph whose spectral gap is as large as possible; these are used in communication network design.
• **The Tau Function:** Ramanujan defined the $\tau(n)$ function, which has deep connections to modular forms and is a central object in modern number theory.
• **Ramanujan’s Constant:** The number $e^{\pi \sqrt{163}}$ is sometimes called Ramanujan's constant, though he did not discover it; he noted its remarkable property of being very close to an integer.
• **The Partition of 200:** Ramanujan and Hardy calculated that there are 3,972,999,029,388 ways to partition the number 200, a feat that showcased their formula's power.
• **The "Lost Notebook" Rediscovery:** When George Andrews found the notebook in 1976, he described it as "a discovery that would be like finding a new sketch-book of Beethoven."
• **Ramanujan’s Humility:** Despite his fame in England, he remained a humble man, always crediting his success to his family and his faith.
• **The "Kid Wiz" Focus:** For General Knowledge purposes, students should remember his birth date, his most famous number (1729), and his mentor (G.H. Hardy).
• **The Symbol of Indian Intellectual Power:** Ramanujan’s success at Cambridge during the colonial era was a source of great pride for the Indian independence movement.

• **The Concept of Partitions:** A partition of a positive integer 'n' is a way of writing 'n' as a sum of positive integers. For example, the partitions of 4 are: 4, 3+1, 2+2, 2+1+1, and 1+1+1+1.
• **Growth of Partitions:** Ramanujan was fascinated by how quickly the number of partitions grows as 'n' increases (e.g., $p(10)=42$, but $p(100)=190,569,292$).
• **Ramanujan’s Congruence for 5:** He proved that $p(5n + 4)$ is always a multiple of 5.
• **Ramanujan’s Congruence for 7:** He proved that $p(7n + 5)$ is always a multiple of 7.
• **Ramanujan’s Congruence for 11:** He proved that $p(11n + 6)$ is always a multiple of 11.
• **The Hardy-Ramanujan-Rademacher Series:** This is the perfected version of the partition formula that gives the exact value of $p(n)$ for any integer.
• **The Circle Method Explained:** This method involves using complex analysis to "circle" a singularity in a power series to extract coefficients (like partition numbers).
• **Waring’s Problem:** Ramanujan contributed to this problem, which asks whether every natural number 'n' has a maximum number of powers of integers that sum up to it.
• **The Mock Theta Mystery:** For 80 years, mathematicians didn't know what "Mock Theta Functions" were; they were finally defined as the holomorphic parts of harmonic weak Maass forms.
• **Black Hole Entropy:** In 2010, physicists discovered that Ramanujan’s mock theta functions help explain the entropy of black holes in string theory.
• **Ramanujan’s Summation:** A method he used to assign a value to divergent series, such as $1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ... = -1/12$ (in a specific mathematical context).
• **The Riemann Zeta Function:** Ramanujan independently discovered many properties of the Zeta function, which is central to the "Riemann Hypothesis," the most famous unsolved problem in math.
• **Gamma Function:** He developed new identities for the Gamma function, which extends the concept of factorials to complex and real numbers.
• **Elliptic Functions:** Ramanujan’s work on elliptic functions was decades ahead of his time and provided the foundation for much of 20th-century analysis.
• **The Master Theorem Application:** It is used to calculate definite integrals that are otherwise very difficult to solve.
• **Ramanujan’s Class Invariants:** He calculated values for certain algebraic numbers that were incredibly precise and complex.
• **The "Lost Notebook" Content:** It contains over 600 formulas on q-series, mock theta functions, and modular equations.
• **Mathematical Beauty:** Ramanujan’s work is often cited as the pinnacle of "mathematical beauty" because of the unexpected connections he found between different areas of math.
