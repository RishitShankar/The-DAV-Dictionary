• **Chapter Introduction:** Welcome to The DAV Dictionary Academic Notes for Class 7 Science, specifically covering Chapter 12, "Light," designed in accordance with the Dayanand Anglo-Vedic Public School Board curriculum.
• **Definition of Light:** Light is defined as a form of invisible electromagnetic energy that produces the sensation of vision upon entering our eyes, allowing us to perceive the physical world around us.
• **Nature of Light:** Light travels in the form of electromagnetic waves, which do not require any material medium for propagation, meaning light can travel freely through the absolute vacuum of outer space.
• **Luminous Objects:** Objects that possess their own internal source of light and emit it independently are known as luminous objects, such as the Sun, stars, burning candles, and glowing electric lightbulbs.
• **Non-Luminous Objects:** Objects that do not produce their own light are classified as non-luminous objects; they are visible to human eyes only because they reflect light falling on them from external luminous sources.
• **Rectilinear Propagation of Light:** A fundamental property of light is that it always travels in a straight line path through a homogeneous and transparent medium, a phenomenon scientifically termed the rectilinear propagation of light.
• **Ray of Light:** A single straight line path along which light energy travels is represented diagrammatically as a ray of light, indicated with an arrowhead showing the exact direction of travel.
• **Beam of Light:** A collection, bundle, or group of numerous light rays traveling together in a specific direction is known as a beam of light.
• **Parallel Beam:** A beam of light in which all individual light rays run parallel to one another, typically emitted by distant sources like the Sun or lasers, is called a parallel beam of light.
• **Convergent Beam:** A beam of light in which rays originating from a wider area come closer together and meet at a single point (focus) is defined as a convergent beam of light.
• **Divergent Beam:** A beam of light in which rays start from a single point source and spread out in various directions, increasing the distance between them, is called a divergent beam of light.
• **Transparent Materials:** Materials that allow almost all incident light to pass straight through them without scattering are called transparent materials, such as pure water, clean glass, and clear air.
• **Translucent Materials:** Materials that allow only a fraction of incident light to pass through them while scattering the rest, rendering objects on the other side blurry, are termed translucent materials like frosted glass and waxed paper.
• **Opaque Materials:** Materials that completely block the passage of light and allow zero light transmission are called opaque materials, including wood, metals, thick stone, and cardboard.
• **Shadow Formation:** Shadows are dark, patch-like areas formed behind opaque objects when they obstruct the path of light traveling in straight lines, proving the rectilinear propagation of light.

• **Essential Requirements for Shadows:** The formation of a distinct shadow requires three indispensable components: a source of light, an opaque object to obstruct the light, and an opaque screen on which the shadow is cast.
• **Natural Screens:** Shadows can be cast on natural screens present in our environment, such as the ground, walls of buildings, or the surface of water bodies.
• **Characteristics of Shadows - Color:** The color of a shadow is always black or dark gray, regardless of the actual bright color or physical appearance of the opaque object casting it.
• **Characteristics of Shadows - Details:** Shadows never show any internal structural details, surface features, or colors of the object; they only outline the outer external shape and general contour of the object.
• **Dependence on Distance:** The size and sharpness of a shadow depend heavily on the relative distances between the light source, the blocking object, and the surface of the screen.
• **Effect of Object-Screen Distance:** When an opaque object is moved closer to the screen while keeping the light source fixed, the size of the cast shadow decreases and becomes sharper.
• **Effect of Light Source Distance:** When the source of light is brought closer to the opaque object, the size of the resulting shadow increases significantly due to greater divergence of light rays.
• **Umbra:** The central, innermost region of a shadow where complete darkness prevails because all direct light rays from the source are completely blocked by the opaque object is called the umbra.
• **Penumbra:** The lighter, outer fringe or partial shadow surrounding the umbra, where only a portion of the light rays are blocked while others pass through, is called the penumbra.
• **Pinhole Camera:** A simple optical device without any lenses, based entirely on the rectilinear propagation of light, used to observe the formation of real, inverted images of luminous objects.
• **Structure of Pinhole Camera:** A classic pinhole camera consists of two concentric boxes sliding into each other, where one box has a tiny pinhole at the front and the other has a translucent grease-paper screen at the back.
• **Image Formation in Pinhole Camera:** Light rays from the top of an object pass through the tiny pinhole and travel straight to the bottom of the back screen, while rays from the bottom go to the top, causing inversion.
• **Characteristics of Pinhole Image:** The image formed inside a pinhole camera is always real, inverted (upside down), and generally smaller in size compared to the actual object being viewed.
• **Natural Pinhole Cameras:** Under large leafy trees on sunny days, the small gaps between leaves act as natural pinholes, casting numerous circular, inverted images of the Sun on the ground below during eclipses.
• **Reflection of Light:** The phenomenon in which a ray of light bounces back into the same medium after striking a polished, smooth, and shiny boundary surface is known as reflection of light.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Mirrors as Reflectors:** A plane mirror is a flat, highly polished glass surface coated with a thin layer of reflective metal (like silver or aluminum) backed by protective paint, serving as an ideal reflector of light.
• **Incident Ray:** The specific ray of light that travels through the medium and strikes the reflecting surface of a mirror is mathematically and scientifically termed the incident ray.
• **Point of Incidence:** The exact point on the mirror's reflecting surface where the incident ray strikes is known as the point of incidence.
• **Reflected Ray:** The ray of light that bounces off the reflecting surface and travels back into the original medium from the point of incidence is called the reflected ray.
• **Normal:** An imaginary geometric line drawn perpendicular (at a 90-degree angle) to the reflecting surface at the exact point of incidence is referred to as the normal.
• **Angle of Incidence (i):** The acute angle formed between the incident ray and the normal at the point of incidence is designated as the angle of incidence, denoted by the symbol 'i'.
• **Angle of Reflection (r):** The acute angle formed between the reflected ray and the normal at the point of incidence is designated as the angle of reflection, denoted by the symbol 'r'.
• **First Law of Reflection:** The primary law of reflection states that the angle of incidence is always precisely equal to the angle of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$) under all conditions.
• **Second Law of Reflection:** The secondary law of reflection states that the incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in one and the same plane.
• **Regular Reflection (Specular Reflection):** Reflection of light from extremely smooth, polished surfaces (like plane mirrors or calm water) where parallel incident rays are reflected as a parallel beam in a definite direction is regular reflection.
• **Diffuse Reflection (Irregular Reflection):** Reflection of light from rough, uneven surfaces (like unpolished wood, paper, or walls) where parallel incident rays are scattered in various random directions is called diffuse reflection.
• **Visibility of Objects:** Diffuse reflection is immensely important in daily life because it enables us to see non-luminous objects from all possible viewing angles since light is scattered in every direction.
• **Image Formation by Plane Mirror:** A plane mirror forms an image of an object placed in front of it due to the regular reflection of light rays entering our eyes from the mirror surface.
• **Characteristics of Plane Mirror Image - Virtual:** The image formed by a plane mirror is virtual, meaning it cannot be captured or received on a physical screen because light rays do not actually meet at the image location.
• **Characteristics of Plane Mirror Image - Erect:** The image formed by a plane mirror is always erect or upright, meaning if the top of the object points upward, the top of the image also points upward.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Characteristics of Plane Mirror Image - Lateral Inversion:** The image formed by a plane mirror exhibits lateral inversion, a phenomenon where the right side of the object appears as the left side of the image and vice versa.
• **Characteristics of Plane Mirror Image - Distance:** In a plane mirror, the perpendicular distance of the image behind the mirror is mathematically equal to the perpendicular distance of the object in front of the mirror.
• **Characteristics of Plane Mirror Image - Size:** The size of the image formed by a plane mirror is always identically equal to the actual size of the object placed in front of it.
• **Ambulance Lettering:** The word 'AMBULANCE' is painted in front of emergency vehicles in a laterally inverted form so that drivers of vehicles ahead can read it correctly in their rearview mirrors and yield right of way.
• **Spherical Mirrors:** Mirrors whose reflecting surfaces are curved inward or outward, representing a small section of a hollow glass sphere, are classified as spherical mirrors.
• **Concave Mirror:** A spherical mirror whose inner hollow curved surface acts as the reflecting surface is known as a concave mirror (converging mirror).
• **Convex Mirror:** A spherical mirror whose outer bulging curved surface acts as the reflecting surface is known as a convex mirror (diverging mirror).
• **Pole (P) of Spherical Mirror:** The geometric center point of the curved reflecting surface of a spherical mirror is defined as the pole of the mirror.
• **Center of Curvature (C):** The center of the imaginary hollow glass sphere of which the spherical mirror forms a distinct part is termed the center of curvature.
• **Radius of Curvature (R):** The radius of the imaginary sphere of which the spherical mirror is a part, representing the distance between the pole and the center of curvature, is the radius of curvature.
• **Principal Axis:** The straight imaginary line passing directly through the pole and the center of curvature of a spherical mirror is called the principal axis.
• **Principal Focus (F) of Concave Mirror:** The point on the principal axis where parallel rays of light converge after suffering reflection from a concave mirror is called its principal focus.
• **Principal Focus (F) of Convex Mirror:** The point on the principal axis from which parallel rays of light appear to diverge after reflection from a convex mirror is called its virtual principal focus.
• **Focal Length (f):** The shortest distance measured along the principal axis between the pole of the mirror and its principal focus is defined as the focal length of the spherical mirror.
• **Relation Between R and f:** For any spherical mirror of small aperture, the radius of curvature (R) is always twice the focal length of the mirror ($R = 2f$ or $f = R/2$).
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Image Formation by Concave Mirrors:** Concave mirrors are capable of forming both real and virtual images, as well as images that are magnified, diminished, or equal in size, depending on the object's position.
• **Object at Infinity (Concave):** When an object is placed at a very large (infinite) distance from a concave mirror, a real, inverted, highly diminished, point-sized image is formed right at the principal focus.
• **Object Beyond C (Concave):** When an object is placed beyond the center of curvature (C), the concave mirror forms a real, inverted, and diminished image located between the focus (F) and the center of curvature (C).
• **Object at C (Concave):** When an object is placed exactly at the center of curvature (C) of a concave mirror, a real, inverted image of the exact same size as the object is formed at C.
• **Object Between C and F (Concave):** When an object is positioned between the center of curvature and the principal focus, a real, inverted, and magnified (enlarged) image is formed beyond the center of curvature.
• **Object at F (Concave):** When an object is placed precisely at the principal focus of a concave mirror, the reflected rays become parallel and meet at infinity, forming a real, highly magnified image.
• **Object Between P and F (Concave):** When an object is placed between the pole and the principal focus of a concave mirror, it forms a virtual, erect, and magnified image behind the mirror.
• **Uses of Concave Mirrors - Shaving/Makeup:** Concave mirrors are widely used as shaving and makeup mirrors because they form an erect and magnified image of the face when held close.
• **Uses of Concave Mirrors - Dentists:** Dentists use small concave mirrors to examine cavities and teeth closely because the mirror forms an enlarged, clear image of the teeth.
• **Uses of Concave Mirrors - Headlights:** Concave mirrors are used in the reflectors of automobile headlights, torchlights, and searchlights to produce a powerful, parallel beam of intense light rays.
• **Uses of Concave Mirrors - Solar Furnaces:** Large concave mirrors are utilized in solar cookers and solar furnaces to collect and concentrate a large amount of sunlight onto a focal point to generate intense heat.
• **Image Formation by Convex Mirrors:** Convex mirrors always form virtual, erect, and diminished (smaller than the object) images regardless of the distance of the object from the mirror.
• **Field of View of Convex Mirrors:** Convex mirrors have a much wider field of view compared to plane mirrors of the same size because they curve outward, allowing them to cover a large area.
• **Uses of Convex Mirrors - Rearview Mirrors:** Convex mirrors are universally used as rearview or side-view mirrors in cars, scooters, and buses so drivers can see a wide view of traffic behind them safely.
• **Uses of Convex Mirrors - Security/Shop Mirrors:** Convex mirrors are installed at sharp blind turns on mountain roads and inside large department stores as security mirrors to prevent accidents and monitor aisles.

• **Sunlight Composition:** Sunlight, which appears white to the naked eye, is actually a complex mixture of seven distinct visible colors blended together, known as white light.
• **Spectrum of Colors:** The seven constituent colors present in white light always appear in a definite, unchanging sequence remembered by the acronym VIBGYOR.
• **VIBGYOR Breakdown:** The letters in VIBGYOR stand for Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red, representing the complete spectrum of visible light.
• **Dispersion of Light:** The phenomenon of splitting of a beam of white light into its seven constituent component colors when it passes through a transparent medium like a glass prism is called dispersion.
• **Cause of Dispersion:** Different colors of light travel at slightly different speeds through a refractive medium like glass, causing them to bend (refract) by different angles, thus separating them out.
• **Newton's Prism Experiment:** Sir Isaac Newton was the first scientist to demonstrate using a glass prism that white light is composed of seven colors, and he also proved using a second inverted prism that the colors could be recombined into white light.
• **Rainbow Formation:** A natural phenomenon of dispersion of sunlight by countless tiny water droplets suspended in the atmosphere after a rain shower results in the magnificent display of a rainbow in the sky.
• **Role of Water Droplets:** Each spherical water droplet suspended in the air acts like a tiny miniature glass prism that refracts, internally reflects, and disperses incoming sunlight into its component colors.
• **Viewing a Rainbow:** To observe a natural rainbow in the sky, the observer must always stand with their back facing directly toward the Sun while looking at the water droplets suspended in the opposite direction.
• **Newton's Disc Experiment:** Sir Isaac Newton demonstrated the synthesis of white light by painting a cardboard disc with the seven rainbow colors in proper proportions and rotating it rapidly on a spindle.
• **Color Blending on Rotation:** When Newton's color disc is rotated at a high speed, individual colors blur together due to the persistence of vision of the human eye, and the spinning disc appears whitish-grey.
• **Persistence of Vision:** The physiological phenomenon by which an impression of an image or color persists on the retina of the human eye for about one-sixteenth (1/16th) of a second after the actual visual stimulus has ceased.
• **Primary Colors of Light:** The primary colors of light are Red, Green, and Blue; mixing these three primary colored lights in varying intensities can produce almost any color, including white light.
• **Secondary Colors of Light:** Colors formed by the additive combination of any two primary colors of light, such as Cyan, Magenta, and Yellow, are known as secondary colors of light.
• **Optics History:** The study of light and its properties, known as optics, has evolved from ancient philosophical speculations to modern quantum physics, significantly shaping human technological advancement.

• **Historical Milestone - Ibn al-Haytham:** The pioneering medieval Arab scientist Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) revolutionized optics in the 11th century by proving that light travels from objects into the eyes, rather than originating from the eyes.
• **Book of Optics (Kitab al-Manazir):** Ibn al-Haytham authored the monumental treatise *Kitab al-Manazir*, which laid down the experimental foundations of modern optics, reflection, refraction, and camera obscura principles.
• **Refraction Introduction:** Although DAV Class 7 focuses primarily on reflection and rectilinear propagation, refraction is the bending of light as it passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another of different optical density.
• **Optical Density:** The property of a transparent medium that determines the speed of light through it is called optical density; denser media slow down light more than rarer media.
• **Bending Toward Normal:** When a ray of light travels from an optically rarer medium (like air) into an optically denser medium (like water or glass), it bends toward the imaginary normal line.
• **Bending Away from Normal:** When a ray of light travels from an optically denser medium (like glass) into an optically rarer medium (like air), it bends away from the imaginary normal line.
• **Twinkling of Stars:** The phenomenon of twinkling of stars in the night sky is caused by the continuous, rapid refraction of starlight through fluctuating layers of Earth's turbulent atmosphere.
• **Apparent Depth of Water:** A swimming pool or a pond containing clear water always appears to be shallower than its actual physical depth due to the upward refraction of light rays coming from the bottom.
• **Bending of Pencil in Water:** A straight pencil or wooden stick partially immersed in a glass of water appears bent or broken at the water-air interface because of the refraction of light rays.
• **Scattering of Light:** The phenomenon in which light rays are deflected in various random directions when they strike tiny dust particles, gas molecules, or smoke droplets in the atmosphere is called scattering.
• **Blue Color of Sky:** The sky appears blue during daylight hours because the Earth's atmosphere scatters shorter wavelengths of light (blue and violet) much more strongly than longer wavelengths (red and orange) in a process known as Rayleigh scattering.
• **Red Color of Sunset/Sunrise:** During sunrise and sunset, sunlight has to travel a much longer path through the atmosphere, during which most of the blue light is scattered away, leaving the unscattered red and orange light to reach our eyes.
• **Danger Signals:** Red color is universally used for danger signals, railway signals, and stoplights because it has the longest wavelength among visible colors and is least scattered by fog, dust, or smoke, making it visible from great distances.
• **Clouds Appearance:** Clouds appear white because water droplets and ice crystals in clouds are much larger than the wavelength of light, causing all colors of sunlight to scatter equally (Mie scattering).
• **Laser Light Properties:** A laser (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) produces an intense, highly directional, monochromatic (single color) beam of coherent light unlike ordinary light sources.

• **Lenses Introduction:** Although mirrors reflect light, transparent curved devices called lenses refract light to form images, finding extensive use in spectacles, microscopes, telescopes, and cameras.
• **Convex Lens (Converging Lens):** A lens that is thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges is called a convex lens; it bends parallel rays of light inward, converging them to a point focus.
• **Concave Lens (Diverging Lens):** A lens that is thinner in the middle and thicker at the edges is called a concave lens; it bends parallel rays of light outward, causing them to diverge.
• **Optical Center of Lens:** The central geometric point of a lens through which any ray of light passes without suffering any deviation or bending is called the optical center.
• **Principal Focus of Convex Lens:** The point on the principal axis where parallel incident light rays actually converge after passing through a convex lens is its real principal focus.
• **Principal Focus of Concave Lens:** The point on the principal axis from which parallel incident rays appear to diverge after passing through a concave lens is its virtual principal focus.
• **Uses of Convex Lenses:** Convex lenses are used in reading glasses for long-sightedness (hypermetropia), magnifying glasses, simple microscopes, cameras, and movie projectors.
• **Uses of Concave Lenses:** Concave lenses are used in spectacles to correct short-sightedness (myopia), in peepholes of doors, and in combination with convex lenses in high-powered telescopes.
• **Human Eye as Optical Instrument:** The human eye is a natural optical instrument equipped with a living natural convex lens made of transparent proteins, capable of adjusting its focal length to view objects at varying distances.
• **Cornea:** The transparent, dome-shaped outer covering at the front of the eye through which light enters is called the cornea, which performs most of the light refraction.
• **Iris and Pupil:** The colored circular muscular diaphragm behind the cornea is the iris, which controls the size of the central aperture called the pupil to regulate the amount of light entering the eye.
• **Retina:** The delicate, light-sensitive inner membrane at the back of the eyeball containing millions of photoreceptor cells (rods and cones) where real and inverted images are formed is the retina.
• **Rod Cells:** Photoreceptor cells in the retina that are sensitive to dim light and help us see in the dark (black and white vision) are called rod cells.
• **Cone Cells:** Photoreceptor cells in the retina that are sensitive to bright light and enable color vision and fine detail perception are called cone cells.
• **Optical Nerve:** The nerve that transmits electrical impulses from the light-sensitive cells of the retina to the visual processing center of the brain is the optical nerve.

• **Optical Illusions:** Optical illusions are visual phenomena where our brain misinterprets visual information received from our eyes, often highlighting the limitations or complex processing of human vision in relation to light.
• **Mirage Formation:** A mirage is an optical illusion observed in deserts or on hot asphalt roads during summer, where layers of air at different temperatures refract light, making distant objects appear mirrored on a pool of water that does not exist.
• **Shadow Puppetry:** An ancient traditional art form utilizing light, opaque puppets, and a white screen to tell stories through the projection of moving shadows, demonstrating the principles of rectilinear propagation.
• **Optical Fibers:** Modern communication technology relies on hair-thin flexible glass or plastic fibers called optical fibers, which transmit light signals over vast distances without loss through continuous total internal reflection.
• **Endoscopy in Medicine:** Doctors use medical instruments called endoscopes, equipped with optical fibers and tiny lenses, to peer inside the human digestive tract and internal organs using light transmission.
• **Solar Eclipse:** A celestial phenomenon where the Moon comes directly between the Sun and the Earth, casting its shadow (umbra and penumbra) on the Earth's surface, temporarily blocking sunlight.
• **Lunar Eclipse:** A celestial phenomenon where the Earth comes directly between the Sun and the Moon, casting its vast shadow onto the lunar surface during a full moon night.
• **Shadow Sharpness and Umbra Size:** During a solar eclipse, observers standing in the umbra experience a total solar eclipse, while those in the penumbra experience a partial solar eclipse.
• **Bioluminescence:** A fascinating biological phenomenon where living organisms such as fireflies, deep-sea anglerfish, and certain fungi produce cold light internally through specialized chemical reactions.
• **Fluorescence and Phosphorescence:** Special substances absorb invisible ultraviolet light and instantly re-emit it as visible light (fluorescence) or continue glowing long after the light source is removed (phosphorescence).
• **Ultraviolet and Infrared Rays:** Beyond the visible spectrum of VIBGYOR, sunlight contains invisible electromagnetic radiations such as ultraviolet (UV) rays (shorter than violet) and infrared (IR) rays (longer than red).
• **Greenhouse Effect:** Infrared rays from the Sun pass through the Earth's atmosphere and warm the planet; greenhouse gases trap this outgoing thermal radiation, maintaining Earth's habitable average temperature.
• **Photoperiodism:** The physiological reaction of organisms, particularly flowering plants, to the length of day or night (photoperiod), showing how light regulates biological cycles in nature.
• **Photosynthesis:** The vital biochemical process carried out by green plants using solar energy absorbed by chlorophyll to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, sustaining all life on Earth.
• **Chapter Summary:** Chapter 12 of DAV Class 7 Science thoroughly establishes that light is an energy form traveling in straight lines, undergoing reflection, refraction, dispersion, and shadow formation, acting as the fundamental key to vision and life on Earth.

• **Light Ray Reversibility:** The principle of reversibility of light states that if the direction of a light ray is reversed after undergoing multiple reflections or refractions, it will retrace its exact original path backward.
• **Specular vs Diffuse Reflection Summary:** Regular reflection creates clear, distinct mirror images, whereas diffuse reflection scatters light uniformly, making non-luminous objects visible from any viewing angle in a room.
• **Angle Geometry in Mirrors:** For any plane mirror reflection, the sum of the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection ($\angle i + \angle r$) equals twice the angle of incidence, since $\angle i = \angle r$.
• **Periscope Working Principle:** A periscope is an optical device used to see over obstacles (like in submarines or trenches), working on the principle of multiple reflections of light between two parallel plane mirrors inclined at 45 degrees.
• **Kaleidoscope Optics:** A kaleidoscope is a cylindrical toy containing inclined plane mirrors (usually three at 60 degrees) and colored glass pieces that creates beautiful, symmetrical repeating patterns through multiple reflections.
• **Number of Images Formula:** When two plane mirrors are placed at an angle $\theta$ to each other, the number of images ($n$) formed of an object placed between them is given by $n = (360^\circ / \theta) - 1$ (if $360/\theta$ is even).
• **Sunlight Travel Time:** Although light travels at an incredible vacuum speed of approximately $3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$ ($300,000 \text{ km/s}$), sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to travel the 150 million kilometers from the Sun to Earth.
• **Shadow Edges (Umbra-Penumbra Transition):** The sharpness of shadows depends on the size of the light source; a point light source creates a sharp umbra with no penumbra, while an extended light source creates both umbra and penumbra.
