• **Definition of a Sentence**: A sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense and expresses a complete thought.
• **Structural Components**: Every complete sentence is fundamentally composed of two essential parts: the Subject and the Predicate.
• **The Subject**: The subject is the part of the sentence that names the person, place, animal, or thing we are talking about.
• **The Predicate**: The predicate is the part of the sentence that tells us something about the subject.
• **Core Function**: The subject acts as the "doer" or the "topic," while the predicate acts as the "action" or "description."
• **Example Analysis**: In the sentence "The dog barked," "The dog" is the subject, and "barked" is the predicate.
• **Sentence Completeness**: A group of words cannot be classified as a sentence unless it contains both a subject and a predicate.
• **Grammatical Agreement**: The subject and predicate must agree in number and person to maintain grammatical accuracy.
• **The Subject's Position**: In most standard English sentences, the subject usually appears at the beginning of the sentence.
• **The Predicate's Position**: The predicate typically follows the subject and contains the main verb of the sentence.
• **Identifying the Subject**: To find the subject, ask the question "Who?" or "What?" before the verb.
• **Identifying the Predicate**: To find the predicate, look for the verb and everything that follows it in the sentence.
• **Noun as Subject**: A subject is most commonly a noun or a pronoun.
• **Verb as Predicate**: A predicate must always contain at least one verb, which is the action or state of being.
• **Simple Subject**: The simple subject is the main noun or pronoun without any modifiers.
• **Complete Subject**: The complete subject includes the simple subject plus all the words that modify it.
• **Simple Predicate**: The simple predicate is the main verb or verb phrase of the sentence.
• **Complete Predicate**: The complete predicate includes the verb and all the words that complete its meaning.

• **Subject Complexity**: A subject can consist of a single word or a group of words acting as a noun phrase.
• **Predicate Complexity**: A predicate can be a single verb or a complex phrase including objects, adjectives, and adverbs.
• **Action Verbs**: Many predicates contain action verbs that describe what the subject is doing (e.g., "The bird flies").
• **Linking Verbs**: Some predicates use linking verbs to connect the subject to a description (e.g., "The sky is blue").
• **Subject-Verb Relationship**: The predicate provides the necessary information about the subject's state or action.
• **Sentence Variety**: Sentences vary in length, but the division between subject and predicate remains constant.
• **The "Who" Test**: If you are unsure of the subject, identify the verb and ask "Who is performing this action?"
• **The "What" Test**: If the subject is an object, ask "What is being discussed?" to isolate the subject.
• **Predicate Scope**: The predicate encompasses everything in the sentence that is not the subject.
• **Noun Phrases**: Often, the subject is a noun phrase, such as "The hungry little cat."
• **Verb Phrases**: The predicate often contains a verb phrase, such as "is sleeping on the mat."
• **Subjective Pronouns**: Pronouns like I, you, he, she, it, we, and they often function as subjects.
• **Predicate Objects**: Many predicates include direct or indirect objects that receive the action of the verb.
• **Adverbial Modifiers**: Predicates frequently include adverbs that tell us how, when, or where the action happened.
• **Adjectival Modifiers**: Subjects often include adjectives that describe the noun, such as "The tall tree."
• **Prepositional Phrases**: These can appear in either the subject or the predicate to provide extra detail.
• **Sentence Logic**: Understanding the split between subject and predicate is the foundation of syntax.
• **Clarity in Writing**: Proper identification of these parts ensures that sentences are structured logically and clearly.

• **Compound Predicates**: A sentence can have more than one verb or action for the same subject (e.g., "She sang and danced").
• **Subject-Predicate Balance**: A well-formed sentence maintains a balance between the subject's identity and the predicate's information.
• **The Role of the Verb**: The verb is the heart of the predicate; without it, the predicate cannot exist.
• **Subjective Case**: The subject must be in the subjective case (e.g., "He" instead of "Him" as the subject).
• **Predicate Agreement**: The verb in the predicate must match the subject in number (singular vs. plural).
• **Singular Subjects**: A singular subject requires a singular verb form in the predicate (e.g., "The boy runs").
• **Plural Subjects**: A plural subject requires a plural verb form in the predicate (e.g., "The boys run").
• **Inverted Sentences**: Sometimes the subject comes after the predicate for emphasis (e.g., "Down the hill rolled the ball").
• **Identifying Inverted Subjects**: In inverted sentences, the subject is still the entity performing the action.
• **Imperative Sentences**: In commands, the subject "you" is often hidden or "understood."
• **Understood Subjects**: In the sentence "Close the door," the subject is "You" (implied).
• **Predicate in Commands**: In "Close the door," the entire sentence is the predicate because the subject is implied.
• **Subject Modifiers**: Words like "the," "a," "my," or "those" are part of the complete subject.
• **Predicate Complements**: Words that follow a linking verb to rename or describe the subject are part of the predicate.
• **Direct Objects**: These are part of the predicate and receive the action of the transitive verb.
• **Indirect Objects**: These are also part of the predicate and tell to whom or for whom the action is done.
• **Sentence Construction**: Building a sentence requires choosing a subject and then providing a predicate that makes sense.
