• **Present Continuous Tense Overview:** The Present Continuous tense describes an action that is happening right now, at the exact moment of speaking or writing.
• **Structural Formula:** The core formula for the Present Continuous tense is Subject + *is* / *am* / *are* + (Verb + *ing*) + Object/Complement.
• **Subject-Auxiliary Agreement in Present:** 
  - Use **am** exclusively with the first-person singular pronoun (*I*).
  - Use **is** with third-person singular subjects (*He*, *She*, *It*, singular nouns).
  - Use **are** with second-person and all plural subjects (*We*, *You*, *They*, plural nouns).
• **Primary Function (Immediate Action):** Indicating actions currently in progress, such as "The children are playing in the school garden."
• **Temporary Situations:** Expressing temporary states that may not be happening at the exact micro-second of speech, e.g., "My cousin is staying with us for the weekend."
• **Developing Situations:** Describing gradual changes or trends over a current period, such as "The climate is getting warmer every year."
• **Future Arrangements:** Using the Present Continuous to denote a definite future plan or fixed arrangement, e.g., "We are visiting Agra tomorrow."
• **Time Markers:** Common adverbial phrases signaling this tense include *at present*, *at this moment*, *now*, *currently*, and *look!* or *listen!*.
• **Spelling Rules for 'ing' forms:** Drop the silent 'e' at the end of a base verb before adding 'ing' (e.g., *write* becomes *writing*).
• **Double Consonant Rule:** Double the final consonant of a single-syllable verb ending in consonant-vowel-consonant before adding 'ing' (e.g., *run* becomes *running*).
• **Exceptions in 'ing' Spelling:** Verbs ending in 'ie' change 'ie' to 'y' before adding 'ing' (e.g., *lie* becomes *lying*).
• **Stative vs. Dynamic Verbs:** Stative verbs (like *love*, *know*, *belong*) are generally not used in the Present Continuous tense because they express states rather than actions.
• **Negative Sentences:** Formulated by placing the particle *not* immediately after the auxiliary verb: Subject + *is* / *am* / *are* + *not* + (Verb + *ing*).
• **Interrogative Sentences:** Formulated by inverting the subject and the auxiliary verb: *Is* / *Am* / *Are* + Subject + (Verb + *ing*)?
• **DAV Classroom Tip:** Students must remember that omitting the auxiliary verb (*is*, *am*, *are*) is the most frequent error when writing in the Present Continuous tense.

• **Past Continuous Tense Overview:** The Past Continuous tense is utilized to express an action that was ongoing or in progress at a specific point in the past.
• **Structural Formula:** The fundamental structure for the Past Continuous tense is Subject + *was* / *were* + (Verb + *ing*) + Object/Complement.
• **Subject-Auxiliary Agreement in Past:**
  - Use **was** with singular subjects (*I*, *He*, *She*, *It*, singular nouns).
  - Use **were** with plural subjects and the pronoun 'you' (*We*, *You*, *They*, plural nouns).
• **Primary Function (Interrupted Action):** Describing a longer ongoing past action that was interrupted by a shorter action in the simple past, e.g., "I was reading a book when the phone rang."
• **Parallel Past Actions:** Expressing two or more continuous actions happening simultaneously in the past, e.g., "While mother was cooking, the children were watching television."
• **Specific Past Moment:** Indicating an action that was ongoing at a precise time in the past, e.g., "At 8 o'clock last night, we were having dinner."
• **Atmosphere and Background:** Setting the descriptive background or atmosphere in a narrative before introducing the main events.
• **Time Markers:** Frequently used with time expressions such as *while*, *when*, *all day yesterday*, *at that moment*, and *at this time yesterday*.
• **Role of 'While':** The conjunction *while* is almost always followed immediately by the Past Continuous tense because it emphasizes duration.
• **Role of 'When':** The conjunction *when* is typically followed by the Simple Past tense when introducing an interruption to a continuous action.
• **Negative Formation:** Constructed by adding *not* after the past auxiliary: Subject + *was* / *were* + *not* + (Verb + *ing*).
• **Interrogative Formation:** Created by placing *was* or *were* at the very beginning of the sentence: *Was* / *Were* + Subject + (Verb + *ing*)?
• **Distinction from Simple Past:** While the Simple Past states an action completed in the past, the Past Continuous emphasizes its duration and flow.
• **Common Usage Error:** Students often mistakenly use the Simple Past instead of the Past Continuous when describing background scenes in past stories.
• **DAV Practice Focus:** Exercises in this section require students to combine clauses using *when* and *while* with correct tense matching.

• **Future Continuous Tense Overview:** The Future Continuous tense represents an action that will be ongoing at a specific time or over a period in the future.
• **Structural Formula:** The standard formula for this tense is Subject + *will be* / *shall be* + (Verb + *ing*) + Object/Complement.
• **Auxiliary Verb Usage:** *Shall be* is traditionally used with first-person pronouns (*I*, *we*), while *will be* is used with all subjects (*I*, *we*, *you*, *he*, *she*, *it*, *they*). Modern English heavily favors *will be* across all persons.
• **Primary Function (Future Projections):** Predicting actions that will be in progress at a given future moment, e.g., "This time tomorrow, I will be flying to London."
• **Expected Future Events:** Describing routine future events that are expected to happen as part of a natural course of action, e.g., "The principal will be addressing the assembly at 10 AM."
• **Polite Inquiries:** Using the Future Continuous to ask about someone's plans without sounding demanding, e.g., "Will you be using your bicycle this evening?"
• **Time Markers:** Key indicators include *this time tomorrow*, *at this hour next week*, *next year*, and specific future time points.
• **Contrast with Simple Future:** The Simple Future (*will go*) announces a decision or simple occurrence, whereas the Future Continuous (*will be going*) visualizes the action unfolding over time.
• **Negative Construction:** Formed by inserting *not* between the modal auxiliary and 'be': Subject + *will* / *shall* + *not* + *be* + (Verb + *ing*).
• **Interrogative Construction:** Formed by placing the modal verb at the beginning, followed by the subject and *be*: *Will* / *Shall* + Subject + *be* + (Verb + *ing*)?
• **Exclusion of Stative Verbs:** Just like present and past continuous forms, stative verbs are avoided in the Future Continuous tense.
• **Narrative Horizon:** Writers use the Future Continuous to set a scene in futuristic science fiction or speculative writing.
• **DAV Curriculum Insight:** DAV Class 6 exercises emphasize transforming simple future sentences into future continuous statements to reinforce structural awareness.
• **Clarity in Scheduling:** Helps students articulate long-term academic schedules and extracurricular timetables clearly.
• **Summary of Continuous Aspect:** Across present, past, and future, the continuous aspect always pairs a form of the verb 'be' with the present participle (*ing* form).

• **Present Perfect Tense Overview:** The Present Perfect tense connects past actions or states with the present moment, emphasizing relevance, completion, or experience.
• **Structural Formula:** The structural framework is Subject + *has* / *have* + Past Participle (V3 form) + Object/Complement.
• **Subject-Auxiliary Agreement:**
  - Use **has** with third-person singular subjects (*He*, *She*, *It*, singular nouns).
  - Use **have** with first-person, second-person, and all plural subjects (*I*, *We*, *You*, *They*, plural nouns).
• **Primary Function (Completed Action with Present Relevance):** Expressing actions that finished recently and whose effects are felt now, e.g., "She has cleaned her room" (it is clean now).
• **Life Experiences:** Describing actions or experiences up to the present without specifying the exact time, e.g., "I have visited the Taj Mahal twice."
• **Actions with 'Just':** Indicating an action completed moments ago using the adverb *just*, e.g., "The train has just arrived."
• **Unfinished Time Periods:** Used with time expressions that are still ongoing, such as *today*, *this week*, *this year*, e.g., "We have had three tests this week."
• **Role of 'Since' and 'For':**
  - **Since** is used with a specific point in time (e.g., *since 2010*, *since Monday*).
  - **For** is used with a duration of time (e.g., *for five years*, *for two hours*).
• **Time Markers:** Common adverbs include *already*, *yet*, *ever*, *never*, *just*, *recently*, *lately*, *so far*.
• **Usage of 'Yet':** Typically placed at the end of negative sentences and questions, e.g., "Have you finished your homework yet?" or "The bell has not rung yet."
• **Negative Construction:** Formed by placing *not* between the auxiliary and the past participle: Subject + *has* / *have* + *not* + V3.
• **Interrogative Construction:** Inverted by placing *has* or *have* first: *Has* / *Have* + Subject + V3?
• **Common Error (Simple Past Confusion):** Students often confuse the Present Perfect with the Simple Past by including specific past time markers like *yesterday* with 'has/have'. This is grammatically incorrect.
• **DAV Rule Enforcement:** DAV grammar modules strictly require V3 (Past Participle) mastery; forms like *goed* or *writed* are penalized.
• **Importance in Diary Entries:** The Present Perfect is essential for diary writing and personal recounts where recent past events impact current feelings.

• **Past Perfect Tense Overview:** The Past Perfect tense is used to express an action that was completed before another specific action or point in the past.
• **Structural Formula:** The structural rule is Subject + *had* + Past Participle (V3 form) + Object/Complement.
• **Universal Auxiliary:** Unlike present and future tenses, the auxiliary verb *had* remains unchanged regardless of whether the subject is singular or plural.
• **Primary Function (The 'Earlier Past'):** When two past actions happen at different times, the Past Perfect describes the earlier action, while the Simple Past describes the later action.
• **Classic Example:** "The train had left before we reached the station" (Leaving happened first, reaching happened second).
• **Narrative Sequencing:** Essential for storytelling to show flashbacks or actions that occurred prior to the main narrative timeline.
• **State of Completion in the Past:** Expressing a condition or state that was fulfilled before a past milestone, e.g., "By the time he was ten, he had learned three languages."
• **Time Markers and Conjunctions:** Frequently introduced with connecting words and phrases like *before*, *after*, *by the time*, *when*, and *already*.
• **Clause Pairing Rule:** 
  - Past Perfect clause + *before* + Simple Past clause.
  - After clause (Simple Past) + comma + Past Perfect clause.
• **Negative Construction:** Formed by adding *not* after *had*: Subject + *had* + *not* (or *hadn't*) + V3.
• **Interrogative Construction:** Formed by placing *had* at the beginning of the sentence: *Had* + Subject + V3?
• **Common Error (Double Past):** Students often erroneously use the Simple Past for both clauses (e.g., *The train left before we reached*), missing the hierarchical timing distinction.
• **DAV Teaching Technique:** Timeline diagrams are used in DAV materials to visually separate the earlier past action from the later past action.
• **Formal Writing Value:** Demonstrates sophisticated control over narrative complexity, elevating student writing standards in examinations.
• **Summary of Past Perfect:** It is the grammar of historical priority—always looking further back into the past from a reference point.

• **Future Perfect Tense Overview:** The Future Perfect tense is used to describe an action that will be completed by a certain specified point in the future.
• **Structural Formula:** The standard formula is Subject + *will have* / *shall have* + Past Participle (V3 form) + Object/Complement.
• **Auxiliary Breakdown:** Uses modal verbs *will* or *shall* combined with the auxiliary *have* and the V3 form of the main verb.
• **Primary Function (Future Deadline):** Indicating an action that will be finished prior to a specific deadline or future time marker, e.g., "By next month, I will have finished my DAV project."
• **Expression of Confidence:** Used when speakers are certain that an event will reach completion by a designated future horizon.
• **Key Preposition:** The preposition **by** is almost always used in Future Perfect sentences to denote the deadline (e.g., *by DAV annual day*, *by 5 PM*).
• **Time Markers:** Common temporal phrases include *by this time tomorrow*, *by the end of the year*, *in two years' time*, and *before then*.
• **Contrast with Future Continuous:** Future Continuous emphasizes an ongoing process at a future time, whereas Future Perfect emphasizes a completed result.
• **Negative Construction:** Formed by inserting *not* between the modal and *have*: Subject + *will* / *shall* + *not* + *have* + V3.
• **Interrogative Construction:** Formed by placing the modal verb at the beginning: *Will* / *Shall* + Subject + *have* + V3?
• **Rare Usage Note:** While grammatically essential, the Future Perfect is used less frequently in everyday spoken English than present or past tenses, appearing mostly in formal or written contexts.
• **DAV Curriculum Focus:** Class 6 students are introduced to this tense to expand their capacity for long-term planning and formal report writing.
• **Subject Independence:** Modern usage overwhelmingly relies on *will have* for all grammatical persons, though *shall have* remains correct for first-person pronouns.
• **Error Prevention:** Students must ensure they never use the base verb or 'has' with *will* (e.g., *will has finished* is completely incorrect; it must always be *will have finished*).
• **Summary of Perfect Aspect:** Across all timeframes, the perfect aspect always incorporates a form of the verb 'have' followed by a past participle (V3).

• **Comprehensive Aspect Comparison:** Tenses (Part-III) unites continuous and perfect aspects, requiring students to clearly distinguish between actions in progress and completed actions.
• **Continuous Aspect Core:** Defined by duration, flow, and ongoing progression, always utilizing a form of the auxiliary *be* + (Verb + *ing*).
• **Perfect Aspect Core:** Defined by completion, relevance, results, and priority, always utilizing a form of the auxiliary *have* + Past Participle (V3).
• **Comparative Table - Present Tenses:**
  - Present Continuous: *He is writing.* (Happening now)
  - Present Perfect: *He has written.* (Completed with current relevance)
• **Comparative Table - Past Tenses:**
  - Past Continuous: *He was writing.* (Ongoing at a past moment)
  - Past Perfect: *He had written.* (Completed before another past action)
• **Comparative Table - Future Tenses:**
  - Future Continuous: *He will be writing.* (Ongoing at a future moment)
  - Future Perfect: *He will have written.* (Completed by a future deadline)

  - Structure: Subject + *will be* / *shall be* + V-ing.
  - Core Use: Ongoing action at a future time.
  - Keyword: *This time tomorrow*.
• **Master Reference Guide - Present Perfect:**
  - Structure: Subject + *has* / *have* + V3.
  - Core Use: Completed action with present relevance.
  - Keyword: *Just*, *Already*, *Yet*.
• **Master Reference Guide - Past Perfect:**
  - Structure: Subject + *had* + V3.
  - Core Use: Action completed before another past action.
  - Keyword: *Before*, *After*, *By the time*.
