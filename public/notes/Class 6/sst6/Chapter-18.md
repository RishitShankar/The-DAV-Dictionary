• **Chapter Context**: Welcome to the DAV Dictionary academic study notes for Class 6 Social Science, subject "We and Our World", focusing on Chapter 18: "Deccan and South India (Fourth Century CE to Seventh Century CE)".
• **Geographical Scope**: The historical focus of this chapter is the region lying south of the Vindhya mountains, encompassing the Deccan plateau and the extreme southern Indian peninsula.
• **Time Period**: The era under study spans from the 4th century CE to the 7th century CE, a period characterized by major political shifts, the rise of powerful regional dynasties, and immense cultural growth.
• **Geographical Barriers and Bridges**: The Vindhya mountain ranges and the Narmada-Tapti river valleys served as both a bridge and a barrier for cultural and political exchanges between North and South India.
• **Natural Wealth**: The Deccan and South India were immensely rich in natural resources, including fertile river deltas, abundant mineral wealth, and extensive coastlines facilitating international maritime trade.
• **Major Rivers of the South**: The Krishna, Godavari, Cauvery, and Tungabhadra river basins acted as the lifelines for agriculture, supporting dense populations and flourishing urban centers.
• **Rise of Regional Kingdoms**: Following the decline of the Satavahanas in the Deccan and the early Tamil kingdoms in the far south, new powerful dynasties emerged to establish political stability.
• **Major Dynasties of the Period**: The primary political powers that dominated this era were the Vakatakas, the Chalukyas of Badami, and the Pallavas of Kanchipuram.
• **Political Fragmentation and Integration**: While the region witnessed the rise of independent regional states, there was also continuous interaction through warfare, alliances, and cultural assimilation.
• **Shift in Political Structure**: Political power became increasingly decentralized, characterized by the rise of hereditary monarchies, powerful feudal lords, and autonomous village assemblies.
• **Sources of History**: Our understanding of this period is derived from diverse sources, including rock-cut inscriptions, copper-plate grants, coins, foreign accounts, and magnificent architectural monuments.
• **Inscriptional Evidence**: Prashastis (praise poems) composed by court poets, such as the Aihole Inscription of Ravikirti, provide invaluable chronological and genealogical details about southern rulers.
• **Architectural Marvels**: The temples and rock-cut cave complexes built during this era stand as enduring testimony to the advanced engineering, artistic brilliance, and religious devotion of the period.
• **Economic Prosperity**: Agricultural expansion, flourishing internal commerce, and vibrant maritime trade with Southeast Asia and the Roman world brought immense wealth to the southern kingdoms.
• **Religious Transformation**: This era witnessed the decline of early Buddhism and Jainism in the south, replaced by the vigorous resurgence of Puranic Hinduism and the Bhakti movement led by Nayanars and Alvars.

• **The Vakataka Dynasty**: Emerging in the central Deccan during the mid-3rd century CE, the Vakatakas played a crucial role in shaping the political and cultural landscape of peninsular India until the late 5th century CE.
• **Vakataka Territories**: The Vakataka kingdom served as a vital bridge between Northern India (the Gupta Empire) and Southern India, facilitating the exchange of administrative ideas and art forms.
• **Vindhyashakti**: He was the founder and first notable ruler of the Vakataka dynasty, establishing local hegemony in the Vidarbha region after the decline of the Satavahanas.
• **Pravarasena I**: The son of Vindhyashakti, he was the only Vakataka ruler to assume the imperial title of *Samrat*, significantly expanding the dynasty's territories across the Deccan.
• **Matrimonial Alliances**: The Vakatakas strengthened their political standing through strategic marriage alliances, most notably when Queen Prabhavatigupta (daughter of the Gupta emperor Chandragupta II) married Rudrasena II.
• **Prabhavatigupta's Rule**: Following the early death of her husband, Queen Prabhavatigupta effectively ruled the Vakataka kingdom as regent for her young sons, maintaining close ties with the Gupta Empire.
• **Cultural Impact of the Gupta-Vakataka Alliance**: The alliance led to a profound Northern influence on Deccan art, Sanskrit literature, and administrative practices during the late 4th and 5th centuries CE.
• **Patronage of Art and Architecture**: The Vakataka rulers were great patrons of art and architecture, most famously associated with the excavation of several magnificent Buddhist caves at Ajanta.
• **Ajanta Caves under Vakatakas**: Caves 16, 17, and 19 at Ajanta were excavated and decorated with world-renowned murals and rock-cut sculptures under the direct patronage of Vakataka rulers and their ministers.
• **Decline of the Vakatakas**: By the late 5th and early 6th centuries, the Vakataka power waned due to internal conflicts and the rise of other regional powers, paving the way for the Chalukyas of Badami.
• **The Chalukyas of Badami**: Also known as the Early Chalukyas, this dynasty rose to prominence in the western Deccan in the mid-6th century CE, establishing a formidable empire.
• **Pulakeshin I**: He was the founder of the Chalukya dynasty of Badami, who fortified the strategic hill fort of Vatapi (modern Badami) and laid the foundation for future imperial expansion.
• **Capital City**: Vatapi (modern Badami in Karnataka) served as the magnificent capital of the Early Chalukyas, adorned with exquisite rock-cut cave temples and structural shrines.
• **Pulakeshin II (609–642 CE)**: He was the greatest and most illustrious ruler of the Chalukya dynasty, transforming it into an imperial power that dominated the entire Deccan plateau.
• **Expansion under Pulakeshin II**: Pulakeshin II subdued neighboring kingdoms, defeated the Kadambas, Gangas, and Mauryas of Konkan, and established undisputed supremacy across the Deccan region.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Conflict with Harshavardhana**: The crowning achievement of Pulakeshin II's military career was his decisive victory over the mighty North Indian emperor Harshavardhana on the banks of the river Narmada.
• **Stopping the Northern Advance**: By halting Harshavardhana's southward expansion, Pulakeshin II established the Narmada River as the traditional boundary between North and South India.
• **Aihole Inscription**: Composed by the court poet Ravikirti in Sanskrit, the Aihole Inscription at the Meguti temple provides a detailed, glowing account of Pulakeshin II's military victories, including his triumph over Harsha.
• **Foreign Relations**: Pulakeshin II maintained diplomatic relations with distant foreign powers, including the Sasanian Empire of Persia, as depicted in contemporary paintings at the Ajanta Caves.
• **Visit of Hiuen Tsang**: The famous Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Hiuen Tsang visited the Chalukyan empire during the reign of Pulakeshin II and left glowing accounts of the king's governance, justice, and the bravery of his people.
• **Decline of Pulakeshin II**: In 642 CE, the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I launched a retaliatory invasion, defeated Pulakeshin II, captured Badami, and temporarily crippled Chalukyan power.
• **Revival under Vikramaditya I**: The Chalukyas recovered their lost glory under Pulakeshin's son, Vikramaditya I, who drove out the Pallavas and re-established Chalukyan sovereignty over Badami.
• **Kirtivarman II**: He was the last ruler of the Early Chalukya dynasty, whose defeat by Dantidurga in the mid-8th century led to the rise of the Rashtrakutas.
• **The Pallavas of Kanchipuram**: Contemporaries of the Early Chalukyas, the Pallavas emerged as a major imperial power in the far south, controlling Tondaimandalam and parts of the northern Tamil region.
• **Origins of the Pallavas**: The exact origins of the Pallavas remain debated among historians, with theories suggesting they were local chieftains, former Satavahana feudatories, or of foreign extraction.
• **Simhavishnu**: He was the real builder of the Pallava power, who defeated the Kalabhras, subdued neighboring rulers, and extended the Pallava frontier up to the Cauvery river.
• **Kanchipuram**: The ancient city of Kanchipuram served as the glorious capital of the Pallavas, renowned as a major center of learning, commerce, art, and religion.
• **Mahendravarman I (600–630 CE)**: A multifaceted genius, Mahendravarman I was a great ruler, poet, dramatist, musician, and pioneer of rock-cut architecture in Tamil Nadu.
• **Architectural Transition**: Mahendravarman I initiated the rock-cut style of architecture in the Tamil region, carving cave temples out of solid rock without using bricks or mortar.
• **Narasimhavarman I (Mahamalla)**: The son and successor of Mahendravarman I, he was the greatest Pallava ruler, known for avenging his father's defeats and patronizing monolithic coastal art.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Title of Mahamalla**: Narasimhavarman I assumed the title 'Mahamalla' (great wrestler), after whom the famous port city of Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram) was named.
• **Naval Power of the Pallavas**: The Pallavas developed a powerful navy to protect their extensive coastline and facilitate vibrant trade and cultural missions to Southeast Asia (Suvarnabhumi).
• **Embarkation to Southeast Asia**: Pallava ports like Mamallapuram and Nagapattinam served as major departure points for merchants, scholars, and princes traveling to modern-day Indonesia, Malaysia, and Cambodia.
• **Spread of Indian Culture**: The intense maritime contacts initiated by the Pallavas resulted in the profound spread of Indian scripts, art, architecture, and religious traditions across Southeast Asia.
• **Later Pallava Rulers**: Following Narasimhavarman I, rulers like Paramesvaravarman I and Narasimhavarman II (Rajasimha) maintained Pallava prestige, focusing extensively on structural temple building.
• **Rajasimha's Structural Temples**: Transitioning from rock-cut caves, Narasimhavarman II pioneered the construction of magnificent structural stone temples, such as the Shore Temple at Mamallapuram and the Kailasanatha Temple at Kanchipuram.
• **Decline of the Pallavas**: By the late 9th century CE, the Pallava dynasty weakened due to continuous wars with the Chalukyas and Pandyas, ultimately being succeeded by the Imperial Cholas.
• **Administration under Deccan and Southern Dynasties**: The political systems of the Vakatakas, Chalukyas, and Pallavas were monarchical, with the king holding supreme executive, judicial, and military authority.
• **Divine Right Theory**: Kings often associated themselves with divine powers, adopting grand titles like *Maharajadhiraja* (King of Kings) and claiming descent from celestial beings to legitimize their rule.
• **Assistance by Ministers**: The monarch was assisted by a council of ministers and high-ranking officials, including the *Amatya* (minister), *Senapati* (commander-in-chief), and royal scribes.
• **Provincial Administration**: Kingdoms were divided into provinces known as *Mandalas* or *Rashtras*, which were further subdivided into districts (*Visayas* or *Nadus*) and sub-districts.
• **Governors and Officials**: Provinces were governed by royal princes (*Kumaramatyas*) or trusted nobles who maintained law, collected revenue, and reported directly to the central court.
• **Village Administration**: The lowest and most stable unit of administration was the village, which enjoyed a high degree of self-sufficiency and local autonomy.
• **Village Assemblies**: Villages were managed by local assemblies, committees, and village headmen who looked after public works, taxation, justice, and temple affairs.
• **Uttiramerur Inscriptions**: Famous inscriptions from Uttiramerur provide detailed descriptions of the democratic election procedures, qualifications, and functioning of rural sabhas during the southern dynastic era.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Revenue System**: Land revenue was the primary source of income for the state, typically calculated as a fraction (usually one-sixth) of the agricultural produce collected in cash or kind.
• **Other Taxes**: Additional state revenues came from customs duties, professional taxes, tolls on trade routes, fines, and tribute collected from subordinate chieftains.
• **Land Grants (Brahmadeya)**: Kings made extensive tax-free land grants to Brahmins, Vedic scholars, and religious institutions (temples and monasteries) to earn spiritual merit and secure loyal support.
• **Agrarian Expansion**: Land grants played a crucial role in clearing new forests, extending agricultural land into peripheral areas, and introducing advanced irrigation techniques in the Deccan and South.
• **Irrigation Infrastructure**: Due to seasonal rainfall dependency, southern rulers invested heavily in the construction of large tanks, artificial reservoirs, canals, and dams to ensure steady crop yields.
• **Major Irrigation Projects**: Rulers like the Pallavas and Chalukyas excavated massive water tanks (e.g., Kaveripakkam tank) that transformed dry tracts into lush, productive agricultural belts.
• **Social Structure**: Society during the 4th to 7th centuries CE was structured around the traditional Varna system, though it grew increasingly complex due to the incorporation of diverse tribal and occupational groups.
• **Position of Brahmins**: Brahmins occupied a highly privileged position in society, enjoying tax-free land grants, high social status, and acting as advisors, educators, and ritual specialists.
• **Rise of Feudalism (Samantas)**: The widespread practice of land grants fostered the growth of a feudal class of hereditary landlords and military chieftains known as *Samantas* or *Mahasamantas*.
• **Role of Samantas**: While initially subordinate to the central monarch, powerful Samantas frequently maintained their own private armies and occasionally challenged imperial authority during times of royal weakness.
• **Position of Women**: Women from royal and elite families enjoyed high social standing, received education, participated in administrative decisions, and engaged in religious patronage.
• **Restrictions on Ordinary Women**: Despite elite privileges, ordinary women faced patriarchal constraints, and the custom of child marriage and restrictions on widows began to take firmer root during this era.
• **Devadasi System**: The early roots of the *Devadasi* tradition emerged during this period, where young girls were dedicated to temple services, learning classical dance (Bharatanatyam) and music.
• **Economic Life and Trade**: The economy was characterized by a thriving agrarian base, vibrant domestic commerce, and extensive international maritime trade networks.
• **Guilds (Shrenis)**: Trade and craft production were organized into powerful corporate guilds (*Shrenis*), which regulated prices, maintained quality standards, trained apprentices, and functioned as banks.

• **Internal Trade Routes**: Well-maintained trade routes crisscrossed the peninsula, connecting inland production centers, mining regions, and agricultural markets with bustling coastal ports.
• **Maritime Trade**: South Indian ports maintained active trade links with Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, China, and the Western world, exporting spices, cotton textiles, precious stones, and pearls.
• **Imports**: In return for local products, southern merchants imported horses from Arabia and Central Asia, silk from China, and glass and pottery from the Roman/Byzantine world.
• **Coinage**: The Chalukyas and Pallavas issued gold, silver, and copper coins featuring dynastic emblems like the boar (*Varaha*) of the Chalukyas and the bull or lion of the Pallavas.
• **Religious Developments**: This period marked a profound religious transition in the Deccan and South India, characterized by the decline of Buddhism and Jainism and the massive rise of Puranic Hinduism.
• **Royal Patronage of Hinduism**: Dynasties like the Vakatakas, Chalukyas, and Pallavas were staunch patrons of Vedic and Puranic Hinduism, constructing thousands of magnificent temples dedicated to Shiva, Vishnu, and Durga.
• **Bhakti Movement**: The 6th and 7th centuries CE witnessed the birth of the devotional Bhakti movement in Tamil Nadu, spearheaded by popular saint-poets who preached personal devotion over ritualism.
• **Alvars**: The Alvar saints were passionate devotees of Lord Vishnu who composed emotional devotional hymns in Tamil, later compiled into the sacred anthology known as the *Nalayira Divya Prabandham*.
• **Nayanars**: The Nayanar saints were ardent devotees of Lord Shiva who traveled across the Tamil region spreading messages of love, equality, and devotion through poetry compiled in the *Tevaram*.
• **Inclusivity of Bhakti**: The Bhakti saints broke rigid caste and gender barriers by welcoming all people, regardless of social status, into the fold of divine worship, singing hymns in vernacular Tamil.
• **Decline of Buddhism and Jainism**: Deprived of royal patronage and facing ideological challenges from dynamic Hindu saints and philosophers, Buddhism and Jainism gradually lost their mass following in the south.
• **Jainism in Karnataka**: While declining in the Tamil region, Jainism retained strong pockets of influence in the Karnataka region, patronized by the Western Ganga rulers and marked by monuments like Shravanabelagola.
• **Art and Architecture (General Overview)**: The architectural achievements of the Deccan and South India represent one of the most glorious chapters in the history of Indian art.
• **Transition from Wood to Stone**: Early structures in South India were made of perishable materials like wood, brick, and thatch; however, this era marked a revolutionary shift toward durable rock-cut and stone construction.
• **Types of Architecture**: Southern architecture of this period is broadly classified into rock-cut cave temples, monolithic chariot temples (*rathas*), and structural stone temples.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Chalukyan Architecture**: The Chalukyas of Badami pioneered the Vesara style of architecture, blending elements of North Indian (Nagara) and South Indian (Dravidian) architectural traditions.
• **Rock-Cut Caves at Badami**: The cave temples at Badami (four in total: three Hindu and one Jain) are masterworks of sculpture, featuring finely carved pillars, spacious halls, and intricate panels depicting Hindu deities.
• **Aihole - Cradle of Temple Architecture**: Aihole is famously known as the "cradle of Indian temple architecture," containing over 70 early temples that showcase experimental structural designs.
• **Notable Temples at Aihole**: The Ladh Khan Temple, the Durga Temple (known for its unique apsidal shape), and the Meguti Jain Temple represent early milestones in structural temple engineering.
• **Pattadakal Monument Complex**: Located on the banks of the Malaprabha River, Pattadakal served as the coronation site for Chalukyan kings and features a magnificent cluster of ten major temples.
• **Synthesis at Pattadakal**: The Pattadakal complex uniquely showcases a side-by-side evolution of both the northern Nagara style (e.g., Papanatha Temple) and the southern Dravidian style (e.g., Virupaksha Temple).
• **Virupaksha Temple**: Built by Queen Lokamahadevi to commemorate her husband's victory over the Pallavas, the Virupaksha Temple at Pattadakal is the grandest Chalukyan monument, anticipating later Dravidian masterpieces.
• **Pallava Architecture**: The Pallavas are celebrated for initiating the Dravidian style of temple architecture, progressing through four distinct evolutionary phases named after famous rulers.
• **Mahendra Style**: The first phase (Mahendra style) involved excavating rock-cut cave temples out of solid rock cliffs, characterized by heavy pillars with bracket capitals.
• **Mamalla Style**: The second phase (Mamallapuram phase) introduced monolithic *rathas* (chariots carved out of single rock boulders) and magnificent open-air rock-cut bas-relief sculptures.
• **The Pancha Pandava Rathas**: At Mamallapuram, five monolithic rock-cut shrines known as the Pancha Pandava Rathas showcase miniature models of different architectural roof types.
• **Descent of the Ganges (Arjuna's Penance)**: This massive open-air rock relief at Mamallapuram depicts animals, celestial beings, and human ascetics rushing toward a river, hailed as a masterpiece of world art.
• **Rajasimha Style**: The third phase marked the transition to structural stone masonry, producing monumental shrines like the Shore Temple at Mamallapuram and the Kailasanatha Temple at Kanchipuram.
• **Nandivarman Style**: The final phase saw smaller, elegant structural temples built with refined artistic details, reflecting the late Pallava aesthetic.
• **Sculptural Excellence**: Sculptures of this period were characterized by fluid grace, realistic anatomical proportions, deep emotional expression, and dynamic portrayal of divine and mythological themes.

• **Literature and Education**: The period from the 4th to 7th centuries CE witnessed extraordinary literary activity in Sanskrit, Prakrit, and vernacular languages like Tamil and Kannada.
• **Patronage of Sanskrit**: Sanskrit enjoyed royal patronage as the language of administration, high literature, and classical epics, promoted extensively by the Vakatakas, Chalukyas, and Pallavas.
• **Court Poets and Epigraphs**: Court poets composed elaborate *Prashastis* in classical Sanskrit, displaying high mastery of poetic meters, metaphors, and historical narrative.
• **Ravikirti**: As the court poet of Pulakeshin II, Ravikirti compared his poetic composition at Aihole to the works of legendary classical Sanskrit poets like Kalidasa and Bharavi.
• **Dandin and Bharavi**: Renowned classical Sanskrit authors like Bharavi (author of *Kiratarjuniya*) and Dandin (author of *Kavyadarsa* and *Dasakumaracarita*) spent time at the Pallava court in Kanchipuram.
• **Mahendravarman I as Author**: The Pallava king Mahendravarman I himself authored brilliant Sanskrit farces, such as *Mattavilasa Prahasana* (The Farce of Drunken Sport) and *Bhagavadajjuka*.
• **Growth of Tamil Literature**: The post-Sangam era saw the flowering of devotional Tamil literature, deeply intertwined with the Bhakti movement led by the Alvars and Nayanars.
• **Sangam Tradition Legacy**: The literary traditions established during the earlier Sangam age continued to inspire poets, lexicographers, and grammarians in the Tamil-speaking south.
• **Development of Kannada Literature**: Although early inscriptions show the use of Kannada, full-fledged literary works in Kannada began to emerge toward the end of this period, later flourishing under the Rashtrakutas.
• **Centers of Higher Learning**: Temples, monasteries (*mathas*), and royal courts functioned as premier centers of higher learning, imparting education in grammar, philosophy, astronomy, medicine, and the Vedas.
• **Kanchipuram as an Educational Hub**: Kanchipuram gained international fame as a great center of learning (Ghatika), attracting students from all over India, Sri Lanka, and China.
• **Science and Technology**: Scientific pursuits in medicine, metallurgy, and astronomy made significant strides during this era, supported by royal artisans and builders.
• **Metallurgical Brilliance**: The ability of southern metalworkers to cast massive bronze sculptures and forge high-quality iron implements is evident in contemporary artifacts and structural temple anchors.
• **Naval Architecture and Navigation**: Advanced knowledge of monsoon wind patterns, shipbuilding techniques, and navigational instruments enabled southern mariners to undertake long-distance voyages across the Indian Ocean.
• **Legacy of the Era**: The political, cultural, and architectural achievements of the Vakatakas, Chalukyas, and Pallavas laid the bedrock for medieval South Indian civilization.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Synthesis of Northern and Southern Traditions**: A defining feature of this period was the harmonious blending of Aryan (Northern) and Dravidian (Southern) cultural elements, creating a pan-Indian synthesis.
• **Spread of Puranic Ideology**: Puranic deities, myths, and temple rituals became deeply integrated into the daily lives of people across the Deccan and South India, superseding older tribal cults.
• **Evolution of Temple Towns**: Temples evolved from mere places of worship into massive economic and social institutions that drove urbanization, trade, and employment in southern towns.
• **Employment Generation by Temples**: Temples provided livelihoods for thousands of priests, sculptors, dancers, musicians, sweepers, cooks, garland makers, and estate administrators.
• **Educational Role of Temples**: Many large structural temples operated residential schools (*Pathashalas*) where students were taught Sanskrit, grammar, religious texts, and mathematics free of cost.
• **Granary and Banking Functions**: Temples functioned as community grain banks during times of famine and served as financial institutions that extended loans to merchants and farmers.
• **Cultural Diplomacy**: South Indian kingdoms utilized art, architecture, and religious missions as tools of soft power and cultural diplomacy, binding Southeast Asian nations to Indian cultural spheres.
• **Artistic Continuity**: The sculptural and architectural techniques perfected by the Chalukyas and Pallavas set aesthetic standards that directly inspired later empires like the Cholas, Hoysalas, and Vijayanagara.
• **Epigraphical Records**: The thousands of inscriptions left behind by these dynasties provide historians with precise chronologies, tax structures, and social histories unmatched in other parts of the contemporary world.
• **Inter-Dynastic Rivalries**: Constant military conflicts between the Chalukyas of Badami and the Pallavas of Kanchipuram dominated the political narrative, often draining resources but stimulating military innovation.
• **The Battle of Vatapi (642 CE)**: The capture and destruction of the Chalukyan capital Vatapi by the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I stands as one of the most dramatic military episodes of early medieval Indian history.
• **Resilience of Dynasties**: Despite military setbacks, both Chalukyan and Pallava lineages demonstrated remarkable resilience, recovering their territories and continuing their patronage of art and culture.
• **Geopolitical Shifts**: The constant power struggles in the Deccan and South India eventually paved the way for new imperial formations, such as the Rashtrakutas in the Deccan and the Cholas in the deep south.
• **Enduring Heritage**: The rock-cut wonders of Ajanta, Badami, and Mamallapuram, along with the structural masterpieces of Pattadakal and Kanchipuram, remain celebrated UNESCO World Heritage sites today.
• **Summary of Historical Significance**: The period from the 4th to 7th centuries CE was a golden age of regional state formation, architectural revolution, and socio-religious transformation in Deccan and South India.
