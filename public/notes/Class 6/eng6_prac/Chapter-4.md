• **Chapter Overview**: Welcome to the DAV Dictionary academic study notes for Class 6 English Practice Book, Chapter 4, focusing comprehensively on the foundational grammatical concept of **Subject-Verb Agreement**.
• **Definition of Subject**: The subject of a sentence is the person, place, thing, or idea that performs the action or is being described by the verb.
• **Definition of Verb**: A verb is the action word or state-of-being word that tells us what the subject is doing, has done, or what state it exists in.
• **Core Principle of Agreement**: Subject-verb agreement dictates that the verb must match, or "agree" with, its subject in terms of **number** (singular or plural) and **person** (first, second, or third).
• **Number Harmony**: If the subject of a sentence is singular, the verb must also be in its singular form; conversely, if the subject is plural, the verb must be plural.
• **Role in Communication**: Proper subject-verb agreement ensures grammatical accuracy, logical clarity, and professional polish in both written and spoken English communications.
• **DAV Curriculum Alignment**: These notes are structured specifically to meet the pedagogical standards of the DAV Public School Board's Class 6 English curriculum.
• **Primary Classification**: English verbs and subjects are divided into distinct categories based on whether they refer to one entity (singular) or more than one entity (plural).
• **Third-Person Singular Rule**: The most critical rule in present tense agreement revolves around the third-person singular pronoun and noun categories.
• **Sentence Construction Foundation**: Mastering this chapter prevents common errors such as pairing plural verbs with singular nouns, a frequent pitfall for middle-school learners.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Singular Nouns**: A singular noun names one single person, animal, place, or thing (e.g., *boy, dog, city, book*).
• **Plural Nouns**: A plural noun names two or more persons, animals, places, or things, usually formed by adding *-s* or *-es* to the singular noun (e.g., *boys, dogs, cities, books*).
• **Singular Verbs in Present Tense**: Unlike nouns, regular verbs in the simple present tense become **singular** by adding an *-s* or *-es* to their base form (e.g., *runs, eats, watches*).
• **Plural Verbs in Present Tense**: The base form of a verb without the *-s* or *-es* ending is generally treated as **plural** in the simple present tense (e.g., *run, eat, watch*).
• **The Inverted Noun-Verb Rule**: Nouns add *-s* to become plural, whereas present-tense verbs add *-s* to become singular; this inverse relationship is a primary source of student confusion.
• **Visualizing the Inverse**: Think of the *-s* as a scarce resource: if the noun has it (*The dog barks*), the verb takes it; if the noun is plural (*The dogs*), the verb loses it (*bark*).
• **First-Person Singular**: The pronoun **I** is singular but uniquely takes a plural verb form in many tenses (e.g., *I walk, I am, I have*).
• **First-Person Plural**: The pronoun **We** is plural and always takes a plural verb form (e.g., *We walk, We are, We have*).
• **Second-Person Application**: The pronoun **You** can refer to one person or multiple people, but it invariably takes a plural verb form in English grammar (e.g., *You walk, You are*).
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Third-Person Singular Pronouns**: The pronouns **He**, **She**, and **It** are strictly singular and require singular verb forms in the simple present tense (e.g., *He runs, She sings, It barks*).
• **Third-Person Plural Pronouns**: The pronoun **They** is plural and requires a plural verb form across all standard verb categories (e.g., *They run, They sing, They bark*).
• **Singular Noun Subject Rule**: A singular common or proper noun functioning as a subject takes a singular verb (e.g., *The child drinks milk*).
• **Plural Noun Subject Rule**: A plural common or proper noun functioning as a subject takes a plural verb (e.g., *The children drink milk*).
• **Proper Noun Agreement**: Singular proper nouns, such as names of people or unique places, take singular verbs (e.g., *Delhi is a large city*, *Rahul plays cricket*).
• **Collective Nouns Overview**: Collective nouns refer to a group of individuals or things acting as a single unit (e.g., *team, family, herd, committee*).
• **Standard Collective Noun Rule**: When a collective noun acts as a single, unified body, it takes a **singular verb** (e.g., *The team wins the championship*).
• **Divided Collective Noun Rule**: If the members of a collective noun act as individuals with separate opinions or actions, it takes a **plural verb** (e.g., *The committee are arguing among themselves*).
• **DAV Textbook Emphasis**: The DAV Class 6 English Practice Book places special emphasis on correctly identifying collective nouns to prevent common agreement errors.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Compound Subjects Overview**: A compound subject consists of two or more individual subjects joined by a conjunction, sharing the same verb.
• **The Conjunction "And"**: When two or more singular or plural subjects are joined by **and**, they almost always form a **plural subject** requiring a **plural verb** (e.g., *Rohan and Sohan are playing*).
• **Single Unit Exception**: If two singular nouns joined by "and" represent a single, inseparable idea, item, or food dish, they take a **singular verb** (e.g., *Bread and butter is my favorite breakfast*).
• **Single Person Exception**: When two nouns joined by "and" refer to the exact same person or title, the verb is singular (e.g., *The novelist and poet has arrived*).
• **Conjunctions "Or" / "Nor"**: When singular subjects are joined by **or**, **nor**, **either...or**, or **neither...nor**, the verb must be **singular** (e.g., *Neither the boy nor the girl is present*).
• **Mixed Subjects with "Or" / "Nor"**: When a singular subject and a plural subject are joined by **or** or **nor**, the verb must agree with the **closer subject** (e.g., *Neither the teacher nor the students are here*).
• **Proximity Rule**: The rule stating that the verb agrees with the subject closest to it in disjunctive constructions is known as the **Rule of Proximity**.
• **Application of Proximity Rule**: *Either the students or the teacher is responsible* uses a singular verb because "teacher" (the closer noun) is singular.
• **Refining Compound Structures**: Careful analysis of conjunctions ("and" vs. "or/nor") is vital for solving DAV Practice Book exercises accurately.

• **Indefinite Pronouns Overview**: Indefinite pronouns do not refer to any specific person, place, or thing, and their grammatical number often confuses students.
• **Always Singular Indefinite Pronouns**: Words such as **everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, anybody, no one, nobody** are strictly singular and take singular verbs (e.g., *Everyone is ready*).
• **Each, Every, and Much**: Pronouns and determiners like **each**, **every**, and **much** always require singular verbs, even when followed by plural modifying phrases (e.g., *Each of the boys has a prize*).
• **Either and Neither as Pronouns**: When used alone as pronouns, **either** and **neither** are treated as singular (e.g., *Either of the options is acceptable*).
• **Plural Indefinite Pronouns**: Words such as **both, few, many, several** are always plural and require plural verbs (e.g., *Several students were absent*).
• **Variable Indefinite Pronouns**: Pronouns like **some, any, all, none** can be singular or plural depending on the uncountable or countable nature of the noun they refer to.
• **Uncountable Variable Rule**: When variable indefinite pronouns refer to an uncountable noun, they take a **singular verb** (e.g., *Some of the milk is spilled*).
• **Countable Variable Rule**: When variable indefinite pronouns refer to a countable plural noun, they take a **plural verb** (e.g., *Some of the apples are rotten*).
• **Mastering Indefinite Sentences**: Memorizing the permanent singular versus permanent plural lists of indefinite pronouns ensures high scores on DAV assessments.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Uncountable Nouns Overview**: Uncountable nouns (mass nouns) represent things that cannot be counted individually and do not have plural forms (e.g., *water, furniture, advice, information, equipment*).
• **Singular Verb with Uncountable Nouns**: Uncountable nouns always take **singular verbs** because they are treated as singular units (e.g., *The furniture looks old*, *Information is power*).
• **Quantifiers with Uncountable Nouns**: Even when preceded by phrases indicating quantity, uncountable nouns maintain singular verb agreement unless the quantifier specifies distinct units.
• **Expressions of Time and Money**: Expressions of time, money, distance, and weight usually take a **singular verb** when viewed as a single, collective amount (e.g., *Ten dollars is a high price*, *Five miles is a long walk*).
• **Plural Amounts Exception**: If individual coins, bills, or distinct periods of time are emphasized rather than the total sum, a plural verb may be used (e.g., *Ten silver dollars were scattered on the table*).
• **Titles of Books and Works**: Titles of books, movies, plays, organizations, and musical compositions take a **singular verb**, regardless of whether the words within the title are plural (e.g., *The Chronicles of Narnia is a fantasy series*).
• **Mathematical Expressions**: Basic mathematical additions and equations can take either singular or plural verbs, though singular is traditionally preferred (e.g., *Two plus two is four*).
• **Fractions and Percentages**: Fractions and percentages take singular or plural verbs depending on the noun that follows the preposition "of" (e.g., *Half of the cake is gone*; *Half of the apples are gone*).

• **Interrogative Sentences Overview**: Sentences that ask questions (interrogative sentences) often invert the normal subject and verb order, making agreement trickier to spot.
• **Identifying the True Subject in Questions**: To check agreement in a question, mentally rephrase the sentence into a declarative statement to clearly isolate the subject from the auxiliary verb.
• **Do / Does / Did Agreement**: In present tense questions, use **does** with singular subjects (e.g., *Does the train arrive on time?*) and **do** with plural subjects (e.g., *Do the trains arrive on time?*).
• **Is / Are / Am in Questions**: Ensure the linking or auxiliary verb matches the inverted subject (e.g., *Where is my book?* vs. *Where are my books?*).
• **Sentences Beginning with "There" and "Here"**: The words **there** and **here** are never subjects; the true subject always follows the verb in these constructions (e.g., *There is a cat in the garden*, *There are cats in the garden*).
• **Applying the "There/Here" Rule**: Always look past the introductory word "there" or "here" to locate the real noun and determine whether to use a singular or plural verb.
• **Relative Pronouns (Who, Which, That)**: When a relative pronoun acts as the subject of a dependent clause, the verb must agree with its **antecedent** (the noun it refers back to).
• **Antecedent Agreement Example**: In the sentence *It is the students who are responsible*, the verb "are" agrees with "students" (the antecedent of "who"), not "It".
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Nouns with Plural Forms & Singular Meaning**: Certain nouns end in *-s* (such as academic subjects, diseases, or games) but are grammatically **singular** (e.g., *mathematics, physics, measles, billiards, news*).
• **Singular Treatment of *-s* Nouns**: These singular *-s* nouns take **singular verbs** (e.g., *Mathematics is my favorite subject*, *The news is surprising*).
• **Plural-Only Nouns**: Nouns that describe tools or articles of clothing made of two parts are always plural and take **plural verbs** (e.g., *trousers, scissors, pliers, binoculars, spectacles*).
• **Using "Pair of"**: When plural-only tool nouns are preceded by the phrase "a pair of", the subject becomes singular and takes a **singular verb** (e.g., *A pair of scissors is on the desk*).
• **Plural Nouns without "Pair of"**: Without the modifier "pair of", those exact tool nouns require plural verbs (e.g., *My scissors are sharp*).
• **Names of Countries and Organizations**: Plural-looking names of countries, sports teams, or organizations usually take **singular verbs** if they represent a single entity (e.g., *The United States is a large country*).
• **Modifying Phrases Between Subject and Verb**: Words, prepositional phrases, or clauses that come between the subject and the verb do not change the number of the subject.
• **Ignoring Intervening Phrases**: In the sentence *The box of chocolates is on the table*, the verb is "is" because the subject is "box", ignoring the intervening plural noun "chocolates".
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Common Student Pitfalls**: Students frequently make mistakes by matching the verb to the noun closest to it rather than the actual grammatical subject (proximity error).
• **Pitfall with Prepositional Phrases**: Avoid errors like *The quality of these mangoes are good*; the correct form is *is good* because the subject is "quality", not "mangoes".
• **Pitfall with "Each of" Constructions**: Students often mistakenly write *Each of the students have a pen*, failing to realize "Each" is singular and requires *has*.
• **Error Correction Methodology**: Always read the sentence aloud, identify the core subject, strip away modifying prepositional phrases, and verify the number before writing the final verb.
• **DAV Practice Book Strategy**: When tackling DAV exercise sets, underline the subject once and circle the verb twice to visually verify their agreement before finalizing answers.
• **Editing and Proofreading**: Effective proofreading involves checking every single subject-verb pairing in a composition to maintain consistent tense and number harmony.
• **Advanced Subject-Verb Alignment**: Multi-clause complex sentences require checking agreement independently in both the main clause and all subordinate clauses.
• **Spoken vs. Written Standard**: While informal spoken English sometimes permits local dialect variations, formal academic writing strictly requires adherence to standard subject-verb agreement rules.

• **Comprehensive Summary**: Subject-verb agreement is the structural backbone of English syntax, ensuring that subjects and verbs match in number and person.
• **Review of Singular vs. Plural**: Singular subjects take singular verbs (marked by *-s* in present tense); plural subjects take plural verbs (base form).
• **Special Rules Checklist**: Always account for compound subjects joined by *and* versus *or/nor*, collective nouns, indefinite pronouns, and uncountable nouns.
• **Inverted Structures Reminder**: Pay special attention to questions, sentences starting with *there/here*, and intervening prepositional phrases that obscure the true subject.
• **Tool and Academic Nouns**: Remember that words ending in *-s* like *mathematics* or *news* take singular verbs, while tool nouns like *scissors* take plural verbs unless modified by *a pair of*.
