• **Chapter Introduction:** Welcome to Chapter 5 of Class 6 Science under The DAV Dictionary curriculum, titled "Changes Around Us", which explores the dynamic, ever-changing nature of the physical world.
• **Scope of Study:** This comprehensive academic note explores the continuous transformations occurring in our daily lives, distinguishing between various types of changes based on reversibility, speed, and nature.
• **Definition of Change:** **Change** is defined as an alteration or modification in the physical state, shape, size, color, position, or chemical composition of an object or substance over a period of time.
• **Ubiquity of Change:** Changes are happening everywhere and at all times around us; not a single object or living being in the universe remains completely static or unchanging.
• **Classification Framework:** To systematically study changes, scientists and students classify them into various categories such as reversible and irreversible, physical and chemical, fast and slow, and desirable and undesirable.
• **Natural vs. Man-made Changes:** Changes can be spontaneous natural occurrences (like changing seasons, ripening of fruit) or deliberate human-induced transformations (like cooking food, building structures).
• **Time Scale of Changes:** Some changes happen instantaneously (like bursting of a balloon), while others take days, months, years, or even geological epochs (like rusting of iron, growth of a child, continental drift).
• **Role of Energy:** Almost every change is driven by the transfer, absorption, or release of energy, most commonly in the form of heat, light, mechanical force, or chemical energy.
• **State of Matter Alterations:** Many everyday changes involve transitions between states of matter—solid, liquid, and gas—primarily influenced by the addition or removal of thermal energy.
• **Importance in Daily Life:** Understanding changes helps us utilize materials effectively, predict outcomes, cook food, preserve perishables, and harness natural processes for technological advancements.
• **Scientific Observation:** Careful observation of changes forms the empirical foundation of the scientific method, encouraging young learners to question "what," "how," and "why" things transform.
• **Matter Conservation Principle:** Although substances change their forms, appearance, or properties during transformations, the total mass of the participating matter remains conserved in physical processes.
• **Internal vs. External Factors:** Changes are triggered by external agents (temperature, pressure, catalysts, force) or internal properties (inherent chemical reactivity, biological aging).
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Reversible Changes Definition:** A **reversible change** is defined as a transformation in which the initial substance can be recovered or brought back to its original state after the change has occurred.
• **Characteristics of Reversibility:** In a reversible change, no new chemical substances are formed; the molecular identity of the material remains fundamentally unaltered despite physical alterations.
• **Melting of Ice:** The melting of ice cubes into liquid water is a classic reversible change; the water can be placed back in a freezer to reform ice.
• **Dissolution of Salt in Water:** Dissolving common salt in water creates a solution, but the salt can be completely recovered by evaporating the water through heating.
• **Stretching of Rubber Band:** Stretching a rubber band alters its length and shape temporarily, but it returns to its original dimensions the moment the stretching force is released.
• **Inflating a Balloon:** Blowing air into an uninflated balloon changes its size and shape, but letting the air escape brings the balloon back to its exact original flat condition.
• **Folding Paper:** Folding a sheet of paper to make a paper airplane alters its spatial form, yet the paper can be unfolded and flattened back to its original sheet form.
• **Boiling and Condensation:** Boiling liquid water turns it into steam (gas), and cooling that steam causes it to condense back into liquid water, demonstrating a complete reversible cycle.
• **Freezing of Liquids:** Liquid juice or water turning into solid popsicles or ice upon cooling is fully reversible simply by increasing the surrounding temperature.
• **Iron Expansion and Contraction:** Heating metal tracks or components causes slight expansion, while cooling causes contraction; these dimensional shifts are entirely reversible without material loss.
• **Dissolution of Sugar:** Sugar dissolved in warm milk or water can be recovered by crystallization and evaporation techniques, marking it as a physically reversible change.
• **Bending a Metal Wire:** Gently bending a soft copper wire changes its geometric configuration, but it can be straightened back manually, demonstrating reversibility.
• **Mechanical Deformation:** Springs compressed or stretched under load resume their initial unweighted shape once the external load is removed, provided the elastic limit is not crossed.
• **Phase Transition Cycles:** Melting, freezing, evaporation, and condensation represent a continuous loop of phase transitions that are inherently reversible physical changes.
• **Key Diagnostic Test:** To determine if a change is reversible, one must ask: "Can the final product be easily converted back into the exact original starting material?"
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Irreversible Changes Definition:** An **irreversible change** is a permanent transformation in which the original substance cannot be recovered back from the final products by simple physical methods.
• **Nature of Irreversible Changes:** These changes typically involve the formation of entirely new substances with distinct chemical and physical properties compared to the reactants.
• **Cooking of Food:** Cooking raw ingredients like rice, vegetables, or batter into cooked meals alters them permanently; cooked food cannot be turned back into raw ingredients.
• **Baking a Cake:** Mixing flour, sugar, eggs, and baking powder and baking them in an oven undergoes complex chemical reactions that form a cake, which cannot be reversed.
• **Burning of Paper or Wood:** Burning a piece of paper produces ash, carbon dioxide, water vapor, and smoke; the original paper can never be retrieved from these products.
• **Ripening of Fruits:** Green, unripe mangoes or bananas turn sweet, soft, and colored upon ripening; this biological transformation is strictly one-way and irreversible.
• **Germination of Seeds:** A tiny seed sprouting into a young seedling and growing into a mature plant is an irreversible biological growth process.
• **Curdling of Milk:** Adding a few drops of lemon juice or curd culture to warm milk turns it into curd and whey; the curd cannot be converted back into liquid milk.
• **Rusting of Iron:** Iron objects exposed to damp air develop a reddish-brown flaky coating of rust, permanently damaging the metal structure and preventing reversion.
• **Digestion of Food:** Food consumed by living organisms is broken down enzymatically into simpler nutrients absorbed by the body, which can never be reassembled into the original food item.
• **Bursting of a Firecracker:** Exploding a firecracker shatters its casing and triggers rapid chemical combustion, transforming the materials into gases, heat, sound, and ash irreversibly.
• **Frying an Egg:** Breaking a raw egg and frying it on a hot pan solidifies the albumen and yolk permanently; the fried egg cannot return to its raw liquid state.
• **Growth in Living Beings:** The growth of humans, animals, and plants from infancy to maturity is a permanent, non-reversible biological progression.
• **Setting of Cement:** Mixing cement with water and sand causes a chemical hardening process called setting; once hardened into concrete, it cannot be melted or reversed.
• **Permanent Staining:** Spilling permanent ink or dye on a white cloth causes a chemical bonding with the fibers that cannot be washed away, representing an irreversible change.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Physical Changes Definition:** A **physical change** is a temporary or permanent alteration only in the physical properties of a substance—such as size, shape, color, state, or appearance—without creating new chemical substances.
• **Molecular Stability:** During a physical change, the chemical composition and molecular formula of the constituent particles remain completely unchanged.
• **Change of State (Melting/Freezing):** Ice melting into water or water vaporizing into steam are classic physical changes because the chemical formula remains $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in all three states.
• **Cracking Glass or Breaking Chalk:** Breaking a piece of chalk into dust or shattering a glass pane alters the size and shape of the object, but the material's chemical identity remains identical.
• **Magnetization of Iron:** Rubbing a permanent magnet along an iron nail aligns its internal magnetic domains, turning the nail into a temporary magnet without changing iron's chemical nature.
• **Dissolving Substances:** Mixing sugar, salt, or powdered drink mix into water results in physical mixing where particles disperse but do not react chemically to form new compounds.
• **Sublimation:** The direct conversion of solid camphor, dry ice (solid $\text{CO}_2$), or iodine crystals into gas without passing through the liquid phase is a physical change.
• **Thermal Expansion:** Heating railway tracks, metal bridges, or mercury inside a thermometer causes expansion due to increased kinetic energy of particles, representing a physical alteration.
• **Drawing Metal Wires (Ductility):** Hammering gold into thin sheets (malleability) or drawing copper into fine wires alters physical dimensions without altering the metal itself.
• **Mixing Sand and Iron Filings:** Combining sand and iron filings forms a mechanical mixture where individual components retain their unique physical properties and can be separated easily.
• **Grinding Solid Crystals:** Crushing large rock salt crystals into fine table salt changes the particle size dramatically, but the chemical substance ($\text{NaCl}$) remains untouched.
• **Filtering Suspensions:** Passing a muddy water mixture through filter paper separates mud particles from water physically without modifying either substance chemically.
• **Evaporation of Seawater:** Evaporating ocean water to harvest salt leaves behind solid sodium chloride crystals through a purely physical separation process.
• **Reversibility Tendency:** Most physical changes are inherently reversible, though exceptions like tearing paper or shattering glass exist.
• **Energy Exchange:** Physical changes involve relatively small amounts of energy absorption or release compared to chemical changes.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Chemical Changes Definition:** A **chemical change** (also called a chemical reaction) is a permanent transformation in which one or more new substances with entirely different chemical and physical properties are formed.
• **Creation of New Matter:** In a chemical change, original reactants break their chemical bonds and atoms rearrange to form completely different product molecules.
• **Permanence:** Chemical changes are almost always irreversible by simple physical methods because the original reactants cannot be easily reconstituted from the products.
• **Evolution of Gas:** Many chemical reactions release gases, often observed through effervescence, bubbling, or fizzing (e.g., baking soda reacting with vinegar releasing carbon dioxide).
• **Change in Color:** A strong indicator of a chemical change is an unexpected color transition, such as copper sulfate solution turning blue to green when iron is added.
• **Temperature Change:** Chemical reactions frequently release heat (exothermic reactions like burning) or absorb heat (endothermic reactions like photosynthesis) spontaneously.
• **Emission of Light or Sound:** Certain chemical reactions release energy in the form of intense light (magnesium ribbon burning) or sound (firecracker detonation).
• **Formation of Precipitate:** Mixing two clear liquid solutions can result in the formation of an insoluble solid called a precipitate, signifying a chemical reaction.
• **Rusting of Iron (Oxidation):** The reaction between iron, oxygen in the air, and moisture creates hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust), a distinct chemical compound.
• **Photosynthesis in Plants:** Green plants convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll, a vital biological chemical change.
• **Respiration in Living Organisms:** Cells break down glucose using inhaled oxygen to release energy, carbon dioxide, and water, representing a continuous internal chemical change.
• **Combustion (Burning):** Burning wood, paper, coal, or LPG reacts hydrocarbons with oxygen to produce ash, heat, light, carbon dioxide, and water vapor.
• **Souring of Milk:** Lactose sugar in milk converts into lactic acid through bacterial action, changing the taste, smell, and chemical structure of the milk.
• **Digestion of Nutrients:** Hydrochloric acid and digestive enzymes break down complex food polymers into simple monomer units inside the gastrointestinal tract.
• **Difference from Physical Changes:** Unlike physical changes, chemical changes alter the intrinsic identity, formula, and chemical reactivity of the matter involved.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Heating as a Catalyst:** Applying heat is one of the most powerful methods to bring about changes in substances, often altering their state, color, or chemical composition.
• **Melting Solids via Heat:** Supplying heat energy to ice, wax, butter, or chocolate causes them to transition from solid to liquid state efficiently.
• **Boiling Liquids:** Heating water until it reaches its boiling point ($100^\circ\text{C}$ at standard pressure) converts it rapidly into water vapor (steam).
• **Thermal Decomposition:** Strongly heating certain compounds causes them to break down into simpler substances, such as limestone (calcium carbonate) decomposing into quicklime and carbon dioxide.
• **Charring of Sugar:** Heating sugar crystals in a spoon causes them to melt, turn brown, and eventually char into a black carbon mass, showing a chemical change induced by heat.
• **Expansion Caused by Heat:** Solids, liquids, and gases expand when heated because thermal energy increases the kinetic energy and average distance between constituent particles.
• **Cooling as a Transformative Agent:** Removing heat (cooling) is equally powerful in driving changes, such as freezing liquids, condensing vapors, and contracting materials.
• **Condensation Mechanism:** Warm air containing water vapor touching a cold surface (like a cold soda can or window pane) cools down, turning vapor into liquid droplets.
• **Freezing Point Transition:** Placing liquid water inside a refrigerator freezer extracts heat, lowering its temperature to $0^\circ\text{C}$ and converting it into solid ice.
• **Contraction on Cooling:** Cooling metal objects, gases, or liquids causes them to shrink or contract as particles lose kinetic energy and move closer together.
• **Role of Water in Changes:** Water acts as a universal solvent and reaction medium, facilitating countless physical and chemical changes like dissolving, rusting, and hydration.
• **Hydration Reactions:** Adding water to anhydrous copper sulfate turns it from white to blue, demonstrating a specific color change driven by chemical hydration.
• **Moisture and Rusting:** Dry iron does not rust; the presence of atmospheric moisture alongside oxygen is an absolute prerequisite for the chemical rusting process.
• **Dissolution Speed:** Heating water increases the rate at which solids like sugar or salt dissolve, demonstrating how temperature and mixing accelerate physical changes.
• **Energy Balance Summary:** Heat input drives endothermic and phase-change processes, while heat removal drives exothermic and solidification processes.

• **Tool Fabrication Process:** Tools made of iron, such as spade blades, axes, and agricultural hoes, have a small ring-like metal eye through which a wooden handle is tightly fixed.
• **Mechanism of Tool Fixing:** The iron ring of the tool is initially slightly smaller in inner diameter than the wooden handle's end cross-section, ensuring a snug fit.
• **Heating the Iron Ring:** To fit the wooden handle, the iron ring is heated over a fire; upon heating, the metal expands and its inner hole enlarges.
• **Inserting the Handle:** While the iron ring is hot and expanded, the wooden handle is easily inserted through the enlarged hole.
• **Cooling and Tightening:** Cold water is immediately poured over the hot iron ring; the ring cools down, contracts sharply, and grips the wooden handle with immense mechanical strength.
• **Preventing Loosening:** This clever application of thermal expansion and contraction ensures that heavy-duty striking tools do not fly off their handles during rigorous field use.
• **Wheel Rim Fitting on Carts:** Traditional wooden cart wheels are fitted with an iron outer rim to increase durability and protect the wood from abrasive wear on rough roads.
• **Rim Sizing Principle:** The circular iron rim is deliberately made slightly smaller in circumference than the wooden wheel frame it is meant to encircle.
• **Heating the Rim:** The iron rim is heated uniformly in a circular forge fire, causing it to expand and stretch just enough to slip over the wooden wheel.
• **Quenching the Rim:** Cold water is splashed onto the hot, fitted iron rim; it contracts violently, squeezing the wooden wheel components together into a rigid, unified structure.
• **Everyday Thermal Marvels:** These classical engineering techniques demonstrate how ancient artisans mastered the physical properties of matter long before modern thermodynamics was formalized.
• **Railway Track Gaps:** Small gaps are deliberately left between successive iron rails on railway tracks to accommodate thermal expansion during scorching summer days.
• **Preventing Buckling:** Without these expansion gaps, summer heat would cause tracks to push against each other and buckle, leading to severe derailment hazards.
• **Bridge Expansion Rollers:** Large steel bridges are mounted on rollers or sliding plates on one end to allow the structure to expand and contract freely with seasonal temperature swings.
• **Thermometer Mechanics:** Liquid mercury or alcohol inside a glass thermometer expands when warmed and contracts when cooled, rising and falling along a calibrated scale to measure temperature accurately.

• **Classroom Demonstration 1 (Melting Ice):** Taking a small quantity of ice in a glass beaker and leaving it at room temperature or heating it causes solid ice to melt into water, proving a reversible physical state change.
• **Classroom Demonstration 2 (Dissolving Salt):** Stirring a spoonful of salt into a glass of water until it completely vanishes demonstrates physical dissolution; boiling the water dry recovers the white salt crystals.
• **Classroom Demonstration 3 (Burning Magnesium Ribbon):** Holding a strip of magnesium ribbon with tongs and igniting it in a Bunsen burner flame produces a dazzling white light and leaves behind a white powdery ash (magnesium oxide), proving an irreversible chemical change.
• **Classroom Demonstration 4 (Heating Wax):** Melting solid candle wax in a metallic dish and letting it cool back into a solid demonstrates phase-based physical reversibility.
• **Classroom Demonstration 5 (Paper Burning vs. Folding):** Folding paper into a boat shape is physical and reversible (by unfolding), whereas burning a piece of paper is a non-reversible chemical change yielding ash and smoke.
• **Classroom Demonstration 6 (Chalk Dust):** Crushing a piece of chalk into powder changes its physical form, but mixing the powder with water and letting it dry recreates a solid chalk-like residue, though grain structure is altered.
• **Classroom Demonstration 7 (Baking Soda and Vinegar):** Mixing baking soda with vinegar inside a test tube produces vigorous effervescence of carbon dioxide gas, which extinguishes a burning splinter, proving a chemical reaction.
• **Classroom Demonstration 8 (Copper Sulfate and Iron):** Dropping an iron nail into an aqueous blue copper sulfate solution coats the nail in reddish-brown copper metal and turns the solution light green, indicating a displacement chemical change.
• **Observation Discipline:** Scientific experiments require accurate recording of initial states, applied treatments, transitional phenomena, and final product characteristics without bias.
• **Safety Precautions:** Handling open flames, hot glass apparatus, concentrated chemicals, and sharp tools during science demonstrations requires strict adherence to safety goggles and teacher supervision.
• **Distinguishing Indicators:** Students learn to separate physical mixing from chemical reactions by checking for color shifts, gas release, precipitate formation, and temperature changes.
• **Everyday Classification Exercises:** Analyzing common household phenomena—like milk turning sour, clothes drying in the sun, butter melting, and fireworks exploding—strengthens analytical thinking.
• **Drying of Wet Clothes:** Water molecules on wet clothes absorb solar heat and ambient air movement, evaporating into water vapor as a physical change where moisture transitions from fabric to atmosphere.
• **Condensation on Cold Surfaces:** Water vapor condensing into dew droplets on cold grass blades or soda cans illustrates atmospheric moisture undergoing a cooling physical phase transition.
• **Real-World Problem Solving:** Recognizing whether a change is desirable or harmful helps humans control environmental conditions, preserve food freshness, and prevent structural corrosion.

• **Desirable Changes Definition:** A **desirable change** is any transformation that is beneficial, useful, or advantageous to human beings, living organisms, and the environment.
• **Examples of Desirable Changes:** Ripening of crops, cooking of food, germination of seeds, changing seasons for agriculture, and curdling of milk into nutritious curd are highly desirable.
• **Undesirable Changes Definition:** An **undesirable change** is any transformation that is harmful, destructive, or disadvantageous, causing loss, spoilage, or danger.
• **Examples of Undesirable Changes:** Spoiling of food, rusting of iron tools, rotting of fresh vegetables, flooding caused by excessive rain, and volcanic eruptions destroying settlements.
• **Controlling Changes:** Human ingenuity often seeks to prevent undesirable changes (such as painting iron to prevent rust or refrigerating milk to prevent souring) while promoting desirable ones.
• **Fast Changes Definition:** A **fast change** (or rapid change) is a transformation that occurs within a fraction of a second, seconds, or minutes (e.g., bursting firecracker, striking a match).
• **Slow Changes Definition:** A **slow change** (or gradual change) is a transformation that takes hours, days, years, or centuries to complete (e.g., rusting, plant growth, day-night cycle, weathering of rocks).
• **Periodic Changes Definition:** A **periodic change** is a transformation that repeats itself at regular, predictable intervals of time (e.g., phases of the moon, changing seasons, beating of a human heart).
• **Non-Periodic Changes Definition:** A **non-periodic change** is a transformation that occurs irregularly and does not repeat at fixed time intervals (e.g., earthquakes, rainstorms, accidents, volcanic eruptions).
• **Natural vs. Artificial Categorization:** Changes driven by nature without human intervention are natural, whereas those initiated by human actions (like driving cars, manufacturing plastics) are artificial or man-made.
• **Biological Changes:** Transformations occurring within living bodies—such as digestion, respiration, blood circulation, and aging—are essential biological changes supporting life.
• **Environmental Impact:** Understanding changes around us empowers environmental conservationists to combat harmful man-made transformations like global warming, deforestation, and pollution.
• **Interconnectedness of Changes:** A single event can trigger multiple changes simultaneously; for instance, burning a candle melts wax (physical change) and produces carbon dioxide, water vapor, heat, and light (chemical changes).
• **Summary of Chapter 5 Core Concepts:** Changes are omnipresent, classified into reversible/irreversible, physical/chemical, fast/slow, and desirable/undesirable categories, governed by energy, temperature, and matter conservation.
