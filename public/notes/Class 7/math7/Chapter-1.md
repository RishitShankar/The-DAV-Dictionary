• **Natural Number Foundations:** **Natural Numbers ($\mathbb{N}$)** form the foundational counting system comprising all positive integers starting from $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ and extending infinitely.
• **Whole Number Expansion:** **Whole Numbers ($\mathbb{W}$)** extend natural numbers by including the absolute zero, represented mathematically as the set $\{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.
• **Integer System Integration:** **Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$)** incorporate all whole numbers along with their negative counterparts, forming the symmetric set $\{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.
• **Limitation of Integers:** While integers solve many arithmetic challenges, they fail to represent fractional parts or ratios of quantities, necessitating a broader number system.
• **Genesis of Rational Numbers:** The term **Rational Number** originates mathematically from the word 'ratio', denoting quantities that can be expressed as a comparative relation between two integers.
• **Formal Mathematical Definition:** A **Rational Number** is defined as any number that can be expressed in the exact form $\frac{p}{q}$, where $p$ and $q$ are both integers and the denominator $q$ is strictly non-zero ($q \neq 0$).
• **Numerator Identification:** In any rational expression $\frac{p}{q}$, the upper component $p$ is designated as the **Numerator**, representing the quantity taken.
• **Denominator Identification:** In the rational expression $\frac{p}{q}$, the lower component $q$ is designated as the **Denominator**, representing the total equal parts into which the whole is divided.
• **Non-Zero Denominator Rule:** The condition $q \neq 0$ is absolute because division by zero is mathematically undefined and yields infinity or indeterminate values.
• **Inclusion of Integers:** Every integer is inherently a rational number because any integer $a$ can be expressed seamlessly as $\frac{a}{1}$.
• **Zero as a Rational Number:** The number zero ($0$) is a valid rational number since it can be written as $\frac{0}{1}, \frac{0}{5}$, or $\frac{0}{-9}$, satisfying the $\frac{p}{q}$ criteria.
• **Inclusion of Fractions:** All positive fractions are automatically rational numbers, though rational numbers also encompass negative fractions, expanding the scope beyond standard arithmetic fractions.
• **Set Notation Representation:** The complete set of rational numbers is conventionally denoted by the blackboard bold symbol $\mathbb{Q}$.

• **Positive Rational Numbers:** A rational number is classified as **Positive** when both its numerator and denominator possess the exact same mathematical sign (either both are positive or both are negative).
• **Example of Positive Rationals:** Expressions such as $\frac{3}{7}$ and $\frac{-5}{-9}$ are strictly positive rational numbers because $\frac{-5}{-9}$ simplifies algebraically to $\frac{5}{9}$ through cancellation of negative signs.
• **Negative Rational Numbers:** A rational number is classified as **Negative** when its numerator and denominator possess opposite mathematical signs (one is positive and the other is negative).
• **Example of Negative Rationals:** Expressions such as $\frac{-3}{7}$ and $\frac{5}{-9}$ are strictly negative rational numbers, regardless of whether the negative sign is attached to the numerator or the denominator.
• **Zero Sign Convention:** The rational number zero ($0$) is unique; it is neither positive nor negative, serving as the neutral origin point on the number line.
• **Standard Form Definition:** A rational number $\frac{p}{q}$ is stated to be in **Standard Form** (or lowest terms) if $q$ is a positive integer and the greatest common divisor (GCD) of $|p|$ and $q$ is exactly $1$.
• **Reduction to Standard Form:** To reduce a rational number to its standard form, both numerator and denominator must be divided repeatedly by their Highest Common Factor (HCF).
• **Handling Negative Denominators:** If a rational number has a negative denominator, it must be converted to standard form by multiplying or dividing both numerator and denominator by $-1$ to shift the negative sign to the numerator.
• **Equality of Rational Numbers:** Two rational numbers $\frac{p}{q}$ and $\frac{r}{s}$ are considered **Equal (Equivalent)** if and only if $p \times s = q \times r$ (cross-multiplication yields identical products).
• **Generation of Equivalent Rations:** Equivalent rational numbers are generated by multiplying or dividing both the numerator and the denominator of a given rational number by the same non-zero integer $k$.
• **Infinite Equivalent Forms:** Every rational number possesses an infinite sequence of equivalent forms, reflecting the proportional nature of numbers on a continuous scale.
• **Simplification Principle:** Simplifying rational numbers preserves their fundamental value while reducing the numerical magnitude of both terms for easier computation.
• **Visualizing Rational Numbers:** Rational numbers can be mapped onto a geometric number line, providing a spatial representation of their relative sizes and distances from zero.
• **Number Line Origin:** The point zero ($0$) acts as the central reference point (origin) on a rational number line, dividing positive and negative domains.
• **Placement of Positive Rationals:** Positive rational numbers are plotted strictly to the right of zero on the horizontal number line, increasing in magnitude as they move further right.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Placement of Negative Rationals:** Negative rational numbers are plotted strictly to the left of zero on the horizontal number line, decreasing in value (becoming more negative) as they move further left.
• **Subdividing Unit Lengths:** To represent a rational number $\frac{p}{q}$ on a number line, each unit distance between integers must be divided into $q$ equal sub-intervals based on the denominator.
• **Counting Intervals:** The numerator $p$ dictates how many of those equal sub-intervals must be counted away from zero in the appropriate directional path to plot the exact position of the rational number.
• **Density Property:** Between any two distinct rational numbers, there exist infinitely many other rational numbers, a profound mathematical characteristic known as the **Density Property of Rational Numbers**.
• **Comparison of Rational Numbers:** Comparing two rational numbers involves determining whether one is greater than, less than, or equal to the other using relational symbols ($>, <, =$).
• **Method of Common Denominators:** To compare two rational numbers with different denominators, one must first convert them into equivalent rational numbers with a **Common Denominator** (typically the LCM of the denominators).
• **Comparison Rule for Positive Denominators:** Once denominators are positive and identical, the rational number with the larger numerator is inherently the greater number.
• **Cross-Multiplication Comparison:** For any two rational numbers $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$ with positive denominators, if $ad > bc$, then $\frac{a}{b} > \frac{c}{d}$; if $ad < bc$, then $\frac{a}{b} < \frac{c}{d}$.
• **Comparison Involving Negatives:** Any positive rational number is always strictly greater than zero, and zero is always strictly greater than any negative rational number.
• **Ordering Rational Numbers:** Arranging a given set of rational numbers in ascending order (smallest to largest) or descending order (largest to smallest) requires systematic comparison using LCM techniques.
• **Absolute Value of Rational Numbers:** The **Absolute Value** of a rational number $\frac{p}{q}$, denoted as $\left|\frac{p}{q}\right|$, represents its absolute magnitude or distance from zero, ignoring any negative signs.
• **Absolute Value Evaluation:** The absolute value of both $\frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{-3}{4}$ is identically $\frac{3}{4}$, as distance is fundamentally a non-negative scalar quantity.
• **Closure Property under Addition:** The sum of any two rational numbers is always another rational number, proving that the set of rational numbers is **Closed under Addition**.
• **Commutative Property of Addition:** For any two rational numbers $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$, addition is commutative, meaning $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c}{d} + \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Associative Property of Addition:** When adding three or more rational numbers, the grouping order does not affect the final sum, expressed as $(\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d}) + \frac{e}{f} = \frac{a}{b} + (\frac{c}{d} + \frac{e}{f})$.

• **Additive Identity Element:** The rational number zero ($0$) serves as the **Additive Identity** because adding $0$ to any rational number leaves the number completely unchanged ($\frac{a}{b} + 0 = \frac{a}{b}$).
• **Additive Inverse Element:** For any rational number $\frac{a}{b}$, its **Additive Inverse** (or negative) is $\frac{-a}{b}$, such that their sum equals the additive identity zero ($\frac{a}{b} + \left(\frac{-a}{b}\right) = 0$).
• **Subtraction of Rational Numbers:** Subtraction of a rational number is defined algebraically as adding its additive inverse; thus, subtracting $\frac{c}{d}$ from $\frac{a}{b}$ equals $\frac{a}{b} + \left(-\frac{c}{d}\right)$.
• **Closure Property under Subtraction:** The difference between any two rational numbers is always another rational number, establishing closure under subtraction.
• **Non-Commutativity of Subtraction:** Subtraction for rational numbers is strictly **Non-Commutative**, as $\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d}$ is generally not equal to $\frac{c}{d} - \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Non-Associativity of Subtraction:** Subtraction is also **Non-Associative**, meaning the grouping of terms alters the final result in subtraction operations.
• **Closure Property under Multiplication:** The product of any two rational numbers is always a rational number, demonstrating that the set $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under multiplication.
• **Commutative Property of Multiplication:** Multiplication of rational numbers is commutative, meaning $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c}{d} \times \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Associative Property of Multiplication:** The grouping of factors in rational multiplication does not alter the product, expressed as $(\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}) \times \frac{e}{f} = \frac{a}{b} \times (\frac{c}{d} \times \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Multiplicative Identity Element:** The rational number one ($1$) acts as the **Multiplicative Identity** because multiplying any rational number by $1$ preserves its exact value ($\frac{a}{b} \times 1 = \frac{a}{b}$).
• **Multiplicative Inverse Definition:** The **Multiplicative Inverse** (or Reciprocal) of a non-zero rational number $\frac{a}{b}$ is the rational number $\frac{b}{a}$, such that their product equals $1$.
• **Reciprocal Constraint:** Zero ($0$) is the only rational number that does not possess a multiplicative inverse, because division by zero is undefined.
• **Sign Rule for Reciprocals:** The reciprocal of a positive rational number is positive, and the reciprocal of a negative rational number is negative; the sign is always preserved.
• **Multiplication Algorithm:** To multiply two rational numbers, one multiplies their numerators together to form the new numerator, and their denominators together to form the new denominator.
• **Cross-Cancellation in Multiplication:** Numerators and denominators can be cross-cancelled by common factors prior to multiplication to simplify the computational process.

• **Closure Property under Division:** The quotient of two rational numbers (provided the divisor is non-zero) is always a rational number, establishing closure under division (excluding divisor zero).
• **Division Algorithm:** Dividing a rational number $\frac{a}{b}$ by another non-zero rational number $\frac{c}{d}$ is performed by multiplying $\frac{a}{b}$ by the reciprocal of the divisor $\frac{d}{c}$.
• **Non-Commutativity of Division:** Division of rational numbers is strictly **Non-Commutative**, as $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} \neq \frac{c}{d} \div \frac{a}{b}$ in general.
• **Non-Associativity of Division:** Division of rational numbers fails the associative property, meaning grouping arrangements change the final quotient outcome.
• **Distributive Property of Multiplication over Addition:** Multiplication distributes over addition for rational numbers, expressed as $\frac{a}{b} \times (\frac{c}{d} + \frac{e}{f}) = (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}) + (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Distributive Property over Subtraction:** Multiplication also distributes over subtraction, formulated as $\frac{a}{b} \times (\frac{c}{d} - \frac{e}{f}) = (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}) - (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Role of Parentheses in Order of Operations:** When simplifying complex rational expressions, standard mathematical precedence rules (**BODMAS/PEMDAS**) govern the sequence of operations.
• **Simplification Priority:** Brackets and grouping symbols must be evaluated first, moving from innermost parentheses (round, curly, square) outward.
• **Exponents and Powers of Rationals:** Raising a rational number to a positive integer exponent means multiplying the rational number by itself that many times.
• **Power Rule for Fractions:** For any rational number $\frac{a}{b}$ and positive integer $n$, $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}$, distributing the exponent to both numerator and denominator.
• **Negative Exponent Rule:** A rational number raised to a negative exponent requires reciprocating the base and changing the exponent to positive: $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{-n} = \left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^n$.
• **Zero Exponent Rule:** Any non-zero rational number raised to the power of zero is identically equal to $1$: $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^0 = 1$.
• **Multiplication Law of Exponents:** When multiplying powers of the same rational base, exponents are added: $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^m \times \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{m+n}$.
• **Division Law of Exponents:** When dividing powers of the same rational base, exponents are subtracted: $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^m \div \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{m-n}$.
• **Power of a Power Law:** Raising a power to another power involves multiplying the exponents: $\left(\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^m\right)^n = \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{m \times n}$.

• **Decimal Representation Connection:** Every rational number can be expressed in decimal form through long division of the numerator by the denominator.
• **Terminating Decimals:** A rational number yields a **Terminating Decimal** expansion if the prime factorization of its denominator in standard form contains only powers of $2$, powers of $5$, or both.
• **Example of Terminating Decimal:** The rational number $\frac{3}{8}$ results in $0.375$ because its denominator $8$ factorizes exclusively as $2^3$.
• **Non-Terminating Repeating Decimals:** A rational number yields a **Non-Terminating Repeating (Recurring) Decimal** if its denominator contains prime factors other than $2$ or $5$.
• **Example of Repeating Decimal:** The rational number $\frac{1}{3}$ results in $0.3333\dots$, commonly written with a vinculum bar as $0.\bar{3}$.
• **Conversion of Terminating Decimals:** Terminating decimals are easily converted back into rational numbers by placing the decimal digits over the appropriate power of ten ($10, 100, 1000$) and simplifying.
• **Conversion of Repeating Decimals:** Pure recurring decimals are converted to rational form $\frac{p}{q}$ using algebraic multiplier techniques involving powers of ten to eliminate the infinite repeating tail.
• **Word Problems Application:** Rational numbers model real-world scenarios such as sharing quantities, measuring physical lengths, financial debts, and calculating proportional recipes.
• **Speed and Distance Application:** Calculating travel times, average speeds, and fractional distances covered frequently requires operations with rational numbers and mixed fractions.
• **Financial Mathematics Application:** Profit, loss, discounts, and simple interest calculations often utilize rational number fractions and percentages interchangeably.
• **Temperature Measurement Context:** Temperatures below zero, measured on scales like Celsius or Fahrenheit, involve negative integers and rational fractional increments.
• **Scientific Notation Preview:** Extremely large or small quantities in science are represented using rational coefficients multiplied by powers of ten, bridging arithmetic and algebra.
• **Mental Arithmetic Strategies:** Utilizing properties like associativity and commutativity allows students to rearrange complex rational number expressions for easier mental calculation.
• **Estimation Techniques:** Approximating rational numbers by rounding decimals or fractions helps verify the reasonableness of calculated mathematical results.
• **Error Analysis in Computation:** Common student errors in rational numbers include improper sign management during subtraction and failing to find a common denominator before adding.

• **Advanced DAV Curriculum Insight:** The DAV curriculum emphasizes conceptual clarity over rote memorization, requiring students to justify every step in rational number proofs.
• **Derivation of Additive Inverse:** The proof that the additive inverse is unique relies on the cancellation laws of addition within the algebraic structure of fields.
• **Field Axioms of Rational Numbers:** The set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ forms an algebraic **Field** because it satisfies field axioms for both addition and multiplication (closure, associativity, commutativity, identities, inverses, and distributivity).
• **Ordering Axiom Compliance:** Along with field axioms, $\mathbb{Q}$ satisfies ordered axioms, making it an **Ordered Field**, ensuring consistent behavior with inequalities and number line placement.
• **Completeness Property Contrast:** Unlike real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$), the rational number system is **Not Complete**, meaning there are "gaps" on the number line corresponding to irrational numbers like $\sqrt{2}$ or $\pi$.
• **Betweenness Theorem:** For any two distinct rational numbers $x$ and $y$, the arithmetic mean $\frac{x + y}{2}$ is always a rational number strictly lying between $x$ and $y$.
• **Generating Multiple Intermediate Rationals:** Repeatedly applying the mean formula or converting denominators allows the systematic generation of any required count of rational numbers between two bounds.
• **LCM Mastery in Operations:** Finding the **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** of denominators is the cornerstone technique for performing addition and subtraction of unlike rational numbers.
• **Prime Factorization Method for LCM:** Determining the LCM efficiently involves prime factorizing each denominator and taking the highest exponent of each prime factor present.
• **Equivalent Fraction Adjustment:** Once the LCM is found, each rational number is multiplied by a strategic factor to convert its denominator to the LCM value without altering its value.
• **Numerator Combination Step:** After achieving common denominators, numerators are combined directly under the shared denominator according to standard integer arithmetic rules.
• **Final Simplification Mandate:** Every final answer in rational number operations must be reduced to its absolute standard form (lowest terms) to be marked correct in DAV assessments.
• **Mixed Fraction Conversion:** Mixed fractions (e.g., $2 \frac{3}{4}$) must be converted into improper fractions ($\frac{11}{4}$) before executing multiplication, division, or sign-sensitive operations.
• **Preserving Signs in Mixed Fractions:** Special care is required with negative mixed numbers (e.g., $-2 \frac{3}{4}$), where the negative sign applies to the entire fractional and whole number quantity ($-\frac{11}{4}$).
• **Contextual Reasoning in DAV Exams:** DAV examination boards reward students who explicitly state mathematical properties (e.g., "By the Distributive Property...") during multi-step problem solving.

• **Master Glossary Term - Rational Number:** A number expressible in the form $\frac{p}{q}$ where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $q \neq 0$.
• **Master Glossary Term - Standard Form:** A rational number $\frac{p}{q}$ where $q > 0$ and $\text{HCF}(|p|, q) = 1$.
• **Master Glossary Term - Equivalent Rational Numbers:** Numbers representing the same proportional value, generated by multiplying or dividing numerator and denominator by the same non-zero integer.
• **Master Glossary Term - Absolute Value:** The magnitude of a rational number, representing its distance from zero regardless of sign.
• **Master Glossary Term - Additive Identity:** The number $0$, which leaves any rational number unchanged upon addition.
• **Master Glossary Term - Additive Inverse:** The negation of a rational number ($\frac{-a}{b}$), yielding $0$ when added to the original number.
• **Master Glossary Term - Multiplicative Identity:** The number $1$, which leaves any rational number unchanged upon multiplication.
• **Master Glossary Term - Multiplicative Inverse (Reciprocal):** The flipped fraction ($\frac{b}{a}$) that yields $1$ when multiplied by the original non-zero rational number.
• **Master Glossary Term - Terminating Decimal:** A decimal expansion that ends after a finite number of decimal places.
• **Master Glossary Term - Recurring Decimal:** A decimal expansion containing a digit or block of digits that repeats infinitely.
• **Master Glossary Term - Density Property:** The mathematical rule stating that between any two rational numbers, infinitely many other rational numbers exist.
• **Master Glossary Term - Closure Property:** The rule stating that an operation performed on two elements of a set always yields an element belonging to the same set.
• **Master Glossary Term - Commutative Property:** The rule stating that the order of operands does not change the result of addition or multiplication ($a + b = b + a$).
