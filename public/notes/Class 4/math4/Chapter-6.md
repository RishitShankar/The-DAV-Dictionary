• **Weight**: The measure of how heavy an object is, determined by the force of gravity acting upon its mass.
• **Standard Unit**: The **Kilogram (kg)** is the standard unit of weight used for measuring heavy objects.
• **Smaller Unit**: The **Gram (g)** is the standard unit used for measuring lighter objects.
• **Relationship**: 1 kilogram is exactly equal to 1000 grams (1 kg = 1000 g).
• **Conversion Rule**: To convert kilograms to grams, multiply the number of kilograms by 1000.
• **Reverse Conversion**: To convert grams to kilograms, divide the number of grams by 1000.
• **Measurement Tool**: A **Physical Balance** or **Beam Balance** is used to compare the weights of two objects.
• **Digital Scales**: Modern **Electronic Weighing Machines** provide precise digital readouts of weight in kg and g.
• **Spring Balance**: A device that uses a spring to measure the weight of an object by the extent of the spring's extension.
• **Common Objects**: Heavy items like sacks of rice, wheat, or a person’s body weight are measured in kilograms.
• **Light Objects**: Small items like gold ornaments, spices, or medicines are measured in grams.
• **Milligram (mg)**: A unit smaller than a gram, used for measuring very light substances like medicine doses.
• **Relationship**: 1 gram is equal to 1000 milligrams (1 g = 1000 mg).
• **Weight vs Mass**: While often used interchangeably in primary school, weight is the force exerted by gravity on an object's mass.
• **Estimation**: Developing the ability to estimate weight helps in understanding the scale of objects in daily life.

• **Addition of Weights**: Weights are added by grouping kilograms with kilograms and grams with grams.
• **Column Method**: Write weights in two columns, one for kg and one for g, to ensure accuracy.
• **Regrouping**: If the sum of grams is 1000 or more, convert the excess into kilograms and carry it over to the kg column.
• **Example**: Adding 2 kg 400 g and 3 kg 700 g results in 6 kg 100 g.
• **Subtraction of Weights**: Similar to addition, subtract grams from grams and kilograms from kilograms.
• **Borrowing**: If the grams in the top number are smaller than the grams in the bottom number, borrow 1 kg (1000 g) from the kg column.
• **Real-world Application**: Calculating the total weight of grocery items purchased from a market.
• **Difference Calculation**: Finding the difference in weight between two objects is essential for comparing their heaviness.
• **Precision**: Always ensure that the units are aligned correctly before performing any arithmetic operation.
• **Decimal Representation**: Weights can be written using decimals, such as 1.500 kg representing 1 kg 500 g.
• **Decimal Point**: The decimal point separates the kilograms (whole number) from the grams (fractional part).
• **Three-Digit Rule**: When writing grams in decimal form, always use three digits (e.g., 250 g is 0.250 kg).
• **Addition with Decimals**: Align the decimal points vertically before adding or subtracting weights.
• **Mental Math**: Practice converting small amounts like 500g to 0.5 kg to improve speed.
• **Consistency**: Always maintain the same unit throughout a calculation to avoid errors.

• **Standard Weights**: Metal weights used on a beam balance come in standard denominations like 5 kg, 2 kg, 1 kg, 500 g, 200 g, 100 g, and 50 g.
• **Combination**: Any weight can be measured by using a combination of these standard metal weights.
• **Balance Principle**: An object is weighed by placing it on one pan and adding standard weights to the other until the beam is horizontal.
• **Equilibrium**: When the beam is perfectly horizontal, the weight of the object equals the sum of the standard weights.
• **Accuracy**: The accuracy of a physical balance depends on the precision of the standard weights used.
• **Tare Weight**: The weight of the container or packaging is known as the 'tare' and must be subtracted to find the 'net weight'.
• **Gross Weight**: The total weight of the product plus the weight of its packaging.
• **Net Weight**: The actual weight of the contents inside a package (Gross Weight - Tare Weight).
• **Consumer Awareness**: Reading labels on packets helps in identifying the net weight of the product.
• **Standardization**: Weights and measures are regulated by government departments to ensure fair trade.
• **Calibration**: Weighing scales must be calibrated regularly to ensure they remain accurate over time.
• **Error Margin**: Small differences in weight can occur due to mechanical wear in older scales.
• **Digital Precision**: Electronic scales are preferred in retail because they eliminate human error in reading the balance.
• **Unit Labels**: Always check the unit label (kg or g) on a scale before recording a measurement.
• **Safety**: Never overload a weighing scale beyond its maximum capacity to prevent damage.
