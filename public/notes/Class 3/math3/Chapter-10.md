• **Capacity: Definition** – Capacity is the measure of the amount of liquid a container can hold when it is completely filled.
• **Liquid Measurement** – Unlike solids that are measured by length or weight, liquids take the shape of the container they are poured into.
• **Standard Units** – The standard unit for measuring the capacity of liquids is the **Litre (l)**.
• **Smaller Units** – For measuring smaller quantities of liquid, we use the **Millilitre (ml)**.
• **Relationship** – 1 Litre is equal to 1000 Millilitres (1 l = 1000 ml).
• **Conversion Rule** – To convert Litres into Millilitres, we multiply the number of litres by 1000.
• **Conversion Rule** – To convert Millilitres into Litres, we divide the number of millilitres by 1000.
• **Common Containers** – Measuring cylinders, beakers, and graduated jugs are tools used to measure capacity accurately.
• **Everyday Examples** – Milk, water, oil, petrol, and juice are common substances measured using capacity units.
• **Large Quantities** – Large volumes, such as the water in a swimming pool or a water tank, are measured in Litres.
• **Small Quantities** – Small volumes, such as medicine in a spoon or ink in a pen, are measured in Millilitres.
• **Standard Notation** – The symbol 'l' is used for Litre and 'ml' is used for Millilitre.
• **Visualizing Capacity** – A standard water bottle often holds 1 litre, while a small eye-dropper holds only a few millilitres.
• **Estimation** – Estimating capacity involves guessing how much liquid a container can hold before measuring it.
• **Precision** – Accurate measurement requires placing the container on a flat surface and reading the level at eye level.

• **Addition of Capacity** – Adding capacities involves adding litres to litres and millilitres to millilitres separately.
• **Column Addition** – When adding, write 'l' and 'ml' in separate columns to avoid confusion during calculation.
• **Regrouping** – If the sum of the millilitres column is 1000 or more, convert the excess into litres and carry it over to the litre column.
• **Example of Addition** – Adding 2l 400ml and 3l 300ml results in 5l 700ml.
• **Step-by-Step Addition** – Always start adding from the smallest unit, which is the millilitre side.
• **Carrying Over** – If the millilitre column totals 1200ml, write 200ml under the ml column and carry 1l to the litre column.
• **Subtraction of Capacity** – Subtraction involves taking away a smaller quantity from a larger quantity of liquid.
• **Column Subtraction** – Similar to addition, arrange litres and millilitres in distinct columns.
• **Borrowing** – If the millilitre value to be subtracted is greater than the available millilitre value, borrow 1 litre from the litre column.
• **Conversion during Borrowing** – Remember that 1 litre borrowed becomes 1000 millilitres when added to the millilitre column.
• **Verification** – After subtraction, the difference plus the smaller quantity should equal the original larger quantity.
• **Real-world Application** – Addition is used to find the total capacity of two different containers combined.
• **Real-world Application** – Subtraction is used to find how much liquid is left after some has been poured out or consumed.
• **Logical Sequence** – Always ensure the units are aligned correctly before performing any arithmetic operation.
• **Consistency** – Maintain the same unit throughout the calculation process to ensure accuracy.

• **Measuring Tools** – Standard measuring vessels are marked with graduations to indicate specific volumes.
• **Graduated Cylinder** – A tall, narrow container used in laboratories to measure precise volumes of liquid.
• **Measuring Jug** – A kitchen tool with markings for litres and millilitres, often used for cooking and baking.
• **Reading the Meniscus** – When reading a liquid level, look at the bottom of the curve (the meniscus) at eye level for accuracy.
• **Parallax Error** – Viewing the liquid level from above or below the meniscus leads to incorrect readings.
• **Standardized Containers** – Many commercial products like milk cartons or soda bottles come in standard sizes like 500ml or 1l.
• **Half Litre** – 500 millilitres is exactly half of a litre (1/2 l = 500 ml).
• **Quarter Litre** – 250 millilitres is a quarter of a litre (1/4 l = 250 ml).
• **Three-Quarter Litre** – 750 millilitres represents three-quarters of a litre (3/4 l = 750 ml).
• **Fractional Understanding** – Understanding fractions helps in measuring liquids when standard containers are not available.
• **Capacity vs. Volume** – Capacity refers to the potential of a container, while volume refers to the actual amount of liquid inside.
• **Full Capacity** – A container is at full capacity when it is filled to the brim.
• **Partial Capacity** – A container is at partial capacity when it is filled only to a certain mark.
• **Empty Container** – An empty container has a capacity but zero volume of liquid.
• **Safety** – Never overfill a container, as it may cause spills or damage to the vessel.

• **Comparison of Capacity** – Comparing capacities involves determining which container holds more or less liquid.
• **Direct Comparison** – If two containers are the same shape, the one with the higher liquid level holds more.
• **Indirect Comparison** – If containers are different shapes, use a standard measuring tool to find the exact capacity of each.
• **Greater Than** – Use the symbol '>' to represent that one capacity is larger than another.
• **Less Than** – Use the symbol '<' to represent that one capacity is smaller than another.
• **Equality** – Use the symbol '=' to show that two containers hold the same amount of liquid.
• **Ordering** – Arranging capacities from smallest to largest is called ascending order.
• **Ordering** – Arranging capacities from largest to smallest is called descending order.
• **Standardization** – To compare accurately, convert all values to the same unit (e.g., convert everything to millilitres).
• **Visual Estimation** – While useful, visual estimation is often inaccurate for containers of different designs.
• **The Role of Shape** – A tall, thin glass might hold the same amount as a short, wide bowl; never judge by height alone.
• **Mathematical Comparison** – 1500ml is greater than 1l because 1500ml > 1000ml.
• **Logical Deduction** – If Container A holds 2l and Container B holds 1500ml, Container A is larger.
• **Consistency in Units** – Always convert to the smaller unit (ml) when comparing values to avoid errors.
• **Practical Testing** – Pouring liquid from one container to another is the most reliable way to compare capacity physically.

• **Addition Strategy** – When adding 4l 600ml and 2l 700ml, add the ml first: 600 + 700 = 1300ml.
• **Conversion during Addition** – Since 1300ml = 1l 300ml, keep 300ml and carry 1l to the litre column.
• **Final Sum** – 4l + 2l + 1l (carried) = 7l. The final result is 7l 300ml.
• **Subtraction Strategy** – When subtracting 2l 300ml from 5l 100ml, you cannot subtract 300 from 100.
• **Borrowing Process** – Borrow 1l from 5l, leaving 4l. The 1l becomes 1000ml.
• **Adjusted Value** – Add the 1000ml to the existing 100ml to get 1100ml.
• **Final Difference** – Subtract 300ml from 1100ml to get 800ml, and 2l from 4l to get 2l. The result is 2l 800ml.
