• **Chapter Overview**: Welcome to the DAV Academic Notes for Class 7 Secondary Mathematics, Chapter 10: "Construction of Triangles", designed to provide an exhaustive theoretical and practical framework for geometric constructions.
• **Fundamental Goal**: The primary objective of this chapter is to enable students to construct various types of triangles accurately using standard geometric instruments based on given sets of measurements.
• **Essential Instruments**: Successful geometric construction requires a high-degree of precision using a ruler (scale), a pair of compasses, a protractor, and a sharp pencil for fine lines.
• **Role of the Ruler**: The ruler is used to draw straight line segments of specified lengths and to measure distances with precision up to millimeters.
• **Role of the Pair of Compasses**: The compass is an indispensable tool used for drawing arcs, circles, and marking equal lengths or equidistant points from a fixed center.
• **Role of the Protractor**: The protractor is utilized for measuring and drawing angles of exact degree measures when constructing triangles involving angle specifications.
• **Role of the Set-Squares and Divider**: While dividers help transfer lengths directly, set-squares assist in drawing parallel and perpendicular lines, supporting structural accuracy in geometric figures.
• **Prerequisite Knowledge**: Before constructing triangles, students must recall fundamental properties of triangles, including side-length relationships, angle sum properties, and types of triangles based on sides and angles.
• **Triangle Inequality Theorem**: A fundamental prerequisite is that the sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must always be strictly greater than the length of the third side.
• **Angle Sum Property**: The interior angles of any triangle always add up to exactly $180^\circ$, a crucial check when constructing triangles involving angle specifications.
• **Types of Triangles by Sides**: Triangles are categorized into Equilateral (all sides equal), Isosceles (two sides equal), and Scalene (all sides different) triangles, each dictating specific construction approaches.
• **Types of Triangles by Angles**: Triangles are also classified as Acute-angled, Right-angled, and Obtuse-angled triangles based on the nature of their interior angles.
• **Meaning of "Given Conditions"**: A triangle is uniquely determined when a specific, sufficient subset of its six components (three sides and three angles) is provided.
• **Insufficient Conditions**: Providing only the three angles of a triangle is insufficient for a unique construction because countless similar triangles of different sizes can be drawn.
• **Sufficient Conditions for Congruence and Construction**: Triangles can be uniquely constructed under four primary sets of given conditions, forming the core topics of this DAV chapter.

• **Core Condition 1 (SSS)**: The **SSS (Side-Side-Side) Criterion** states that a unique triangle can be constructed when the lengths of all three sides are explicitly given.
• **Core Condition 2 (SAS)**: The **SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Criterion** states that a unique triangle can be constructed when the lengths of two sides and the measure of the included angle are given.
• **Core Condition 3 (ASA)**: The **ASA (Angle-Side-Angle) Criterion** states that a unique triangle can be constructed when the measures of two angles and the length of the side included between them are given.
• **Core Condition 4 (RHS)**: The **RHS (Right-angle-Hypotenuse-Side) Criterion** states that a unique right-angled triangle can be constructed when the length of the hypotenuse and the length of one side (leg) are given.
• **Importance of Rough Sketches**: Every construction in this chapter must begin with a **Rough Sketch**, which acts as a visual roadmap indicating where each given measurement belongs.
• **Labeling Conventions**: In any triangle $ABC$, vertices are labeled with capital letters ($A, B, C$), and the sides opposite to these vertices are conventionally represented by corresponding small letters ($a, b, c$ or $BC, CA, AB$).
• **Step-by-Step Methodology**: DAV curriculum emphasizes writing systematic, sequential steps of construction alongside the geometric diagram to articulate the logical procedure followed.
• **Precision in Drawing**: Sharp pencil leads are mandatory because blunt pencils introduce thickness errors that accumulate and prevent geometric lines from intersecting at precise points.
• **Arc Intersection Technique**: Intersecting arcs drawn via compasses determine exact vertices of triangles, eliminating guesswork and ensuring mathematical accuracy.
• **Verification of Triangle Validity**: Before beginning an SSS construction, always check if the sum of the two smaller sides exceeds the largest side; otherwise, the triangle is non-existent.
• **Definition of SSS Construction**: The SSS method relies entirely on the intersection of two arcs of specific radii originating from the endpoints of a known baseline segment.
• **Base Selection in SSS**: For an SSS construction with sides $a$, $b$, and $c$, it is strategically advantageous to choose the longest side as the base segment to minimize steepness and construction errors.
• **Executing SSS Step 1**: Draw a line segment $AB$ equal to the first given side length using a properly calibrated ruler and a sharp pencil.
• **Executing SSS Step 2**: With center $A$ and radius equal to the second given side length, draw an arc above or below the line segment $AB$.
• **Executing SSS Step 3**: With center $B$ and radius equal to the third given side length, draw another arc to intersect the previously drawn arc at a common point, labeled as vertex $C$.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Executing SSS Step 4**: Join vertex $C$ to point $A$ and point $B$ using a straight-edge ruler to complete the required triangle $ABC$.
• **Final Verification in SSS**: Measure all three constructed sides with a scale to confirm they match the original problem parameters exactly.
• **Introduction to SAS Construction**: The **Side-Angle-Side (SAS)** criterion is deployed when two adjacent side lengths and the exact angle enclosed between them are specified.
• **Significance of "Included" Angle**: The term **Included Angle** means the vertex where the two given sides meet is precisely the vertex at which the given angle must be constructed.
• **Executing SAS Step 1**: Draw a baseline segment corresponding to one of the two given side lengths, labeling its endpoints clearly as $B$ and $C$.
• **Executing SAS Step 2**: At one of the endpoints (say, vertex $B$), use a protractor or compass-based angle construction method to draw a ray making the specified angle degree measure with the baseline.
• **Executing SAS Step 3**: Along this newly drawn ray, measure and mark off a length equal to the second given side using a compass or ruler, establishing the position of the third vertex $A$.
• **Executing SAS Step 4**: Connect the newly marked vertex $A$ to the remaining endpoint $C$ of the baseline to finalize the unique triangle $ABC$.
• **Role of Protractors in SAS**: When the angle given is a standard constructible angle (like $60^\circ, 90^\circ, 45^\circ$), compass constructions are preferred over protractors for maximum geometric precision.
• **Introduction to ASA Construction**: The **Angle-Side-Angle (ASA)** criterion governs the construction of a triangle when two interior angles and the side joining their vertices are provided.
• **Significance of "Included" Side in ASA**: The **Included Side** is the specific line segment that connects the two vertices at which the given angles are located.
• **Executing ASA Step 1**: Draw the baseline line segment corresponding to the length of the given included side, labeling its endpoints $X$ and $Y$.
• **Executing ASA Step 2**: At endpoint $X$, construct a ray making an angle equal to the first given angle measure using a protractor or compass.
• **Executing ASA Step 3**: At endpoint $Y$, construct another ray making an angle equal to the second given angle measure on the same side of the baseline segment.
• **Executing ASA Step 4**: Extend both newly drawn rays until they intersect each other at a single point, which automatically forms the third vertex $Z$ of the triangle.
• **Angle Sum Check in ASA**: Before completing an ASA construction, ensure that the sum of the two given angles is strictly less than $180^\circ$; otherwise, the rays will never intersect.

• **Introduction to RHS Construction**: The **Right-angled-Hypotenuse-Side (RHS)** criterion is a specialized case of SAS designed exclusively for right-angled triangles.
• **Anatomy of a Right Triangle**: A right-angled triangle consists of a **Hypotenuse** (the side opposite the $90^\circ$ right angle, which is always the longest side) and two perpendicular legs.
• **Conditions for RHS**: To construct a right-angled triangle using RHS, the length of the hypotenuse and the length of one of the remaining two perpendicular sides must be provided.
• **Executing RHS Step 1**: Draw a baseline segment equal to the given perpendicular side (leg) and label it clearly, for instance, $BC$.
• **Executing RHS Step 2**: At one of the endpoints (say, $B$), construct a perpendicular ray forming a $90^\circ$ angle with the baseline segment $BC$.
• **Executing RHS Step 3**: With the compass opened to a radius equal to the given **Hypotenuse** length, place the compass needle on the other endpoint ($C$) and draw an arc that intersects the perpendicular ray.
• **Executing RHS Step 4**: Label the point of intersection as vertex $A$, and join vertex $A$ to endpoint $C$ to complete the right-angled triangle $ABC$.
• **Why RHS Works**: The RHS criterion guarantees uniqueness because the fixed hypotenuse acts as a rigid boundary, forcing the third side into a unique geometric position once the right angle and leg are established.
• **Special Case - Equilateral Triangle**: Constructing an equilateral triangle requires only **one side length** because all three sides are equal and all interior angles are identically $60^\circ$.
• **Equilateral Construction Method**: Draw base $AB$, draw $60^\circ$ at $A$ and $B$ (or use SSS with all sides equal to length $s$), and find vertex $C$.
• **Special Case - Isosceles Triangle**: An isosceles triangle can be constructed if DAV problems provide DAV-specific combinations, such as the base and equal sides, or the vertex angle and equal sides.
• **Construction of Isosceles Triangle (Given Base and Equal Sides)**: This reduces directly to an **SSS construction** since all three side lengths (base, equal side, equal side) are known.
• **Construction of Isosceles Triangle (Given Base and Base Angles)**: This reduces directly to an **ASA construction** because the base is the included side, and the two base angles in an isosceles triangle are equal in measure.
• **Geometric Reasoning in Constructions**: Every construction step must be backed by Euclidean geometry theorems, ensuring students understand *why* a particular arc or angle placement yields the correct triangle.
• **Error Minimization Strategies**: To avoid cumulative drafting errors, students must keep compass hinges tight, pencils sharply pointed, and scales firmly held without slipping.

• **Advanced DAV Problem Types**: DAV examination questions frequently test conceptual depth by giving indirect measurements, such as perimeter and angle ratios, requiring preliminary calculations before construction.
• **Perimeter-Based Constructions**: If the perimeter and two base angles of a triangle are given, students must use advanced auxiliary construction techniques involving angle bisectors and perpendicular bisectors.
• **Angle Bisector Definition**: An **Angle Bisector** is a ray that divides an angle into two identical, congruent smaller angles, frequently used in advanced DAV triangle construction problems.
• **Perpendicular Bisector Definition**: A **Perpendicular Bisector** is a line perpendicular to a segment that divides it into two equal halves, passing through its exact midpoint.
• **Constructing a $60^\circ$ Angle**: To construct standard angles without a protractor, draw an arc from vertex, keep the same radius, and cut the arc to intersect at $60^\circ$.
• **Constructing a $120^\circ$ Angle**: Extending the $60^\circ$ arc intersection twice along a baseline circular arc yields a precise $120^\circ$ angle.
• **Constructing a $90^\circ$ Angle**: A $90^\circ$ angle is constructed by creating a perpendicular bisector or by bisecting the $180^\circ$ straight angle, or by bisecting the angle between $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$.
• **Constructing a $30^\circ$ Angle**: A $30^\circ$ angle is obtained by bisecting a standard $60^\circ$ constructed angle.
• **Constructing a $45^\circ$ Angle**: A $45^\circ$ angle is obtained by bisecting a standard $90^\circ$ right angle.
• **Constructing a $15^\circ$ Angle**: A $15^\circ$ angle is obtained by bisecting a constructed $30^\circ$ angle.
• **Constructing a $75^\circ$ Angle**: A $75^\circ$ angle is constructed by bisecting the angle formed between the $60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ angle marks.
• **Constructing a $105^\circ$ Angle**: A $105^\circ$ angle is constructed by bisecting the angle between the $90^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ angle marks.
• **Constructing a $135^\circ$ Angle**: A $135^\circ$ angle is constructed by bisecting the angle between $90^\circ$ and a straight $180^\circ$ extension.
• **Role of Compass Radius Consistency**: Never change the compass width (radius) while drawing arcs for angle bisectors or standard angle constructions, as doing so ruins geometric proportions.
• **Checking Feasibility**: Always verify whether angle measurements sum correctly and side lengths satisfy triangle inequalities before committing to paper.

• **Common Student Pitfalls (Drafting Inaccuracies)**: Slipping compass points, thick pencil lines, and misaligned zero marks on rulers are the primary sources of point deductions in DAV evaluations.
• **Remedy for Compass Slippage**: Pressing the compass needle gently into the paper while keeping the pencil lead slightly shorter or level with the needle prevents slipping.
• **Proper Use of Scale**: Always align the physical zero mark of the ruler with the starting point of a line segment rather than the physical edge of the plastic scale.
• **Importance of Cleanliness**: Erasure marks should be minimized; multiple overlapping construction arcs can confuse the final labeling of vertices $A, B,$ and $C$.
• **Final Labeling Protocol**: Once construction is complete, vertices must be labeled in capital block letters, and side lengths or angle values should be neatly noted alongside the drawing.
• **DAV Marking Scheme Breakdown**: DAV board examinations typically divide construction marks into three components: 1 mark for the rough sketch, 2 marks for the main construction, and 1 mark for the steps of construction.
• **Necessity of Steps of Construction**: Omitting the sequential **Steps of Construction** results in a loss of marks, even if the final geometric figure is drawn with absolute perfection.
• **Language of Steps**: Steps of construction must be written in clear, imperative mathematical English (e.g., "Step 1: Draw a line segment $XY = 5\text{ cm}$.").
• **Chronological Alignment**: The numbering in the written steps of construction must strictly correspond to the chronological order in which the geometric tool actions were performed on paper.
• **Real-World Applications**: The principles of triangle construction form the backbone of engineering, architecture, bridge building, and roof truss designs where structural rigidity is paramount.
• **Rigidity Property of Triangles**: Unlike quadrilaterals, which are flexible and unstable, triangles are **structurally rigid** because their shape cannot be changed without altering the lengths of their sides.
• **Trangulation in Surveying**: Surveyors use triangle construction principles to measure inaccessible distances and map land areas through a technique known as **Triangulation**.
• **Roof Truss Engineering**: Roof trusses utilize interconnected triangular frames because triangles distribute weight efficiently across their sides without collapsing under heavy loads.
• **Bridge Architecture**: Steel bridges incorporate networks of triangles in their side girders to maximize load-bearing capacity and resist twisting forces.

• **Comprehensive Review of SSS**: The **Side-Side-Side (SSS)** construction requires no angle measurements, depending entirely on intersecting circular arcs corresponding to side lengths $a, b,$ and $c$.
• **Degenerate Triangle Case**: If the sum of the two smaller side lengths in an SSS problem equals the largest side length, the triangle collapses into a straight line segment, known as a **degenerate triangle**.
• **Impossible Triangle Case**: If the sum of the two smaller side lengths is less than the largest side, the arcs fail to intersect, proving that such a triangle **cannot exist**.
• **Comprehensive Review of SAS**: The **Side-Angle-Side (SAS)** construction anchors two known side lengths with an angle explicitly trapped between them at their shared vertex.
• **Why Non-Included Angles Fail**: If the angle given in an SAS problem is *not* the included angle (i.e., it is adjacent to only one of the given sides), two different triangles can sometimes be formed, creating an ambiguous case.
• **Comprehensive Review of ASA**: The **Angle-Side-Angle (ASA)** construction creates a unique triangle by anchoring two angle rays at the endpoints of a common baseline segment.
• **AAS Derivation**: The **Angle-Angle-Side (AAS)** criterion is a direct corollary of ASA because knowing two angles automatically determines the third angle via the angle sum property ($180^\circ$).
• **Comprehensive Review of RHS**: The **Right-angled-Hypotenuse-Side (RHS)** construction is uniquely tailored for right-angled triangles where the hypotenuse and one leg dictate the geometry.
• **Why AAA Fails**: The **Angle-Angle-Angle (AAA)** criterion fails to construct a unique triangle because it guarantees only *similarity* (same shape) rather than *congruence* (same size).
• **Scaling in AAA**: Multiple triangles can share the exact same three interior angles while possessing vastly different side lengths, which is why AAA produces an infinite family of triangles.
• **Role of Auxiliary Lines**: In complex DAV problems, constructing auxiliary perpendiculars or angle bisectors transforms non-standard problems into standard SSS, SAS, ASA, or RHS formats.
• **Symmetry in Isosceles Triangles**: Isosceles triangle constructions exhibit line symmetry; drawing an altitude from the vertex angle bisects both the base and the vertical angle symmetrically.
• **Properties of Equilateral Triangles**: Equilateral triangles exhibit rotational symmetry of order 3 and three lines of reflectional symmetry, all intersecting at the geometric centroid.
• **Drafting Accuracy Check**: A well-constructed triangle must pass the "paper fold test" or instrument verification where side lengths match given dimensions within a millimeter tolerance.

• **Deep Dive into SSS Execution**: To reinforce SSS mastery, consider constructing $\triangle PQR$ where $PQ = 6\text{ cm}$, $QR = 7\text{ cm}$, and $PR = 8\text{ cm}$.
• **SSS Execution Setup**: Begin by drawing the longest side, $PR = 8\text{ cm}$, horizontally across the center of the drawing sheet to ensure ample space for intersecting arcs.
• **SSS Arc Placement**: Open the compass to radius $6\text{ cm}$ (for side $PQ$), place the needle at vertex $P$, and draw an arc above the baseline segment $PR$.
• **SSS Second Arc Placement**: Open the compass to radius $7\text{ cm}$ (for side $QR$), place the needle at vertex $R$, and draw a second intersecting arc to cut the first arc at point $Q$.
• **SSS Finalization**: Connect point $Q$ to $P$ and point $Q$ to $R$, then write down all four structured steps of construction to complete the DAV presentation standard.
• **Deep Dive into SAS Execution**: To reinforce SAS mastery, construct $\triangle XYZ$ where $XY = 5\text{ cm}$, $\angle Y = 60^\circ$, and $YZ = 7\text{ cm}$.
• **SAS Execution Setup**: Draw the baseline segment $YZ = 7\text{ cm}$ using a sharp pencil and a clean ruler.
• **SAS Angle Construction**: At endpoint $Y$, construct an angle of $60^\circ$ using a compass by drawing an arc from $Y$, cutting it with the same radius, and drawing a ray through the intersection.
• **SAS Side Measurement**: Measure $5\text{ cm}$ on the compass, place the needle at vertex $Y$, and cut an arc along the newly created $60^\circ$ ray to establish vertex $X$.
• **SAS Finalization**: Join vertex $X$ to endpoint $Z$ with a straight line, and formulate the sequential steps of construction detailing the angle and arc operations.
• **Deep Dive into ASA Execution**: To reinforce ASA mastery, construct $\triangle ABC$ where $BC = 6.5\text{ cm}$, $\angle B = 45^\circ$, and $\angle C = 75^\circ$.
• **ASA Execution Setup**: Draw the included base segment $BC = 6.5\text{ cm}$.
• **ASA Angle B at $45^\circ$**: At endpoint $B$, construct a $90^\circ$ angle and bisect it to obtain a precise $45^\circ$ ray extending upwards.
• **ASA Angle C at $75^\circ$**: At endpoint $C$, construct a $90^\circ$ angle, a $60^\circ$ angle, and bisect the $15^\circ$ gap between $60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ to obtain a precise $75^\circ$ ray.
• **ASA Intersection**: Extend the $45^\circ$ ray from $B$ and the $75^\circ$ ray from $C$ until they intersect at vertex $A$, completing the triangle construction.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Deep Dive into RHS Execution**: To reinforce RHS mastery, construct right-angled $\triangle LMN$ where right angle is at $M$, hypotenuse $LN = 7\text{ cm}$, and leg $MN = 4\text{ cm}$.
• **RHS Execution Setup**: Draw the given perpendicular leg $MN = 4\text{ cm}$ as a vertical or horizontal baseline segment.
• **RHS Right Angle Setup**: At endpoint $M$, construct a perpendicular $90^\circ$ ray using a compass.
• **RHS Hypotenuse Arc**: Set the compass width to $7\text{ cm}$ (the hypotenuse length), place the needle at vertex $N$, and draw an arc that intersects the perpendicular ray at vertex $L$.
• **RHS Finalization**: Connect vertex $L$ to endpoint $N$ to form the hypotenuse, finalizing the right-angled triangle $LMN$ along with its systematic steps of construction.

• **Advanced Challenge Topics**: High-achieving DAV students must be prepared for multi-step construction problems where triangles are embedded within quadrilaterals or composite polygons.
• **Constructing Quadrilaterals via Triangles**: Every quadrilateral can be decomposed into two distinct triangles by drawing a diagonal, meaning quadrilateral construction is fundamentally an application of multiple triangle constructions.
• **Constructing a Rhombus**: A rhombus can be constructed using its side length and one diagonal, which breaks the figure down into two congruent SSS triangles sharing a common diagonal base.
• **Constructing a Parallelogram**: A parallelogram can be constructed when two adjacent sides and the included angle are given, or when two adjacent sides and a diagonal are provided.
• **Constructing a Rectangle**: A rectangle requires only two adjacent side lengths (length and breadth) because all four interior angles are automatically known right angles ($90^\circ$).
• **Constructing a Square**: A square can be constructed using just a single side length since all sides are equal and all interior angles are right angles.
• **Using Scale Factors in Construction**: Advanced geometry problems sometimes require constructing a triangle similar to a given triangle with a specific scale factor, expanding DAV syllabus boundaries.
• **Locus Concepts in Construction**: A **Locus** is the path traced by a point that moves under a specific geometrical condition, often used when determining possible positions for triangle vertices.
• **Equidistance Locus**: The locus of a point equidistant from two fixed points is the perpendicular bisector of the line segment joining them.
• **Angle Bisector Locus**: The locus of a point equidistant from two intersecting lines is the pair of angle bisectors of the angles formed by those lines.
