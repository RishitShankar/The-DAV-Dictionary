• **Chapter Introduction:** Welcome to DAV Academic Notes for Class 7 Science, Chapter 14: "Fabric from Fibre," designed to provide an exhaustive, high-yield conceptual breakdown of textile science.
• **Core Definition of Fibre:** A fibre is defined as a very thin, long, flexible, thread-like strand or filament from which various types of yarns and fabrics are subsequently manufactured.
• **Core Definition of Yarn:** Yarn is a continuous, long strand of interlocked, twisted fibres that serves as the immediate precursor material used directly in weaving and knitting fabrics.
• **Core Definition of Fabric:** Fabric refers to any cloth or material produced by methods such as weaving, knitting, felting, or braiding interlocking networks of yarn.
• **Significance of Clothing:** Clothes are fundamental human necessities that protect our bodies from harsh climatic elements, physical injuries, insect bites, and ultraviolet solar radiation.
• **Historical Context of Clothing:** Primitive humans utilized large tree leaves, heavy animal pelts, and animal skins wrapped around their bodies to stay warm before the discovery of spinning and weaving.
• **Agricultural Evolution in Textiles:** With the advancement of human civilization and the advent of agriculture, people learned to utilize twigs, grass, and animal hair to weave mats, baskets, and simple garments.
• **Unstitched Era of Clothing:** Ancient civilizations like the early Indians and Egyptians primarily draped unstitched long sheets of cotton or linen fabrics around their bodies in various styles.
• **Invention of the Sewing Needle:** The invention of the bone and metal sewing needle marked a monumental technological leap, allowing humans to stitch tailored garments that fit body contours snugly.
• **Primary Classification of Fibres:** Fibres are broadly classified into two main categories based on their origin: Natural Fibres and Synthetic (Man-Made) Fibres.
• **Natural Fibres Definition:** Natural fibres are obtained directly from natural sources, specifically from plants and animals, without undergoing heavy chemical transformation.
• **Plant-Based Natural Fibres:** Examples of plant fibres include cotton, jute, flax (linen), hemp, coir, and bamboo, which are predominantly composed of plant cell wall material (cellulose).
• **Animal-Based Natural Fibres:** Examples of animal fibres include wool and silk, which are fundamentally composed of complex structural proteins.
• **Synthetic Fibres Definition:** Synthetic fibres are man-made polymer materials manufactured by scientists in chemical laboratories and industrial factories using petroleum-derived petrochemicals.
• **Examples of Synthetic Fibres:** Common synthetic fibres include nylon, polyester (e.g., terylene, terrycot), acrylic, and rayon (semi-synthetic), known for high durability and wrinkle resistance.
• **Advantages of Natural Fibres:** Natural fibres are generally highly breathable, absorb moisture and sweat efficiently, feel comfortable against the skin, and are biodegradable.
• **Advantages of Synthetic Fibres:** Synthetic fibres dry exceptionally fast, possess high tensile strength, do not wrinkle easily, resist insect attacks (like moths), and are highly durable.

• **Focus on Plant Fibres:** Plant fibres form the backbone of everyday textiles, providing cool, lightweight, and durable materials suited for tropical and temperate climates.
• **Cotton Overview:** Cotton is the world's most widely cultivated and utilized natural plant fibre, cherished for its softness, breathability, and versatility in daily wear.
• **Agricultural Requirements for Cotton:** Cotton plants thrive best in warm tropical or subtropical climates requiring abundant sunshine, moderate rainfall (or irrigation), and fertile black or alluvial soil.
• **Geographical Distribution in India:** In India, cotton is extensively cultivated in states with rich black soil, prominently including Gujarat, Maharashtra, Punjab, Haryana, and Madhya Pradesh.
• **Anatomy of a Cotton Plant:** The cotton plant is a bushy shrub that bears small, yellow-white flowers which eventually transform into green, spherical seed pods known as cotton bolls.
• **Maturation of Cotton Bolls:** As the cotton bolls mature and ripen under the sun, they swell, burst open, and expose fluffy, white masses of cotton fibres enclosing tiny seeds within.
• **Ginning Definition:** Ginning is the traditional and industrial mechanical process of separating raw cotton fibres from their hard, embedded seeds using specialized metal combs or machine rollers.
• **Historical Ginning Methods:** Historically, the ginning of cotton was performed entirely by hand through tedious manual labor, which was later revolutionized by Eli Whitney’s mechanical cotton gin.
• **Baling Ginned Cotton:** Once separated from seeds, the loose ginned cotton is compressed tightly into massive, rectangular industrial bundles called bales for easy transportation to textile mills.
• **Carding Process:** Carding is the mechanical procedure where ginned cotton fibres are passed through fine wire-toothed combs to straighten them out, remove residual impurities, and form a continuous web.
• **Conversion to Sliver:** After carding, the aligned cotton fibres are gathered into a soft, untwisted rope-like strand called a sliver, which is ready for the spinning stage.
• **Spinning Definition:** Spinning is the crucial manufacturing process of drawing out, drafting, and twisting together fibres from a sliver to form a strong, continuous yarn.
• **Traditional Spinning Devices:** Historically, spinning was performed manually using primitive hand spindles (takli) and the traditional Indian spinning wheel, known as the charkha.
• **Industrial Spinning Machines:** In modern industrial textile factories, massive, high-speed spinning machines process thousands of meters of yarn simultaneously with minimal human intervention.
• **Significance of the Charkha:** The charkha holds deep historical and cultural significance in India, having been popularized by Mahatma Gandhi as a symbol of self-reliance (Swadeshi) during the freedom movement.
• **Yarn to Fabric Conversion:** Once high-strength yarn is successfully spun on spools or bobbins, it is transported to weavers and knitting units to be transformed into finished fabrics.

• **Jute Overview:** Jute is a rough, durable, and cost-effective natural vegetable fibre often referred to colloquially in the textile industry as the "Golden Fibre" due to its shiny, brownish-gold appearance.
• **Source of Jute Fibre:** Jute fibre is extracted exclusively from the inner bast (phloem) layer of the stem of the jute plant, scientifically belonging to the genus *Corchorus*.
• **Climatic and Soil Conditions for Jute:** Jute cultivation requires a hot, humid climate, heavy monsoon rainfall, and fresh, fertile alluvial soil, which is predominantly found in river delta regions.
• **Major Global Jute Producer:** India and Bangladesh are the world's leading producers of raw jute and jute-based products, owing to the fertile plains of the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta.
• **Harvesting Jute Plants:** Jute plants are typically harvested at the flowering stage, which yields the best quality and strongest fibre before the stems become too woody.
• **Retting Definition:** Retting is the crucial biological process where harvested jute plant bundles are submerged and steeped in stagnant pools of water for several days.
• **Role of Microorganisms in Retting:** During retting, naturally occurring water bacteria decompose and rot the soft cellular plant tissues and sticky gums holding the fibres together.
• **Fibre Extraction (Stripping):** Once retting is complete, the softened outer bark is peeled away, and the clean jute fibres are forcefully stripped out from the woody stalk by hand.
• **Washing and Drying Jute:** The extracted jute fibres are thoroughly washed in clean flowing water to remove residual dirt and bark particles, and then dried in the sun by hanging them on bamboo racks.
• **Processing Jute Yarn:** Dried jute fibres are graded, combed, treated with batching oil to soften them, and subsequently spun into coarse, strong jute yarns used for heavy-duty applications.
• **Uses of Jute Fabric:** Jute is extensively utilized to manufacture heavy sacks, gunny bags, durable ropes, carpets, shopping bags, decorative tapestries, and eco-friendly geo-textiles.
• **Eco-Friendly Nature of Jute:** Jute is 100% biodegradable, sustainable, and environment-friendly, making it an excellent natural substitute for synthetic plastic packaging materials.
• **Flax (Linen) Introduction:** Flax is another important ancient plant fibre obtained from the stem of the flax plant (*Linum usitatissimum*), yielding a strong, highly absorbent fabric known as linen.
• **Characteristics of Linen:** Linen fabrics are exceptionally cool in hot weather, crisp in appearance, become softer with every wash, and dry much faster than cotton.
• **Coir Fibre Origin:** Coir is a coarse, stiff, natural lignocellulosic fibre extracted from the thick, fibrous outer husk of the coconut fruit (*Cocos nucifera*).
• **Uses of Coir:** Coir is predominantly used to manufacture resilient floor mats, doormats, brushes, mattress padding, and packaging materials due to its exceptional resistance to saltwater damage.

• **Focus on Animal Fibres:** Animal fibres are natural, protein-based textile materials harvested from specialized body coverings of specific mammals, providing exceptional thermal insulation.
• **Wool Overview:** Wool is a natural protein fibre obtained primarily from the thick, fluffy fleece (hair coat) of sheep, as well as other fleece-bearing animals.
• **Sources Beyond Sheep:** In addition to domestic sheep, high-quality luxury wool is obtained from goats (cashmere and angora), camels, alpacas, llamas, and yaks living in cold mountainous regions.
• **Cashmere Wool:** Cashmere is an ultra-soft, luxurious, and lightweight wool obtained from the underfur of the Cashmere goat native to the high-altitude Himalayan regions of Kashmir, Tibet, and Mongolia.
• **Angora Wool:** Angora wool is exceptionally soft, fluffy, and warm, harvested specifically from the long, silky hair coats of Angora rabbits.
• **Alpaca and Llama Wool:** Alpacas and llamas, native to the high Andes mountains of South America, provide fine, durable, and hypoallergenic wool used for premium winter garments.
• **Yak Wool:** Yaks living in the freezing sub-zero plateaus of Ladakh and Tibet possess a dense, coarse outer coat and a soft, insulating undercoat used by nomads to weave tents and warm clothing.
• **Geographical Adaptability of Wool:** Sheep and other wool-bearing mammals thrive in diverse climates, ranging from arid temperate plains to freezing alpine zones, adapting their coats accordingly.
• **Body Heat Insulation Mechanism:** Wool fibres are naturally crimped and wavy, which traps large volumes of stationary air between the strands, acting as a superb thermal insulator against freezing cold.
• **Shearing Definition:** Shearing is the painless process of removing the thick fleece of a sheep along with a very thin layer of dead skin cells, usually performed manually or with electric clippers during spring.
• **Why Shear in Spring:** Sheep are sheared in warm spring weather so that they do not suffer from heat exhaustion, and they have sufficient time to regrow a protective fleece coat before winter arrives.
• **Scouring Definition:** Scouring is the industrial process of thoroughly washing sheared raw wool in tanks filled with warm soapy water and detergent to remove grease, sweat, dust, and dirt.
• **Sorting Process:** After scouring and drying, the clean wool is graded and sorted by trained workers based on fibre length, color, texture, and cleanliness into different quality bins.
• **Carding and Combing Wool:** The sorted wool fibres are passed through carding and combing machines to detangle the strands, remove minor residual burrs, and align them uniformly.
• **Dyeing Wool Fibres:** Since natural wool is typically off-white, brown, or black, the fibres are dyed in vibrant color vats before being spun into colorful yarns.
• **Spinning Wool Yarn:** The carded, combed, and dyed wool fibres are finally drawn out and spun into bouncy, warm woolen yarns used for knitting sweaters, scarves, caps, and weaving blankets.

• **Silk Overview:** Silk is a luxurious, natural animal protein fibre renowned universally for its extraordinary lustrous sheen, smooth texture, incredible tensile strength, and graceful drape.
• **Source of Silk Fibre:** Silk is harvested from the continuous protein filament spun by the larvae of specific insects, most notably the domesticated mulberry silkworm, *Bombyx mori*.
• **Sericulture Definition:** Sericulture is the specialized, scientifically managed agricultural practice of breeding, cultivating, and rearing silkworms specifically for the commercial production of raw silk.
• **Life Cycle of the Silkworm:** The silkworm undergoes a complete metamorphosis involving four distinct biological life stages: Egg, Larva (caterpillar), Pupa (chrysalis), and Adult Moth.
• **Stage 1: Egg Production:** Female silk moths lay hundreds of tiny eggs on clean mulberry leaves during summer, which are carefully incubated by farmers until they hatch into tiny caterpillars.
• **Stage 2: The Caterpillar Larva:** Newly hatched caterpillars are voracious eaters; they spend their entire larval stage consuming fresh, tender mulberry leaves day and night, growing rapidly in size.
• **Specialized Glands in Caterpillars:** As caterpillars mature, two specialized internal silk-producing salivary glands run along their bodies, secreting a thick, viscous liquid protein.
• **Stage 3: Spinning the Cocoon:** When ready to enter the pupa stage, the caterpillar secretes the liquid protein through a spinneret near its mouth, which hardens instantly upon contact with air.
• **Construction of the Cocoon:** The caterpillar weaves this continuous protein filament around itself in a figure-eight motion, forming a protective, oval-shaped capsule called a cocoon.
• **Nature of Silk Protein:** The silk filament is composed of two main structural proteins: fibroin (the tough core fibre) and sericin (a sticky water-soluble gum holding the outer layers together).
• **Stage 4: Metamorphosis and Pupa:** Inside the protective silk cocoon, the caterpillar transforms into an inactive pupa, undergoing a complex biological reorganization into an adult silk moth.
• **Commercial Processing Intervention:** In commercial sericulture, cocoons are harvested before the adult moth emerges and breaks the continuous filament by secreting an alkaline fluid.
• **Stifling the Cocoons:** Harvested cocoons are subjected to heat treatment—either by steaming, sun-drying, or boiling—to kill the developing pupa inside, preventing it from damaging the silk thread.
• **Reeling the Silk:** Reeling is the delicate industrial process of unwinding the long, continuous silk filament from softened cocoons by dissolving the outer sericin gum in hot water baths.
• **Combining Filaments:** During reeling, typically 3 to 10 individual silk filaments are pooled and twisted together simultaneously to form a single, strong commercial raw silk thread.
• **Spinning and Weaving Silk:** The reeled raw silk threads are twisted, cleaned, and sent to power looms or handlooms to be woven into exquisite fabrics like chiffon, satin, organza, and brocade.

• **Synthetic Fibres Overview:** Synthetic fibres are entirely man-made textile polymers synthesized by chemists in industrial laboratories using basic raw materials derived from petroleum (crude oil).
• **Polymerization Concept:** Synthetic fibres are built from massive giant molecules called polymers, which are created by chemically linking thousands of smaller, identical repeating units called monomers.
• **Nylon Characteristics:** Nylon is the first fully synthetic fibre ever invented (1935), known for being exceptionally strong, elastic, lightweight, lustrous, wrinkle-resistant, and quick-drying.
• **Applications of Nylon:** Due to its immense tensile strength, nylon is widely used to manufacture climbing ropes, fishing nets, parachutes, toothbrush bristles, sportswear, and mechanical gear components.
• **Polyester Overview:** Polyester is currently the most widely produced synthetic fibre globally, synthesized by reacting petroleum-derived chemical compounds under high heat and pressure.
• **Characteristics of Polyester:** Polyester fabrics maintain their crisp shape well, resist shrinking and stretching, dry rapidly, and are completely immune to damage from molds and moths.
• **Popular Polyester Blends:** Polyester is frequently blended with natural fibres to create hybrid fabrics, such as 'Terrycot' (polyester mixed with cotton) and 'Terrywool' (polyester mixed with wool).
• **Acrylic Fibre (Artificial Wool):** Acrylic is a lightweight, soft, and warm synthetic fibre manufactured from petroleum derivatives, serving as an affordable and durable substitute for natural wool.
• **Rayon (Artificial Silk):** Rayon is a semi-synthetic fibre manufactured by chemically processing natural wood pulp (cellulose), possessing a lustrous shine and drape resembling natural silk.
• **Advantages of Synthetic Fibres:** Synthetic fibres do not wrinkle easily, wash and dry with extreme ease, are highly durable, cost-effective, and immune to biological degradation by insects.

• **Fabric Manufacturing Techniques:** Once yarns are successfully produced, they must be converted into wearable fabrics using two primary foundational textile techniques: Weaving and Knitting.
• **Weaving Definition:** Weaving is the systematic mechanical process of arranging two distinct sets of yarn strands interlaced at right angles to each other to form a coherent sheet of fabric.
• **The Loom Definition:** Weaving is performed on specialized machines and wooden frames called looms, which hold one set of yarns taut while interlacing the crossing set through them.
• **Warp Yarns:** The longitudinal (vertical) set of yarns held under high tension on a loom frame during the weaving process is officially referred to as the warp.
• **Weft (Filling) Yarns:** The transverse (horizontal) set of yarns that is shuttled back and forth alternately over and under the warp yarns is called the weft or filling.
• **Handlooms vs. Powerlooms:** Handlooms are operated entirely by human physical labor through foot pedals and hand shuttles, whereas powerlooms are heavy-duty industrial machines driven by electric motors.
• **Knitting Definition:** Knitting is the textile manufacturing method of producing fabric by interlocking a series of connected loops of a single continuous yarn using knitting needles or machines.
• **Difference in Structure:** While weaving uses two perpendicular sets of intersecting yarns, knitting relies on interlocking loops of a single continuous yarn strand, making it stretchier.
• **Applications of Knitting:** Knitting is predominantly utilized to manufacture stretchy garments such as socks, woolen sweaters, scarves, gloves, caps, and snug thermal undergarments.
• **Felting Method:** Felting is an ancient non-woven fabric-making technique where loose animal wool fibres are matted, condensed, and pressed together using moisture, heat, and mechanical friction.
• **Crocheting Method:** Crocheting is a handicraft method of creating fabric by interlocking loops of yarn using a specialized hooked needle (crochet hook), producing intricate patterned textiles.
• **Difference Between Yarn and Fabric:** Yarn is a continuous linear strand of twisted fibres, whereas fabric is a two-dimensional sheet-like web of interlaced or interlooped yarns.
• **Porosity of Fabrics:** Woven and knitted fabrics contain microscopic air spaces between intersecting yarns, which dictates their breathability, wind resistance, and thermal insulation capacity.
• **Role of Friction in Yarns:** The structural strength and integrity of yarn rely entirely on the microscopic surface friction between individual overlapping fibres when twisted together.
• **Post-Weaving Processing:** Newly manufactured raw fabrics undergo finishing treatments, including bleaching, dyeing, printing with patterns, and chemical anti-shrinkage treatments before commercial sale.

• **History of Clothing Materials:** Ancient humans relied heavily on natural environmental protective coverings before discovering the art of spinning, weaving, and sewing tailored garments.
• **Stone Age Clothing:** Prehistoric humans utilized cave paintings, animal hides, furs, and large leaves to shield themselves from freezing temperatures and rain during the Stone Age.
• **Discovery of Flax and Cotton:** Archaeological evidence reveals that ancient Egyptians cultivated flax along the banks of the Nile River as early as 5000 BCE to weave fine linen cloth.
• **Early Indian Cotton Cultivation:** The inhabitants of the ancient Indus Valley Civilization were among the earliest global pioneers to cultivate cotton and weave sophisticated garments.
• **Introduction of Stitched Clothes:** With the invention of bone needles around 30,000 years ago, humans began piercing and stitching animal skins and woven cloths to create fitted garments.
• **Evolution of Loom Technology:** Loom technology evolved from simple backstrap looms used in ancient civilizations to complex automated powerlooms driving the modern global textile industry.
• **Traditional Indian Textiles:** India has a rich, unbroken heritage of traditional handloom textiles, including Banarasi silk, Kanjeevaram, Chanderi, Pashmina, and Khadi cotton fabrics.
• **Significance of Khadi:** Khadi represents handspun and handwoven cotton cloth that became a powerful economic and political symbol of self-reliance in India's struggle for independence.
• **Industrial Revolution Impact:** The Industrial Revolution in the 18th and 19th centuries replaced cottage-industry hand spinning and weaving with steam-powered factories, mass-producing textiles.
• **Modern Technical Textiles:** Modern textile engineering has expanded beyond clothing into technical textiles used in civil engineering, aerospace, medicine, sports gear, and automotive manufacturing.

• **Properties of Natural vs. Synthetic Fibres:** A thorough comparative analysis of textile fibres reveals distinct advantages and drawbacks suited for different domestic and industrial applications.
• **Moisture Absorption Comparison:** Natural fibres (cotton, wool, silk) absorb moisture and sweat efficiently, whereas synthetic fibres (nylon, polyester) repel water and dry remarkably quickly.
• **Thermal Regulation Comparison:** Wool and silk provide superior thermal insulation in cold weather, while cotton offers cooling breathability in hot, humid tropical climates.
• **Durability and Strength Comparison:** Synthetic fibres possess significantly higher tensile strength and abrasion resistance compared to most natural fibres, making them extremely long-lasting.
• **Wrinkle Resistance Comparison:** Synthetic polymers resist creasing and wrinkling naturally, whereas natural plant fibres like cotton and linen require frequent ironing to maintain a crisp look.
• **Environmental Impact of Natural Fibres:** Natural fibres are inherently biodegradable, breaking down safely in soil without polluting ecosystems, though agriculture requires land and water resources.
• **Environmental Impact of Synthetic Fibres:** Synthetic fibres are derived from non-renewable petroleum resources and are non-biodegradable, posing severe long-term plastic pollution challenges in landfills and oceans.
• **Care and Maintenance of Cotton:** Cotton fabrics require washing in warm or cold water, tolerate ironing at high temperatures, but are susceptible to mildew if stored damp.
• **Care and Maintenance of Wool:** Wool garments require gentle hand washing or dry cleaning with mild detergents in cold water to prevent shrinking, felting, and fiber damage.
• **Care and Maintenance of Silk:** Silk fabrics require delicate handling, dry cleaning or gentle hand washing with neutral pH soaps, and must be dried in the shade away from direct sunlight.
• **Care and Maintenance of Synthetics:** Synthetic fabrics wash easily, dry rapidly, do not require ironing, but can melt or scorch easily under high heat or open flames.
