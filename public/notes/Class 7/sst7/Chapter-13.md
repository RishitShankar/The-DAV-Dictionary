• **Subject and Grade Context**: Welcome to the DAV Class 7 Social Science study notes for "We and Our World," specifically focusing on Chapter 13: "Delhi Sultanate."
• **Historical Timeline**: The period of the Delhi Sultanate spans from 1206 CE to 1526 CE, marking a crucial era of medieval Indian history characterized by the establishment of Muslim rule in northern India.
• **Geographical Significance**: Delhi emerged as the nerve center of political power, trade, and culture during the medieval period due to its strategic location between the Yamuna River and the Aravalli hills.
• **Primary Sources of History**: Historians reconstruct the history of the Delhi Sultanate using various archaeological and literary sources, including coins, inscriptions, and monumental architecture.
• **Inscriptions and Epigraphy**: Stone and copperplate inscriptions from the Sultanate period provide invaluable details about administrative decrees, royal grants, and genealogies of rulers.
• **Numismatics (Coins)**: The study of *Jitals* (copper coins) and *Tankas* (silver coins) minted during the Sultanate period helps historians understand the economic conditions, trade, and monetary systems of the era.
• **Monumental Architecture**: Architectural marvels such as the Qutb Minar, Alai Darwaza, and various mosques serve as physical evidence of the technological advancements and aesthetic preferences of the era.
• **Literary Sources**: Written records form the backbone of Delhi Sultanate historiography, offering first-hand accounts of court life, military campaigns, and administrative structures.
• **Tawarikh (Chronicles)**: *Tawarikh* (singular: *Tarikh*) were Persian chronicles written by learned scholars, courtiers, and secretaries who lived in the cities and rarely in the villages.
• **Role of Chroniclers**: Court chroniclers often wrote histories in the hope of receiving rich rewards from the Sultans, which means their accounts must be read critically while understanding their biases.
• **Minhaj-i-Siraj**: A prominent 13th-century chronicler who wrote extensively about the reigns of early Delhi Sultans, including the challenges faced by the rulers.
• **Ziauddin Barani**: A celebrated political thinker and historian of the 14th century whose works, such as *Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi*, provide deep insights into the administrative policies of Muhammad bin Tughlaq and Firoz Shah Tughlaq.
• **Ibn Battuta**: The famous 14th-century Moroccan traveler and explorer whose travelogue, *Rihla*, offers vivid descriptions of social life, trade, and administration under Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
• **Foreign Accounts**: Descriptions left by foreign visitors like Ibn Battuta act as an external window into the realities of everyday life, justice systems, and urban centers in the Sultanate.
• **Persian Language**: Persian served as the official court language of the Delhi Sultanate, significantly influencing the administrative vocabulary and literary traditions of medieval India.

• **Five Dynasties**: The Delhi Sultanate is broadly classified into five successive dynasties that ruled Delhi over a span of more than three centuries.
• **Mnemonic for Dynasties**: A helpful historical aid to remember the order of dynasties is the phrase: "Gul-Khile-Toh-Sans-Lo" (Slave, Khilji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi).
• **Slave Dynasty (1206–1290 CE)**: Also known as the Mamluk dynasty, it was founded by Qutb-ud-din Aibak, a trusted general of Muhammad Ghori.
• **Meaning of Mamluk**: The word 'Mamluk' is an Arabic term meaning "owned" or "slave," referring to military slaves who were trained for high-ranking administrative and military duties.
• **Qutb-ud-din Aibak (1206–1210 CE)**: The founder of the Slave dynasty who earned the title *Lakh Baksh* (giver of lakhs) due to his immense generosity and charitable nature.
• **Construction of Qutb Minar**: Aibak initiated the construction of the famous Qutb Minar in Delhi, dedicated to the Sufi saint Khwaja Qutb-ud-din Bakhtiyar Kaki.
• **Shams-ud-din Iltutmish (1211–1236 CE)**: Considered the real consolidator of the Delhi Sultanate, he saved Delhi from the impending Mongol invasions of Genghis Khan by refusing shelter to the Shah of Khwarazm.
• **Introduction of the Tanka and Jital**: Iltutmish standardized the currency system of the Sultanate by introducing the silver *Tanka* and the copper *Jital*.
• **The Chalisa (Group of Forty)**: Iltutmish organized a loyal corps of elite Turkish slave nobles known as *Turkan-i-Chahalgani* to assist him in administration.
• **Razia Sultan (1236–1240 CE)**: The daughter of Iltutmish who ascended the throne, becoming the first and only Muslim woman ruler of medieval India.
• **Challenges Faced by Razia**: Razia faced severe opposition from Turkish nobles who resented a woman exercising sovereign power and challenging patriarchal norms.
• **Ghiyas-ud-din Balban (1266–1287 CE)**: A powerful ruler of the Slave dynasty who restored royal prestige and absolute authority of the crown after years of political instability.
• **Theory of Kingship**: Balban believed that the Sultan was the "Shadow of God on Earth" (*Niyabat-i-Khudai*) and that royal power was divine in origin.
• **Sijda and Paibos**: Balban introduced strict court etiquettes such as *Sijda* (prostration) and *Paibos* (kissing the monarch's feet) to emphasize the absolute superiority of the Sultan over the nobles.
• **Iron and Blood Policy**: Balban adopted the stern "Policy of Iron and Blood" to suppress internal rebellions and secure the north-western frontiers against Mongol raids.

• **Khilji Dynasty (1290–1320 CE)**: The overthrow of the Slave dynasty by Jalal-ud-din Khilji marked the beginning of the Khilji imperial phase, characterized by aggressive territorial expansion.
• **Ala-ud-din Khilji (1296–1316 CE)**: The most powerful and ambitious ruler of the Khilji dynasty who transformed the Sultanate into an empire through widespread military conquests.
• **Imperialist Ambitions**: Ala-ud-din dreamed of conquering the world like Alexander the Great (*Sikandar-i-Sani*) and also sought to bring the entire Indian subcontinent under central control.
• **Conquest of Northern India**: Under Ala-ud-din, his armies successfully annexed prosperous regions like Gujarat, Ranthambore, Mewar, Malwa, and parts of Rajputana.
• **Southern Campaigns**: Ala-ud-din was the first Delhi Sultan to send military expeditions into southern India, led by his trusted general Malik Kafur.
• **Nature of Southern Conquests**: Unlike northern territories, the southern kingdoms (Devagiri, Warangal, Hoysalas, and Madurai) were not annexed directly; instead, they were forced to pay heavy annual tributes and accept the Sultan's suzerainty.
• **Drain of Wealth**: The massive wealth plundered from southern military campaigns poured into Delhi, financing Ala-ud-din's vast standing army and ambitious economic reforms.
• **Permanent Standing Army**: Ala-ud-din maintained a large, professional standing army, doing away with the traditional feudal levies of local chiefs.
• **Chehra and Dagh System**: To prevent fraud, Ala-ud-din introduced the *Chehra* (descriptive roll of individual soldiers) and the *Dagh* (branding of horses) systems.
• **Market Control and Price Regulation**: To maintain a large army on modest salaries, Ala-ud-din fixed the prices of essential commodities, ranging from food grains to horses and slaves.
• **Strict Enforcement**: The Sultan appointed special market officers called *Shahna-i-Mandi* and relied on secret intelligence agents (*Munhiyan*) to ensure merchants adhered strictly to price controls.
• **Land Revenue Reforms**: Ala-ud-din measured cultivated land and fixed the state share at 50 percent of the total produce, collected directly by royal officials.
• **Taxes Imposed**: In addition to land revenue, he levied a house tax (*Gharhi*) and a pasture tax (*Charai*) on cattle.
• **Control Over Nobility**: To prevent conspiracies, Ala-ud-din banned the social gatherings of nobles, prohibited the consumption of alcohol, and restricted inter-marriages among elite families without royal permission.
• **Legacy of Khilji Rule**: Although ruthless and authoritarian, Ala-ud-din's administrative and economic measures ensured domestic peace and shielded the economy from inflationary pressures.

• **Tughlaq Dynasty (1320–1414 CE)**: Founded by Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq after defeating the last Khilji ruler, this dynasty witnessed both the maximum geographical extent and the rapid fragmentation of the Delhi Sultanate.
• **Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq (1320–1325 CE)**: An experienced military commander who adopted a conciliatory policy toward nobles, repaired irrigation canals, and restored order in administration.
• **Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351 CE)**: One of the most fascinating, erudite, and controversial rulers of medieval India, known for his grand intellectual pursuits and disastrous administrative experiments.
• **Scholarly Pursuits**: Muhammad bin Tughlaq was deeply well-versed in philosophy, mathematics, astronomy, Persian literature, and theology.
• **Transfer of Capital (1327 CE)**: In an effort to centrally govern the expanding empire, Muhammad bin Tughlaq ordered the transfer of his capital from Delhi to Daulatabad (Devagiri) in the Deccan.
• **Outcome of Capital Transfer**: While Daulatabad was centrally located, forcing the entire population, including nobles and Sufi saints, to march hundreds of miles caused immense hardship, death, and widespread resentment.
• **Return to Delhi**: Realizing the strategic mistake of abandoning the north-western frontiers to Mongol incursions, the Sultan ordered the population to return to Delhi a few years later.
• **Token Currency Experiment (1329–1330 CE)**: Due to a shortage of silver and high costs of military campaigns, the Sultan introduced bronze and copper coins with the same face value as silver *Tankas*.
• **Failure of Token Currency**: The state failed to maintain a monopoly over metal minting; people easily counterfeited coins at home using local metals and paid their taxes in fake currency, forcing the Sultan to withdraw them in exchange for gold and silver.
• **Taxation in Doab**: The Sultan increased land revenue in the fertile Ganga-Yamuna Doab region during a severe famine, driving desperate peasants to abandon their lands and flee to forests.
• **Khurasan and Qarachil Expeditions**: Planned military campaigns to conquer Khurasan (Persia) and the Himalayan foothills (Qarachil) resulted in heavy financial losses and loss of soldiers due to harsh terrain and logistical failures.
• **"A Man of Contrasts"**: Historians view Muhammad bin Tughlaq as a ruler ahead of his time, whose brilliant conceptual ideas failed due to poor execution, lack of patience, and rigid temperament.
• **Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388 CE)**: The cousin and successor of Muhammad bin Tughlaq who focused on welfare measures, public works, and pacifying disgruntled nobles and religious leaders.
• **Concessionary Policies**: Firoz Shah wrote off loans, restored the *Iqta* system to hereditary status, and abolished numerous harsh and illegal taxes (*Abwabs*).
• **Public Works of Firoz Shah**: He excavated a network of irrigation canals (such as the Yamuna and Sutlej canals), founded new towns like Firozabad and Hisar, and planted extensive gardens.

• **Decline of the Tughlaq Dynasty**: Following the death of Firoz Shah Tughlaq, the central authority collapsed rapidly due to weak successors, regional rebellions, and devastating foreign invasions.
• **Invasion of Timur (1398 CE)**: The Turco-Mongol conqueror Timur Lang invaded India, sacking towns, plundering accumulated wealth, and leaving Delhi in absolute ruins and starvation.
• **Sayyid Dynasty (1414–1451 CE)**: Founded by Khizr Khan, who claimed to represent the Mongol ruler Timur, this dynasty ruled over a vastly diminished and weak Delhi state.
• **Nature of Sayyid Rule**: The Sayyid rulers struggled constantly against internal factions, rebellious provincial governors, and hostile neighboring kingdoms, exercising very little actual authority.
• **Lodi Dynasty (1451–1526 CE)**: Founded by Bahlul Lodi, this marked the final phase of the Delhi Sultanate and the first instance of Afghan rule in northern India.
• **Bahlul Lodi (1451–1489 CE)**: A pragmatic Afghan chieftain who treated his nobles as equals (*Masnad-i-Ali*) rather than subordinates, successfully restoring some degree of order and capturing Jaunpur.
• **Sikandar Lodi (1489–1517 CE)**: The most capable Lodi ruler who expanded the kingdom, established strict administrative control, and founded the city of Agra in 1504 CE to keep a watch on rebellious chieftains.
• **Economic Measures of Sikandar**: He abolished the octroi duty on grain, promoted trade, and introduced the *Gazz-i-Sikandari* (Sikandar’s yard) for accurate land measurement.
• **Ibrahim Lodi (1517–1526 CE)**: The last ruler of the Lodi dynasty and the Delhi Sultanate, whose abrasive, arrogant demeanor alienated the proud Afghan nobility.
• **Alienation of Nobles**: Ibrahim Lodi's insistence on absolute royal subordination angered powerful nobles, leading several disgruntled chieftains to invite Babur, the ruler of Kabul, to invade India.
• **First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE)**: Fought between Ibrahim Lodi and Babur, this decisive battle marked the end of the Delhi Sultanate and the birth of the Mughal Empire.
• **Military Turning Point**: Babur’s victory was secured through the effective tactical use of artillery (*Tulughma* and matchlocks), which infantry-heavy Sultanate armies could not counter.
• **Historical Significance**: The fall of the Delhi Sultanate in 1526 CE closed an important chapter of medieval Indian political evolution and opened the doors for a centralized imperial regime.
• **Political Fragmentation**: Throughout its existence, the Sultanate witnessed a constant struggle for supremacy between the centralizing ambitions of the Sultans and the centrifugal tendencies of regional governors.
• **Legacy of the Dynastic Cycle**: The rapid rise and fall of five distinct dynasties highlights the fragile nature of medieval monarchies dependent on military might and personal leadership.

• **Central Administration**: The Delhi Sultanate was a centralized military state headed by the Sultan, whose word was law in political, military, and judicial matters.
• **The Sultan's Powers**: The Sultan acted as the chief executive, supreme commander-in-chief of the armed forces, and chief dispenser of justice across the empire.
• **Role of the Wazir**: The *Wazir* was the prime minister who headed the finance department (*Diwan-i-Wizarat*) and oversaw revenue collection, taxation, and state expenditure.
• **Diwan-i-Ariz**: The military department, headed by the *Ariz-i-Mumalik*, responsible for the recruitment, payment, and maintenance of the royal army.
• **Diwan-i-Insha**: The department of royal correspondence responsible for drafting royal decrees, farmans, and maintaining communication with provincial governors.
• **Diwan-i-Rasalat**: The department of religious affairs, appeals, and foreign correspondence, which handled diplomatic relations and grants to scholars and holy men.
• **Judicial System**: The Sultan was the highest court of appeal, assisted by the chief qazi (*Qazi-ul-Quzat*), who administered Islamic law (*Sharia*) alongside civil and customary laws.
• **Local Administration**: For administrative convenience, the empire was divided into provinces called *Iqtas*, which were further subdivided into *Shiqs* (districts) and *Parganas* (sub-districts).
• **Role of the Muqti (Iqtadar)**: Governors of *Iqtas* were known as *Muqtis* or *Iqtadars*, responsible for maintaining law and order and collecting land revenue in their assigned territories.
• **The Iqta System**: A unique administrative and financial mechanism where revenue assignments (*Iqtas*) were granted to nobles and military commanders instead of cash salaries.
• **Accountability of Muqtis**: *Muqtis* collected revenues, deducted their personal expenses and military upkeep costs, and were required to remit the surplus balance (*Fawaz*) to the central treasury.
• **Transferability of Muqtis**: To prevent regional governors from becoming too powerful or establishing hereditary independent principalities, early Sultans frequently transferred *Muqtis* from one region to another.
• **Village Administration**: The village remained the basic unit of administration, managed by traditional hereditary officials such as the *Khukuts* (village headmen), *Patwaris* (accountants), and *Chowkidars*.
• **Role of Local Chieftains**: Local land-owning chiefs known as *Rai*, *Rana*, or *Rawat* often acted as intermediaries between the central government and the peasantry for revenue collection.
• **Checks and Balances**: The central government maintained a network of spies (*Munhiyan*) and news-writers (*Barids*) across provinces to report directly to the Sultan on the conduct of *Muqtis*.

• **Agrarian Economy**: Agriculture formed the economic bedrock of the Delhi Sultanate, supporting urban centers, trade networks, and the luxurious lifestyles of the ruling elite.
• **Peasantry and Production**: The primary producers were peasants who cultivated various crops, including wheat, rice, pulses, oilseeds, sugarcane, and cotton, utilizing traditional tools and animal power.
• **Land Revenue Demand**: Land revenue was the principal source of state income, generally ranging from one-third to one-half of the gross agricultural produce, collected in cash or kind.
• **Impact of Famines**: Erratic monsoons frequently triggered droughts and famines, placing immense financial stress on farming communities who bore the brunt of heavy taxation.
• **Irrigation Infrastructure**: Rulers like Ghiyas-ud-din and Firoz Shah Tughlaq invested heavily in digging canals, wells, and reservoirs to boost agricultural output and protect crops from drought.
• **Trade and Commerce**: The establishment of the Sultanate stimulated internal and external trade, leading to the rapid growth of urban market centers (*Kasbas* and *Shahars*).
• **Urbanization**: Cities like Delhi, Agra, Lahore, Multan, and Daulatabad transformed into thriving hubs of craft production, banking, and mercantile activity.
• **Guilds and Craftsperson**: Artisans and craftspeople organized themselves into guilds (*Shrenis*), producing fine textiles, metalwork, stone carvings, and luxury goods for the nobility.
• **Internal Trade Routes**: A well-maintained network of roads connected various parts of the empire, facilitated by royal rest-houses (*Sarais*), milestones, and security patrols against highway robbers.
• **External Maritime and Overland Trade**: India maintained robust trade relations with West Asia, Central Asia, Southeast Asia, and China through ports on the western coast and overland mountain passes.
• **Imports and Exports**: India exported textiles, spices, precious stones, and metal crafts, while importing horses from Arabia and Central Asia, silk from China, and glass and glassware from the Mediterranean.
• **Monetary Stability**: The circulation of standardized silver *Tankas* and copper *Jitals* simplified commercial transactions, eliminating the reliance on barter systems in urban markets.
• **Role of Bankers and Merchants**: Specialized mercantile communities, such as the *Multanis* and *Jains*, played a crucial role in providing credit facilities, financing long-distance trade, and money-lending.
• **State Monopoly and Karkhanas**: The royal household maintained manufacturing units called *Karkhanas*, where skilled artisans worked under state supervision to produce luxury items, robes of honor, and weapons.
• **Economic Stratification**: While the ruling elite, nobles, and wealthy merchants enjoyed immense prosperity, the vast majority of peasants and urban laborers lived close to subsistence levels.

• **Social Structure**: Society during the Delhi Sultanate period was deeply hierarchical, stratified along lines of religion, race, status, wealth, and occupation.
• **The Ruling Elite**: At the apex of society stood the Sultan, the royal family, high-ranking military commanders, and Turkish, Afghan, and Persian nobles who held elite status.
• **Racial and Ethnic Divisions**: Early in the Sultanate, Turkish nobles held a monopoly over high administrative and military positions, often looking down upon Indian converts and other ethnic groups.
• **The Ulema**: A powerful class of Islamic scholars and theologians (*Ulema*) wielded immense influence over the Sultans, advising them on religious matters and administration of justice.
• **Status of Women**: Women faced significant patriarchal restrictions, with practices like the *Purdah* system (seclusion) and early marriages becoming widespread among upper-class Hindu and Muslim households.
• **Education for Women**: While formal public education was largely restricted, royal and aristocratic women often received private tutoring in theology, literature, music, and administration.
• **Condition of the Masses**: The common people, comprising peasants, artisans, weavers, and manual laborers, led simple, hard-working lives burdened by taxes and feudal obligations.
• **Slavery in Society**: Slavery was an accepted institutional practice; slaves (*Bandagans*) were owned by nobles and the royal household for domestic chores, military service, and administrative duties.
• **Treatment of Slaves**: While many slaves were treated well and could rise to high ranks (as seen in the Slave Dynasty), others faced harsh exploitation depending on their masters.
• **Cultural Synthesis**: The coming together of Turkish, Persian, Afghan rulers and indigenous Indian populations sparked a vibrant process of cultural exchange and synthesis.
• **Evolution of Urdu (Zeb-o-Zuban)**: Daily interaction between Persian/Turkic-speaking soldiers and local Prakrit/Hindi speakers gave birth to a new composite dialect known as Hindavi or Urdu.
• **Architectural Fusion**: Sultanate architecture combined Islamic features (domes, arches, minarets, geometric patterns) with Indian elements (lintels, brackets, lotus motifs, Hindu stone-carving traditions).
• **Indo-Islamic Architecture**: The *Indo-Islamic* style represented a harmonious architectural blend, visible in monuments like the Qutb Minar, Alai Darwaza, and Lodi tombs.
• **Music and Performing Arts**: Sultans like Ala-ud-din Khilji and Firoz Shah Tughlaq patronized musicians, leading to the blending of Persian musical modes with classical Indian music traditions.
• **Sufi and Bhakti Influence**: Socio-religious movements such as Sufism and the Bhakti movement promoted spiritual equality, humanism, and communal harmony, softening rigid social divisions.

• **Sufism and the Bhakti Movement**: This era witnessed profound religious transformations as popular mystic movements challenged rigid rituals and orthodox hierarchies.
• **Sufi Orders (Silsilas)**: Sufism emphasized direct personal experience of God through love, devotion, and service to humanity, organizing themselves into various orders like Chishti and Suhrawardi.
• **Chishti Silsila**: Founded in India by Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti in Ajmer, the Chishti order gained immense popularity for its emphasis on poverty, pacifism, and openness to all social classes.
• **Sufi Khanqahs**: Sufi hospices (*Khanqahs*) served as vibrant centers of charity, communal dining, spiritual instruction, and shelter for travelers, transcending caste and religious barriers.
• **Sufi Music (Qawwali)**: Devotional music sessions (*Sama*) organized at Sufi shrines used mystical poetry to induce spiritual ecstasy, bridging cultural divides through sound and devotion.
• **The Bhakti Movement**: Parallel to Sufism, the Bhakti saints preached intense personal devotion to a supreme God, rejecting the supremacy of priestly intermediaries and ritualistic caste barriers.
• **Key Bhakti Saints**: Prominent saints of this medieval phase included Ramananda, Kabir, Namdev, Mirabai, and Guru Nanak, who preached in regional vernacular languages.
• **Use of Regional Languages**: By preaching in common spoken languages like Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali, and Marathi, Bhakti and Sufi saints made spiritual knowledge accessible to the ordinary masses.
• **Kabir's Teachings**: Kabir vehemently criticized religious hypocrisy, caste discrimination, ritualism, and orthodox dogmas, emphasizing that true devotion lies in moral integrity and love for humanity.
• **Guru Nanak's Message**: Guru Nanak laid the foundations of Sikhism during this period, preaching the oneness of God (*Ek Onkar*), equality of all human beings, honesty, and charity.
• **Literature and Historiography**: The Sultanate period witnessed a massive output of literary works in Persian, Sanskrit, and regional languages.
• **Amir Khusro (1253–1325 CE)**: Celebrated as the "Parrot of India" (*Tuti-e-Hind*), Amir Khusro was a prolific poet, musician, and historian who served under seven Delhi Sultans.
• **Contributions of Amir Khusro**: He experimented with Persian and Hindavi poetry, invented the musical instrument *Sitar*, and pioneered the *Khayal* style of classical Hindustani music.
• **Sanskrit Literature**: Despite Persian being the court language, Sanskrit works on philosophy, medicine, astronomy, and mathematics continued to be composed and translated.
• **Translation Bureaus**: Several Arabic and Persian scientific works were translated into Sanskrit, and conversely, classic Indian epics and philosophical texts were translated into Persian.

• **Assessment of the Delhi Sultanate**: The three-century rule of the Delhi Sultanate left an indelible imprint on the political, economic, social, and cultural landscape of the Indian subcontinent.
• **Political Unification**: For the first time since the Gupta Empire, large portions of northern and southern India were brought under a centralized administrative network based in Delhi.
• **Administrative Legacy**: The institutional structures established by the Sultans—such as the *Iqta* system, revenue collection methods, and departmental bureaucracies—laid the groundwork for later Mughal administration.
• **Economic Integration**: Standardized currency (*Tanka* and *Jital*), improved roads, safe trade routes, and urbanization stimulated long-term commercial growth and market integration across regions.
• **Technological Innovations**: The Sultanate period introduced several technological advancements to India, including the Persian wheel (*Araghatta*) for irrigation, paper-making techniques, and superior vault-and-dome construction.
