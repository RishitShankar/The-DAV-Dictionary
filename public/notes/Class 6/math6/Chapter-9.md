• **Chapter Introduction:** Welcome to DAV Academic Notes for Class 6 Secondary Mathematics, Chapter 9, dedicated to the study of **Line Segments** and foundational geometry.
• **Core Subject Focus:** This chapter introduces students to the basic building blocks of geometry, moving from abstract spatial intuition to precise geometric definitions.
• **Historical and Pedagogical Context:** Following the DAV curriculum, this chapter bridges the gap between the concept of a point and complex polygons by focusing on bounded linear paths.
• **Fundamental Concept of a Point:** A **point** is an exact location in space, having no length, breadth, or thickness, typically represented by a fine dot made with a sharp pencil.
• **Notation of Points:** In geometry, points are always denoted by capital letters of the English alphabet, such as Point $A$, Point $B$, or Point $P$.
• **Concept of a Line:** A **line** is a straight path that extends infinitely in both directions without any endpoints, having length but no width or thickness.
• **Line Representation:** A line is denoted symbolically by writing any two points on it with a double-headed arrow above them, such as $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$.
• **Concept of a Ray:** A **ray** is a portion of a line that starts at a fixed starting point (origin) and extends infinitely in one direction only.
• **Ray Representation:** A ray with initial point $A$ passing through point $B$ is denoted symbolically as $\overrightarrow{AB}$, where the order of letters matters significantly.
• **Transition to Line Segments:** While lines and rays are infinite, physical and geometric measurements require bounded portions of lines, leading directly to the concept of the **line segment**.
• **Definition of a Line Segment:** A **line segment** is a bounded part of a line consisting of two distinct endpoints and all the points of the line that lie between them.
• **Distinct Endpoints:** Unlike a line, a line segment has a fixed, measurable length because it is strictly bounded by its two distinct endpoints.
• **Notation of a Line Segment:** A line segment with endpoints $A$ and $B$ is denoted symbolically as $\overline{AB}$ or simply as segment $AB$.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Symmetry in Notation:** The order of letters in a line segment is interchangeable; therefore, $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{BA}$ represent the exact same geometric line segment.
• **Key Difference (Line vs. Segment):** A line ($\overleftrightarrow{AB}$) has infinite length and no endpoints, whereas a line segment ($\overline{AB}$) has a finite, definite length and two definite endpoints.
• **Key Difference (Ray vs. Segment):** A ray ($\overrightarrow{AB}$) has one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction, whereas a line segment ($\overline{AB}$) has two distinct endpoints and is finite.
• **Physical Real-World Models:** Common real-world examples of line segments include the edge of a textbook, a stretched string held tightly at both ends, and the edge of a ruler.
• **Drawing Line Segments:** Accurate geometric drawing requires a sharp pencil and a standard ruler marked in centimeters and millimeters for precision.
• **Measuring Line Segments:** To measure a line segment $\overline{AB}$, the zero mark of the ruler is aligned precisely with endpoint $A$, and the reading on the ruler corresponding to endpoint $B$ gives the length.
• **Importance of Eye Position:** Parallax error must be avoided during measurement by keeping the eye vertically directly above the mark being read on the scale.
• **Length as Distance:** The length of a line segment is the shortest distance between its two endpoints, measured in standard units like centimeters ($\text{cm}$) or millimeters ($\text{mm}$).
• **Equality of Line Segments:** Two line segments are said to be **equal or congruent** if and only if they have exactly the same length.
• **Symbol for Congruence:** If segment $AB$ and segment $CD$ are equal in length, it is written symbolically as $\overline{AB} = \overline{CD}$ or $\text{Length of } \overline{AB} = \text{Length of } \overline{CD}$.
• **Comparison by Inspection:** The simplest, though least accurate, method to compare two line segments is visual inspection, which works only if the difference in lengths is very large.
• **Comparison by Tracing:** A more reliable elementary method involves tracing one line segment onto tracing paper and placing it over the second segment to check for exact overlap.
• **Comparison Using Dividers:** The most accurate instrument-based method for comparing line segments involves using a pair of **dividers** to lift the exact length of one segment and test it against another.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Introduction to the Divider:** A divider is a two-legged geometric instrument with both legs ending in sharp steel points, specifically designed for transferring and comparing lengths.
• **Step 1 in Using Dividers:** Place the sharp point of one leg of the divider on endpoint $A$ of the line segment $\overline{AB}$.
• **Step 2 in Using Dividers:** Adjust the other leg of the divider so that its sharp point rests precisely on the second endpoint $B$ of the segment.
• **Step 3 in Using Dividers:** Without disturbing the hinge setting of the divider, lift it carefully and place one leg on the starting point of the second line segment to be compared.
• **Step 4 in Using Dividers:** Observe where the second leg of the divider falls relative to the endpoint of the second segment to determine which segment is longer, shorter, or equal.
• **Construction of a Line Segment:** Constructing a line segment means drawing a segment whose length is exactly equal to a given line segment using geometric tools.
• **Construction Using Ruler Only:** To draw a segment of a given length, say $5\text{ cm}$, align the zero of the ruler with the starting point and make a pencil dot at the $5\text{ cm}$ mark, then join them.
• **Construction Using Ruler and Divider:** To copy a given line segment $\overline{XY}$, open the divider so its tips coincide with $X$ and $Y$, then transfer this spread to a straight line drawn on paper.
• **Additive Property of Line Segments:** If a line segment $\overline{AC}$ contains a point $B$ lying strictly between $A$ and $C$, then the total length equals the sum of its parts: $\text{Length}(\overline{AC}) = \text{Length}(\overline{AB}) + \text{Length}(\overline{BC})$.
• **Collinear Points:** Points that lie on the same single straight line are called **collinear points**; if they do not lie on the same line, they are non-collinear.
• **Betweenness Test:** Point $B$ is said to lie *between* $A$ and $C$ if and only if $A, B,$ and $C$ are collinear and $\text{Length}(\overline{AB}) + \text{Length}(\overline{BC}) = \text{Length}(\overline{AC})$.
• **Subtractive Property:** If $\overline{AB}$ is a part of $\overline{AC}$ such that $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, then the length of the remaining segment is $\text{Length}(\overline{BC}) = \text{Length}(\overline{AC}) - \text{Length}(\overline{AB})$.
• **Intersecting Lines:** When two lines cross each other at a single common point, they are called **intersecting lines**, and that common point is known as the **point of intersection**.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Intersection of Line Segments:** Two line segments can intersect each other if their extensions meet, or if they touch or cross each other within their boundaries.
• **Parallel Lines:** Lines in a plane that never meet or intersect, no matter how far they are extended in either direction, are called **parallel lines**.
• **Perpendicular Lines:** When two intersecting lines meet at a right angle ($90^\circ$), they are referred to as **perpendicular lines**.
• **Perpendicular Segments:** Two line segments are perpendicular if the lines containing them form a right angle at their point of intersection.
• **Perpendicular Bisector Concept:** A **perpendicular bisector** of a line segment is a line, ray, or segment that is perpendicular to the segment and passes through its exact midpoint.
• **Definition of Midpoint:** The **midpoint** of a line segment is the unique point that divides the line segment into two line segments of equal length.
• **Algebraic Representation of Midpoint:** If $M$ is the midpoint of line segment $\overline{AB}$, then $\text{Length}(\overline{AM}) = \text{Length}(\overline{MB}) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Length}(\overline{AB})$.
• **Verifying Midpoints:** A midpoint can be verified by folding a paper strip containing the segment so that the two endpoints coincide; the crease formed passes through the midpoint.
• **Geometric Construction Tools:** Standard geometric constructions of line segments and their parts require exclusively a graduated ruler and a pair of compasses (or dividers).
• **Role of the Compass:** A compass is an instrument with one sharp metal point and one pencil-holding leg, used primarily for drawing arcs and circles and transferring equal lengths.
• **Constructing a Copy of a Segment:** To construct a line segment equal to $\overline{AB}$ using a ruler and compass: draw a long reference line, place compass tips at $A$ and $B$, and swing an arc on the line.
• **Bisection Construction:** Constructing the perpendicular bisector of a given line segment $\overline{AB}$ using a compass involves opening the compass to a radius greater than half the length of $\overline{AB}$.
• **Step-by-Step Bisector Construction (1):** With center $A$ and radius greater than half of $AB$, draw two arcs—one above and one below the line segment $\overline{AB}$.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Step-by-Step Bisector Construction (2):** With center $B$ and the exact same compass radius, draw two intersecting arcs—one above and one below the line segment $\overline{AB}$ to cut the previous arcs.
• **Step-by-Step Bisector Construction (3):** Label the points of intersection of the arcs as $P$ and $Q$, and draw the straight line $\overleftrightarrow{PQ}$ connecting these two intersection points.
• **Validation of Bisector:** The line $\overleftrightarrow{PQ}$ constructed intersects $\overline{AB}$ at its exact midpoint $M$ and forms a $90^\circ$ angle, confirming it is the perpendicular bisector.
• **Axis of Symmetry:** Every line segment possesses a line of symmetry, which is its perpendicular bisector, dividing the segment into two mirror-image halves.
• **Properties of Line Segments:** Line segments are rigid, one-dimensional figures possessing definite length, exact position, and two identifiable boundary points.
• **Naming Conventions:** A line segment must always be named using its two endpoints, such as $\overline{PQ}$ or $\overline{XY}$, never by using arbitrary interior points unless specifically referencing sub-segments.
• **Count of Segments in a Line:** A single straight line contains an infinite number of overlapping line segments of various lengths.
• **Counting Segments on a Ray:** A ray contains an infinite number of line segments that share the fixed origin point as one of their endpoints.
• **Counting Segments on Collinear Points:** If $n$ distinct points lie on a straight line, the total number of line segments formed by these points can be calculated systematically.
• **Formula for Segment Count:** For $n$ collinear points, the total number of line segments is given by the algebraic formula $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
• **Application of Segment Count Formula:** For example, if $4$ collinear points $A, B, C, D$ lie on a line, the number of line segments is $\frac{4(4-1)}{2} = \frac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6$ segments.
• **Listing the Segments:** For the $4$ points $A, B, C, D$, the $6$ segments are $\overline{AB}, \overline{AC}, \overline{AD}, \overline{BC}, \overline{BD},$ and $\overline{CD}$.
• **Understanding Open vs. Closed Paths:** A line segment is a closed geometric path because it includes its endpoints, whereas a line is an open path with no endpoints.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Topological Definition:** In topology and geometry, a line segment is a homeomorphic image of a closed unit interval $[0, 1]$ on the real number line.
• **Distance Formula Connection:** In coordinate geometry, the length of a line segment connecting two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is given by the distance formula $\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$.
• **Midpoint Formula Connection:** In coordinate geometry, the coordinates of the midpoint of a line segment with endpoints $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ are $\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$.
• **Triangular Inequality Principle:** For any three non-collinear points $A, B,$ and $C$, the sum of the lengths of any two line segments must be strictly greater than the length of the third segment: $AB + BC > AC$.
• **Degenerate Triangle Case:** If three points $A, B,$ and $C$ are collinear such that $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, then $AB + BC = AC$, forming a degenerate triangle.
• **Polygon Formation:** Line segments serve as the foundational sides of all rectilinear geometric figures, including triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, and general polygons.
• **Definition of a Polygon:** A polygon is a closed two-dimensional geometric figure bounded entirely by three or more straight line segments joined end-to-end.
• **Sides of a Polygon:** Each individual line segment that makes up the boundary of a polygon is referred to as a **side** of that polygon.
• **Vertices of a Polygon:** The common endpoint where two adjacent line segments of a polygon meet is called a **vertex** (plural: vertices).
• **Diagonal of a Polygon:** A **diagonal** of a polygon is a line segment connecting any two non-consecutive (non-adjacent) vertices of the polygon.
• **Number of Diagonals Formula:** For a polygon with $n$ sides, the total number of diagonals is given by the standard formula $\frac{n(n-3)}{2}$.
• **Application to Quadrilateral:** A quadrilateral ($n=4$) has $\frac{4(4-3)}{2} = 2$ diagonals connecting its opposite non-adjacent vertices.
• **Application to Triangle:** A triangle ($n=3$) has $\frac{3(3-3)}{2} = 0$ diagonals because all its vertices are adjacent to one another.

• **Common Student Error 1:** Confusing the notation $\overline{AB}$ (line segment) with $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ (line) or $\overrightarrow{AB}$ (ray); precision in symbolic notation is strictly evaluated.
• **Common Student Error 2:** Measuring from the edge of the plastic ruler instead of the exact zero mark when measuring the length of a physical line segment.
• **Common Student Error 3:** Forgetting that the order of letters does not matter for line segments ($\overline{AB} = \overline{BA}$), but matters critically for rays ($\overrightarrow{AB} \neq \overrightarrow{BA}$).
• **DAV Examination Tip 1:** Always use a sharp HB pencil for drawing geometric diagrams, and label all endpoints and points of intersection clearly with capital letters.
• **DAV Examination Tip 2:** When asked to construct a line segment of a given length, always write the final measured or constructed length clearly beside the diagram (e.g., $\text{Length} = 6.5\text{ cm}$).
• **DAV Examination Tip 3:** Show all construction arcs clearly when asked to use a compass and ruler; never erase construction arcs as they prove the geometric method used.
• **Real-World Engineering Application:** Civil engineers and architects use line segments and their precise measurements to draft blueprints for buildings, bridges, and roads.
• **Real-World Carpentry Application:** Carpenters use rulers, dividers, and measuring tapes to mark exact line segments on timber before cutting pieces for furniture assembly.
• **Computer Graphics Application:** In digital computer graphics, curves and complex shapes are rendered by connecting thousands of tiny, straight line segments called vectors.
• **Cartography Application:** Map makers use line segments on coordinate grids to measure straight-line (as-the-crow-flies) distances between cities and geographical landmarks.
• **Navigation Application:** Marine and aviation navigation rely on plotting courses as series of connected line segments on nautical charts and flight plans.

• **Advanced Concept - Concurrent Segments:** Line segments or lines that pass through the exact same single common point are known as **concurrent** line segments or lines.
• **Advanced Concept - Congruent Segments Properties:** Congruency of line segments is an equivalence relation because it satisfies the reflexive, symmetric, and transitive properties.
• **Reflexive Property of Segments:** Every line segment is congruent to itself; for any segment $\overline{AB}$, $\overline{AB} \cong \overline{AB}$.
• **Symmetric Property of Segments:** If segment $\overline{AB}$ is congruent to segment $\overline{CD}$, then segment $\overline{CD}$ is congruent to segment $\overline{AB}$ ($\overline{AB} \cong \overline{CD} \implies \overline{CD} \cong \overline{AB}$).
• **Transitive Property of Segments:** If segment $\overline{AB}$ is congruent to segment $\overline{CD}$, and $\overline{CD}$ is congruent to segment $\overline{EF}$, then $\overline{AB}$ is congruent to segment $\overline{EF}$.
• **Segment Addition Postulate Summary:** If $B$ is between $A$ and $C$, then $AB + BC = AC$; conversely, if $AB + BC = AC$, then $B$ is guaranteed to lie on the line segment $\overline{AC}$.
• **Geometric Proofs Introduction:** Simple geometric reasoning in Class 6 relies on the segment addition postulate to prove that composite lengths equal the sum of their individual parts.
• **Example Proof Scenario:** If $\overline{AB} = 3\text{ cm}$ and $\overline{BC} = 4\text{ cm}$ and $B$ is between $A$ and $C$, then $\overline{AC} = 3 + 4 = 7\text{ cm}$ by direct application of the postulate.
• **Bisector Division Principle:** If a line segment is divided into two equal parts by its midpoint, each part is exactly half the total length of the original segment.
• **Trisection of a Line Segment:** Trisection involves dividing a given line segment into three equal parts using geometric constructions or calculated measurements.
• **Calculation in Trisection:** If line segment $\overline{AB}$ is $9\text{ cm}$ long and is trisected by points $P$ and $Q$, then $\text{Length}(\overline{AP}) = \text{Length}(\overline{PQ}) = \text{Length}(\overline{QB}) = 3\text{ cm}$.
• **Graphical Representation of Trisection:** Points $P$ and $Q$ divide $\overline{AB}$ such that $\overline{AP} = \overline{PQ} = \overline{QB}$, with $P$ and $Q$ both lying between $A$ and $B$.
• **Symmetry in Geometric Figures:** Line segments often form the axes of symmetry for regular polygons and symmetrical two-dimensional geometric figures.
