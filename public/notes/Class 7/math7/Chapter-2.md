• **Introduction to Rational Numbers**: A rational number is defined as a number that can be expressed in the form $p/q$, where $p$ and $q$ are both integers, and the denominator $q$ is strictly not equal to zero ($q \neq 0$).
• **Etymology and Origin**: The term 'rational' originates from the word 'ratio', indicating that a rational number represents a ratio of two integers.
• **Components of a Rational Number**: In the rational number $p/q$, the top integer $p$ is called the **numerator**, and the bottom integer $q$ is called the **denominator**.
• **Inclusion of Integers**: Every integer is a rational number because any integer $a$ can be written as $a/1$, maintaining the required fractional form with a non-zero denominator.
• **Inclusion of Fractions**: All standard fractions (proper and improper) are rational numbers, as their numerators and denominators are whole numbers and integers, respectively.
• **Inclusion of Zero**: The number zero ($0$) is a rational number because it can be expressed as $0/1$, $0/2$, or $0/-5$, satisfying the definition $p/q$ where $q \neq 0$.
• **The Non-Zero Denominator Rule**: The denominator of a rational number can never be zero because division by zero is undefined in mathematics, making expressions like $5/0$ meaningless.
• **Positive Rational Numbers**: A rational number is said to be positive if both its numerator and denominator have the same sign (both are positive or both are negative).
• **Examples of Positive Rational Numbers**: Numbers such as $3/7$ and $-5/-9$ are positive rational numbers because their signs cancel out or are identical.
• **Negative Rational Numbers**: A rational number is negative if its numerator and denominator have opposite signs (one is positive and the other is negative).
• **Examples of Negative Rational Numbers**: Numbers such as $-4/9$ and $7/-11$ are negative rational numbers since one component is positive and the other is negative.
• **Standard Form of a Rational Number**: A rational number $p/q$ is said to be in its standard form (or simplest form) if $q$ is a positive integer and $p$ and $q$ have no common factor other than $1$ (i.e., $\text{HCF}(|p|, q) = 1$).
• **Reduction to Lowest Terms**: To reduce a rational number to its standard form, both the numerator and the denominator are divided by their highest common factor (HCF).
• **Equivalence of Rational Numbers**: Two rational numbers are equivalent if they represent the same value, obtained by multiplying or dividing both the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero integer.
• **DAV Curriculum Focus**: The DAV Class 7 Mathematics curriculum emphasizes building a strong foundation in handling signed numbers through systematic operational rules for rational numbers.

• **Comparison of Rational Numbers**: Comparing two rational numbers involves determining whether one is greater than, less than, or equal to the other.
• **Comparison on a Number Line**: On the standard horizontal number line, numbers to the right are always greater than numbers to the left, exactly as observed with integers and fractions.
• **Role of Positive and Negative Numbers**: Every positive rational number is strictly greater than zero, and every negative rational number is strictly less than zero.
• **Comparison Rule for Positive Numbers**: Between two positive rational numbers, the one with the larger value without signs behaves according to standard fraction rules.
• **Comparison Rule Involving Negative Numbers**: Every positive rational number is always greater than any negative rational number.
• **Comparison of Negative Rational Numbers**: Between two negative rational numbers, the number with the smaller absolute value is greater (e.g., $-1/3 > -1/2$).
• **General Comparison via Common Denominators**: To compare any two rational numbers, first ensure their denominators are positive, then convert them to equivalent rational numbers with a common denominator.
• **Least Common Multiple (LCM) Method**: The common denominator used for comparison is typically the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of the denominators of the given rational numbers.
• **Numerator Analysis After LCM**: Once denominators are equalized, the rational number with the larger numerator is the greater rational number.
• **Cross-Multiplication Method for Comparison**: For two rational numbers $a/b$ and $c/d$ with positive denominators $b$ and $d$, compare $ad$ and $bc$; if $ad > bc$, then $a/b > c/d$.
• **Representation on Number Line**: Rational numbers can be systematically represented on a number line by dividing the segments between integers into equal parts corresponding to the denominator.
• **Density Property**: Between any two distinct rational numbers, there exist infinitely many other rational numbers, a property known as the density of rational numbers.
• **Finding Rational Numbers Between Two Numbers**: To find a rational number between two given rational numbers $a$ and $b$, one can compute their mean: $\frac{a + b}{2}$.
• **Alternative Method for Intermediates**: Converting rational numbers to equivalent forms with larger denominators makes it easy to list multiple rational numbers between them.
• **Sign Convention Maintenance**: Throughout all comparison and representation steps, negative signs in the denominator must be shifted to the numerator before processing.

• **Addition of Rational Numbers with Like Denominators**: When adding two or more rational numbers that have the same denominator, keep the common denominator and simply add their numerators together.
• **Formula for Like Denominators**: For rational numbers $a/c$ and $b/c$, the sum is expressed as $\frac{a + b}{c}$.
• **Addition of Rational Numbers with Unlike Denominators**: When denominators are different, first find the LCM of the denominators to convert the numbers into equivalent rational numbers with like denominators.
• **Step-by-Step Unlike Addition**: Convert each rational number to an equivalent form with the LCM as the common denominator, and then add the resulting numerators.
• **Closure Property of Addition**: The sum of any two rational numbers is always a rational number, demonstrating that the set of rational numbers is closed under addition.
• **Commutative Property of Addition**: For any two rational numbers $a/b$ and $c/d$, the order of addition does not affect the sum: $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c}{d} + \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Associative Property of Addition**: When adding three rational numbers, grouping them in any combination yields the same result: $(\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d}) + \frac{e}{f} = \frac{a}{b} + (\frac{c}{d} + \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Additive Identity (Zero)**: The rational number $0$ (or $0/1$) is the additive identity, because adding $0$ to any rational number leaves the number unchanged: $\frac{a}{b} + 0 = \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Additive Inverse (Negative)**: For any rational number $a/b$, its additive inverse is $-a/b$, such that their sum is zero: $\frac{a}{b} + (-\frac{a}{b}) = 0$.
• **Finding the Additive Inverse**: To find the additive inverse of a rational number, simply change its sign (make positive numbers negative and negative numbers positive).
• **Simplification of Sums**: Always reduce the final calculated sum to its standard or simplest form by dividing the numerator and denominator by their HCF.
• **Dealing with Negative Signs in Addition**: If a negative sign appears in the denominator during addition, immediately rewrite the number with the negative sign in the numerator.
• **Additive Property of Zero Application**: Zero serves as a neutral element in rational number additions, mirroring its role in integers and whole numbers.
• **Multiple Rational Number Addition**: The associative and commutative properties allow multiple rational numbers to be added in any order or grouping to simplify calculations.
• **Real-World Contexts**: Addition of rational numbers is applied when combining quantities, measuring distances, or calculating financial balances involving fractional values.

• **Subtraction Concept**: Subtraction of rational numbers is defined as the addition of the additive inverse of the second rational number to the first rational number.
• **Subtraction Rule Formula**: For any two rational numbers $a/b$ and $c/d$, subtracting $c/d$ from $a/b$ is expressed as $\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} + (-\frac{c}{d})$.
• **Like Denominators in Subtraction**: When subtracting rational numbers with like denominators, keep the denominator unchanged and subtract the second numerator from the first: $\frac{a}{c} - \frac{b}{c} = \frac{a - b}{c}$.
• **Unlike Denominators in Subtraction**: For rational numbers with different denominators, first find the LCM of the denominators and convert them into equivalent rational numbers with a common denominator.
• **Closure Property of Subtraction**: The difference between any two rational numbers is always a rational number, proving closure under subtraction.
• **Non-Commutative Nature of Subtraction**: Subtraction of rational numbers is not commutative; changing the order alters the result: $\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d} \neq \frac{c}{d} - \frac{a}{b}$ (except when the numbers are equal).
• **Non-Associative Nature of Subtraction**: Subtraction is not associative for rational numbers, meaning grouping changes the final outcome: $(\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d}) - \frac{e}{f} \neq \frac{a}{b} - (\frac{c}{d} - \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Role of Zero in Subtraction**: Subtracting zero from any rational number leaves it unchanged ($\frac{a}{b} - 0 = \frac{a}{b}$), but subtracting a rational number from zero yields its additive inverse ($0 - \frac{a}{b} = -\frac{a}{b}$).
• **Sign Management in Subtraction**: Special care must be taken with double negatives; subtracting a negative rational number is equivalent to adding its positive counterpart ($\frac{a}{b} - (-\frac{c}{d}) = \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d}$).
• **Simplification Post-Subtraction**: The resulting difference must always be simplified to its lowest terms by canceling common factors between the numerator and denominator.
• **LCM Calculation Reminder**: Accurate subtraction heavily depends on correctly finding the Least Common Multiple for unlike denominators.
• **Consistency with Fractions**: The subtraction rules for rational numbers extend the fraction subtraction rules learned in earlier classes to include negative integers.
• **Verification via Addition**: Subtraction results can be verified by adding the difference to the subtrahend to check if it equals the minuend.
• **Word Problems Application**: Subtraction of rational numbers is used to find differences in temperature, altitude changes, and remaining quantities in various applied contexts.
• **DAV Problem-Solving Strategy**: DAV textbooks emphasize writing intermediate conversion steps clearly to avoid sign errors during subtraction operations.

• **Multiplication of Rational Numbers**: To multiply two rational numbers, multiply their numerators together to form the new numerator, and multiply their denominators together to form the new denominator.
• **Multiplication Formula**: For any two rational numbers $a/b$ and $c/d$, the product is given by $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \times c}{b \times d}$.
• **Handling Signs in Multiplication**: The sign of the product follows standard integer multiplication rules: positive times positive is positive, negative times negative is positive, and positive times negative is negative.
• **Closure Property of Multiplication**: The product of any two rational numbers is always a rational number, showing closure under multiplication.
• **Commutative Property of Multiplication**: The order of multiplying two rational numbers does not change the product: $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{c}{d} \times \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Associative Property of Multiplication**: When multiplying three rational numbers, the grouping does not affect the result: $(\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}) \times \frac{e}{f} = \frac{a}{b} \times (\frac{c}{d} \times \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Multiplicative Identity (One)**: The rational number $1$ (or $1/1$) is the multiplicative identity, because multiplying any rational number by $1$ leaves it unchanged: $\frac{a}{b} \times 1 = \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Multiplication by Zero**: Multiplying any rational number by zero results in zero: $\frac{a}{b} \times 0 = 0$.
• **Multiplicative Inverse (Reciprocal)**: For a non-zero rational number $a/b$, its multiplicative inverse (or reciprocal) is $b/a$, such that their product is $1$: $\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{a} = 1$.
• **Condition for Reciprocal**: Zero does not have a multiplicative inverse because division by zero is undefined ($1/0$ is impossible).
• **Cross-Cancellation Shortcut**: Before multiplying, cross-cancel any common factors between any numerator and any denominator to simplify the calculation early.
• **Standard Form Output**: Always ensure the final product is expressed in its standard form with a positive denominator and reduced numerators and denominators.
• **Distributive Property of Multiplication over Addition**: Multiplication distributes over addition for rational numbers: $\frac{a}{b} \times (\frac{c}{d} + \frac{e}{f}) = (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}) + (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Distributive Property over Subtraction**: Multiplication also distributes over subtraction: $\frac{a}{b} \times (\frac{c}{d} - \frac{e}{f}) = (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d}) - (\frac{a}{b} \times \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Real-Life Applications**: Multiplication of rational numbers is utilized in scaling quantities, calculating areas with fractional dimensions, and determining proportional rates.

• **Division of Rational Numbers**: Division of a rational number by another non-zero rational number is performed by multiplying the first rational number by the reciprocal of the second rational number.
• **Division Formula**: For any two rational numbers $a/b$ and $c/d$ (where $c/d \neq 0$), division is expressed as $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c}$.
* **The Divisor Constraint**: The divisor (the second rational number) can never be zero, as multiplying by the reciprocal of zero is mathematically undefined.
• **Sign Rules in Division**: The rules for signs in division are identical to multiplication: like signs yield a positive quotient, and unlike signs yield a negative quotient.
• **Closure Property Status**: The set of rational numbers is not strictly closed under division because division by zero is undefined, though division of non-zero rational numbers always yields a rational number.
• **Non-Commutative Property of Division**: Division of rational numbers is not commutative; changing the order of dividend and divisor changes the result: $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} \neq \frac{c}{d} \div \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Non-Associative Property of Division**: Division is not associative for rational numbers, meaning grouping affects the outcome: $(\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d}) \div \frac{e}{f} \neq \frac{a}{b} \div (\frac{c}{d} \div \frac{e}{f})$.
• **Division by One**: Dividing any rational number by $1$ leaves the number unchanged: $\frac{a}{b} \div 1 = \frac{a}{b}$.
• **Division of Zero**: Dividing zero by any non-zero rational number always results in zero: $0 \div \frac{a}{b} = 0$.
• **Self-Division Property**: Any non-zero rational number divided by itself equals $1$: $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{a}{b} = 1$.
• **Simplification of Quotients**: After converting a division problem into a multiplication problem using the reciprocal, apply cross-cancellation and reduce the final result to its standard form.
• **Complex Fraction Interpretation**: A complex fraction (a fraction whose numerator and denominator are themselves fractions or rational numbers) represents division and is solved using the reciprocal rule.
• **Step-by-Step Execution**: Always write out the reciprocal conversion step explicitly before computing the final product to avoid operational errors.
• **Consistency with Fractions**: The division algorithm for rational numbers builds directly upon the "keep-change-flip" rule taught for ordinary fractions.
• **DAV Academic Standard**: DAV assessments frequently test the ability to handle negative signs correctly during the reciprocal conversion step of rational division.

• **Properties of Operations Overview**: The study of rational numbers includes verifying fundamental algebraic properties for addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
• **Summary of Closure Property**: Rational numbers are closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication, but division is excluded only due to the restriction of dividing by zero.
• **Summary of Commutative Property**: Addition and multiplication are commutative for rational numbers, whereas subtraction and division are strictly non-commutative.
• **Summary of Associative Property**: Addition and multiplication are associative for rational numbers, whereas subtraction and division are strictly non-associative.
• **Existence of Identity Elements**: Zero acts as the unique additive identity, and one acts as the unique multiplicative identity for rational numbers.
• **Existence of Inverse Elements**: Every rational number has an additive inverse (its negative), and every non-zero rational number has a multiplicative inverse (its reciprocal).
• **Role of the Distributive Property**: Multiplication distributes over both addition and subtraction, serving as a powerful tool for simplifying complex arithmetic expressions.
• **Order of Operations (BODMAS/PEMDAS)**: When evaluating expressions involving multiple operations on rational numbers, follow the standard BODMAS hierarchy: Brackets, Orders, Division, Multiplication, Addition, and Subtraction.
• **Bracket Hierarchy**: Simplify operations inside parentheses $(~)$, curly braces $\{~\}$, and square brackets $[~]$ in that exact inside-out order.
• **Simplifying Nested Expressions**: Address innermost grouping symbols first when tackling multi-step rational number expressions.
• **Priority of Multiplication/Division over Addition/Subtraction**: Perform all multiplication and division operations from left to right before executing any addition or subtraction operations.
• **Common Pitfalls in Order of Operations**: A frequent error is performing addition before multiplication; strict adherence to BODMAS prevents this mistake.
• **Use of Distributive Property to Simplify**: Instead of adding numbers inside brackets first, multiplying the outer rational number across each term inside can often simplify calculations.
• **Verification of Properties**: DAV problem sets often require students to verify properties like $a + b = b + a$ or $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$ using specific rational number values.
