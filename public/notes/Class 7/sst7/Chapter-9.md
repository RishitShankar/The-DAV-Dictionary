• **Subject and Grade Context**: Welcome to The DAV Dictionary academic study notes for Class 7, We and Our World: Social Science, specifically designed for mastering Chapter 9 titled "Medieval Period".
• **Chapter Overview**: This chapter introduces students to the transitional phase of Indian history bridging the Ancient and Modern periods, marked by profound social, political, cultural, and economic transformations.
• **Chronological Framework**: The study of history is broadly divided into three distinct periods—Ancient, Medieval, and Modern—to help historians categorize and analyze societal evolution systematically.
• **Time Span of Medieval Period**: In Indian history, the medieval period broadly spans a thousand years, extending from the 8th century CE to the 18th century CE.
• **Sub-Division of Medieval Era**: Historians further divide the vast medieval period into two distinct phases: the Early Medieval Period (8th to 13th century) and the Late Medieval Period (13th to 18th century).
• **Early Medieval Dynamics**: The Early Medieval Period was characterized by the dominance of regional kingdoms such as the Palas, Gurjara-Pratiharas, Rashtrakutas, and the Cholas in South India.
• **Late Medieval Dynamics**: The Late Medieval Period witnessed the establishment and expansion of the Delhi Sultanate, followed by the grandeur and consolidation of the Mughal Empire.
• **Transition Catalyst**: The decline of the Gupta Empire and the disintegration of Harsha's empire paved the way for the emergence of numerous regional powers, marking the onset of the medieval era.
• **Geographical Significance**: The Indian subcontinent, often referred to as 'Hindustan' or 'Bharat', experienced continuous cultural synthesis and interaction between indigenous traditions and incoming foreign influences.
• **Naming Conventions**: The term 'Medieval' is derived from Latin words meaning 'middle age', representing a bridge between classical antiquity and the modern world.
• **Nature of Historical Sources**: Unlike the ancient period where archaeology dominates, the medieval period is exceptionally rich in both archaeological and literary sources.
• **Availability of Paper**: The increased production and affordability of paper during the medieval period led to the extensive recording of chronicles, administrative reports, and personal memoirs.
• **Role of Scribes**: Manuscripts in the medieval period were hand-copied by professional scribes, which sometimes introduced minor copying errors and variations in historical texts over time.
• **Preservation Challenges**: Many medieval manuscripts were preserved in royal libraries, monasteries, and temples, though many perished due to climatic conditions, insects, and conflicts.

• **Classification of Sources**: Historians categorize the rich sources of the medieval period into two primary categories: Archaeological Sources and Literary Sources.
• **Archaeological Sources**: These include tangible remains of the past such as monuments, inscriptions, coins, paintings, and artifacts that provide physical evidence of medieval life.
• **Architectural Marvels**: Medieval architecture shifted dramatically with the introduction of Islamic structural styles (domes, arches, minarets) blending harmoniously with indigenous Indian temple architecture.
• **Temple Architecture**: Spectacular temples like the Brihadeshwara Temple at Thanjavur and the Sun Temple at Konark showcase the religious devotion, engineering brilliance, and artistic peak of medieval dynasties.
• **Forts and Palaces**: Massive fortifications such as the Chittorgarh Fort, Mehrangarh Fort, and Daulatabad Fort reflect the strategic military planning and architectural grandeur of medieval rulers.
• **Islamic Monuments**: Iconic structures like the Qutb Minar, Alai Darwaza, Agra Fort, Fatehpur Sikri, and the Taj Mahal represent the zenith of Indo-Islamic architectural synthesis.
• **Epigraphy (Inscriptions)**: Inscriptions engraved on copper plates, temple walls, rocks, and pillars provide authentic data regarding land grants, royal decrees, military victories, and genealogy.
• **Prashastis**: Many inscriptions are in the form of 'Prashastis' (in praise of rulers), composed by court poets to glorify the achievements of kings, though they must be analyzed critically by historians.
• **Numismatics (Coins)**: The study of coins, or numismatics, reveals crucial economic data, including the purity of metals used, monetary systems, trade networks, and the titles assumed by medieval monarchs.
• **Mint Towns and Economic Stability**: Coins issued by rulers like Ala-ud-din Khilji and Muhammad bin Tughluq offer insights into currency reforms, administrative control, and economic fluctuations.
• **Paintings and Miniatures**: Medieval miniature paintings, particularly from the Mughal, Rajput, and Pahari schools, vividly portray court life, flora, fauna, warfare, and mythological narratives.
• **Sculptures**: Bronze and stone sculptures from the Chola period and later medieval epochs demonstrate advanced metallurgical skills and aesthetic refinement.
• **Material Culture**: Everyday objects recovered through excavations, such as pottery, textiles, and tools, help historians reconstruct the socio-economic conditions of common people.
• **Reliability of Archaeology**: Archaeological sources are generally considered more reliable than literary texts because they are less prone to deliberate exaggeration, personal bias, or subjective interpretation.
• **Interdisciplinary Study**: Modern historians combine archaeological excavations with literary evidence to create a balanced, comprehensive, and accurate narrative of medieval Indian society.

• **Literary Sources**: The literary wealth of the medieval period is vast, consisting of royal chronicles, travelogues, biographies, administrative manuals, and religious texts.
• **Significance of Chronicles**: Court chroniclers and historians were often patronized by kings to record contemporary events, resulting in detailed accounts of political maneuvers and military campaigns.
• **Persian as Court Language**: With the establishment of Delhi Sultanate and Mughal rule, Persian became the official language of administration, literature, and courtly correspondence in northern India.
• **Regional Languages Literature**: Simultaneously, literature flourished in regional vernacular languages such as Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Telugu, Tamil, and Punjabi due to the Bhakti movement.
• **Tarikh and Tabaqat**: Historical accounts written in Persian during the Sultanate period are generally classified as 'Tarikh' (history) or 'Tabaqat' (chronicles arranged in generations or sections).
• **Minhaj-us-Siraj**: A prominent chronicler of the early Sultanate period who wrote the 'Tabaqat-i-Nasiri', providing valuable information on the reigns of early Delhi Sultans.
• **Zia-ud-din Barani**: Author of 'Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi', Barani offered critical insights into the administrative policies, revenue systems, and social structure during the Tughluq dynasty.
• **Abul Fazl**: The celebrated court historian of Emperor Akbar who wrote the monumental 'Akbarnama' and its administrative counterpart, the 'Ain-i-Akbari'.
• **Ain-i-Akbari**: A treasure trove of statistical data, providing detailed information on Akbar’s empire, administrative divisions, agrarian system, prices, and cultural life.
• **Autobiographies (Tuzuk)**: Personal memoirs written by rulers themselves offer intimate glimpses into their thoughts, administrative philosophies, daily routines, and personal preferences.
• **Tuzuk-i-Baburi (Baburnama)**: Written in Turkic by Babur, the founder of the Mughal Empire, it describes the geography, flora, fauna, people, and political conditions of Hindustan with remarkable honesty.
• **Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri**: The autobiography of Mughal Emperor Jahangir, known for its keen observations on nature, art, justice system, and his personal life.
• **Prithviraj Raso**: An epic poem composed by Chand Bardai in Apabhramsha/Old Hindi, recounting the heroic deeds and romance of Prithviraj Chauhan, reflecting the Rajput ethos.
• **Rajatarangini**: Written by Kalhana in Sanskrit, this historical chronicle of Kashmir is celebrated as India's first genuine historical text, noted for its critical approach to source evaluation.
• **Biographical Texts**: Works like 'Humayun-nama', written by Humayun's sister Gulbadan Begum, provide unique female perspectives and domestic insights into imperial court politics.

• **Foreign Travellers and Accounts**: The accounts left by foreign travelers, merchants, ambassadors, and pilgrims who visited India during the medieval period serve as vital external mirrors of Indian society.
• **Objectivity of Foreign Accounts**: Foreign travelers often recorded unbiased observations about customs, trade, social stratification, and governance because they were neutral observers outside local political pressures.
• **Al-Biruni**: A brilliant polymath from Central Asia who accompanied Mahmud of Ghazni to India and authored 'Kitab-ul-Hind', offering a deep analysis of Indian sciences, religion, caste system, and philosophy.
• **Ibn Battuta**: A Moroccan traveler who arrived in India during the reign of Muhammad bin Tughluq and wrote 'Rihla', detailing the postal system, social life, bustling markets, and administrative conditions.
• **Marco Polo**: The famous Venetian traveler who visited South India by sea and left vivid accounts of the maritime trade, wealth, and prosperity of the Pandyan and Chola kingdoms.
• **Niccolo Conti**: An Italian merchant who traveled through the Vijayanagara Empire, documenting its immense wealth, administrative efficiency, and grand festivals.
• **Domingo Paes**: A Portuguese traveler whose detailed chronicle of Vijayanagara during the reign of Krishnadevaraya paints a picture of a flourishing, cosmopolitan, and well-fortified empire.
• **Francois Bernier**: A French physician who spent years at the Mughal court during the reign of Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb, comparing the socio-economic conditions of India with Europe.
• **Jean-Baptiste Tavernier**: A French gem merchant who traveled extensively across India, providing detailed accounts of diamond mines, jewelry, trade routes, and the luxury of the Mughal nobility.
• **Impact of Travelogues**: These diverse foreign writings help historians cross-verify indigenous records and understand how medieval India was perceived by the outside world.
• **Evolution of Vocabulary**: Medieval texts demonstrate a fascinating linguistic evolution where words acquired new meanings over time, reflecting changing socio-political contexts.
• **Definition of 'Hindustan'**: Initially used by Minhaj-us-Siraj in the 13th century to mean the lands of Punjab, Haryana, and the lands between the Ganga and Yamuna, the term later expanded to represent the entire Indian subcontinent.
• **Concept of 'Foreigner'**: In medieval times, a 'foreigner' (or 'ajnabi' / 'pardesi') did not necessarily mean someone from outside the subcontinent; it often referred to any person who was not part of a specific village or social group.
• **Caste and Community**: Social stratification became more rigid during the medieval period, with the proliferation of sub-castes known as 'jatis', each governed by its own set of rules and panchayats.
• **Synthesis of Cultures**: The interaction between different religious communities, languages, and regional cultures created a unique composite Indian culture (*Ganga-Jamuni tehzeeb*).

• **Political Developments**: The political landscape of medieval India was dynamic, marked by the rise, expansion, and fall of numerous regional kingdoms, imperial states, and foreign incursions.
• **Early Medieval Regional Powers**: Between the 8th and 10th centuries, northern and central India witnessed a prolonged multi-lateral struggle for supremacy known as the Tripartite Struggle.
• **The Tripartite Struggle**: A fierce, century-long conflict fought among three major dynasties—the Gurjara-Pratiharas, the Palas, and the Rashtrakutas—for control over the prized city of Kannauj.
• **Significance of Kannauj**: Kannauj was strategically located in the fertile Gangetic plain, possessed immense economic resources, and symbolized imperial sovereignty in northern India.
• **The Rajputs**: Several Rajput clans, such as the Chauhans, Chandelas, Paramaras, and Solankis, emerged as powerful ruling elites in northern and western India, known for their valor and patronage of art.
• **Invasions from the Northwest**: The political fragmentation of northern India made the subcontinent vulnerable to external raids from Central Asian warlords seeking wealth and territorial expansion.
• **Mahmud of Ghazni**: Between 1000 CE and 1027 CE, the ruler of Ghazni invaded India seventeen times, primarily targeting wealthy temples such as Somnath to plunder riches to finance his Central Asian empire.
• **Muhammad Ghori**: Unlike Mahmud, Muhammad Ghori aimed to establish permanent political dominion and Muslim rule in India, laying the groundwork for the establishment of the Delhi Sultanate.
• **Battles of Tarain**: The decisive battles fought at Tarain in 1191 CE and 1192 CE between Prithviraj Chauhan and Muhammad Ghori dramatically altered the course of Indian history.
• **First Battle of Tarain (1191 CE)**: Prithviraj Chauhan decisively defeated Muhammad Ghori, forcing him to retreat to Ghor.
• **Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE)**: Muhammad Ghori returned with enhanced military tactics and defeated Prithviraj Chauhan, opening the floodgates for Turkish and Afghan rule in India.
• **Establishment of Delhi Sultanate**: Following Ghori's assassination, his general Qutb-ud-din Aibak established the Slave Dynasty in 1206 CE, marking the formal beginning of the Delhi Sultanate.
• **Dynasties of Delhi Sultanate**: The Delhi Sultanate was successively ruled by five distinct dynasties: the Slave, Khilji, Tughluq, Sayyid, and Lodi dynasties.
• **Administrative Experiments**: Sultans like Muhammad bin Tughluq introduced bold administrative experiments, such as token currency and shifting the capital to Daulatabad, showcasing administrative ambition.
• **Foundation of Mughal Empire**: The Delhi Sultanate culminated with the First Battle of Panipat in 1526 CE, where Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi, establishing the magnificent Mughal Empire.

• **Socio-Economic Conditions**: The medieval economy was predominantly agrarian, supported by vibrant trade networks, artisanal production, and a well-defined hierarchical social structure.
• **Agrarian Base**: Agriculture was the primary occupation of the vast majority of the rural population, with peasants cultivating a variety of food crops, cash crops, and spices.
• **Land Revenue System**: Land revenue was the principal source of income for the state, calculated as a share of agricultural produce, meticulously assessed and collected by imperial officials.
• **Village Community**: The Indian village functioned largely as a self-sufficient economic unit, where farmers, blacksmiths, potters, weavers, and carpenters bartered or shared goods and services.
• **Monetization of Economy**: The extensive circulation of silver coins (like the *tanka*) and copper coins (like the *jital*) during the Sultanate and Mughal periods greatly facilitated domestic trade and commerce.
• **Guilds (Shrenis)**: Craftspeople, merchants, and traders organized themselves into guilds (*shrenis* or *sanghs*) that regulated prices, maintained quality control, and trained apprentices.
• **Internal and External Trade**: India maintained robust internal trade routes connecting production centers with ports, alongside flourishing maritime trade with Southeast Asia, West Asia, and Europe.
• **Major Export Items**: India exported fine cotton textiles, silk, spices, precious stones, indigo, and handicrafts, earning vast amounts of gold and silver in return.
• **Major Import Items**: Imports included horses from Central Asia, chinaware, raw silk, and luxury goods meant for the consumption of the wealthy nobility.
• **Urbanization**: The medieval period witnessed the growth of magnificent urban centers and garrison towns (*shahr*) such as Delhi, Agra, Lahore, Ahmedabad, Thanjavur, and Calicut.
• **Social Hierarchy**: Society was sharply divided along economic and social lines; at the apex stood the monarch and the wealthy nobility (*umara*), followed by merchants, artisans, and peasants.
• **Position of Women**: Women experienced varying degrees of freedom and status; while women from royal and elite families often participated in politics, arts, and administration, common women faced social restrictions.
• **Practices like Purdah and Sati**: Social evils such as the purdah system, child marriage, and sati became increasingly prevalent among certain upper-caste communities during the medieval period.
• **Slavery**: Slavery existed in various forms; slaves were employed as domestic servants, agricultural laborers, bodyguards, and high-ranking military commanders (like the Mamluks).
• **Standard of Living**: While the royal court and nobility indulged in unmatched luxury, the common peasantry often suffered from heavy taxation, natural calamities, and famines.

• **Cultural Developments and Religious Movements**: The medieval period was a crucible of profound cultural synthesis, witnessing the birth of revolutionary religious movements and artistic peaks.
• **The Bhakti Movement**: A major socio-religious movement that swept across India, emphasizing pure devotion (*bhakti*), personal communion with God, and universal human brotherhood.
• **Core Tenets of Bhakti**: Bhakti reformers rejected elaborate rituals, caste distinctions, and blind orthodoxy, preaching that God could be attained through love, devotion, and chanting of divine names.
• **Saints of South India**: The Bhakti movement originated in South India, spearheaded by the Alvars (devotees of Vishnu) and Nayanars (devotees of Shiva) through Tamil hymns and poetry.
• **Prominent Bhakti Saints**: Figures like Kabir, Guru Nanak Dev, Mirabai, Surdas, Tulsidas, Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, and Tukaram brought the message of devotion to the common masses in vernacular languages.
• **Kabir**: A weaver poet from Varanasi who criticized religious hypocrisy, caste rigidity, and dogmas of both Hinduism and Islam, preaching a form of formless divinity (*nirguna bhakti*).
• **Guru Nanak Dev**: The founder of Sikhism, who taught the unity of God, equality of all human beings, honesty, and service to humanity, laying down the foundations of the Sikh faith.
• **The Sufi Movement**: A mystical Islamic movement that emphasized spiritual proximity to God through inner purity, asceticism, love, and service to humanity.
• **Sufi Saints (Sufis)**: Prominent Sufi orders (*silsilas*), such as the Chishti and Suhrawardi orders, established hospices (*khanqahs*) that welcomed people from all castes and creeds without discrimination.
• **Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti**: The pioneering Sufi saint of the Chishti order in India, whose shrine (*Dargah*) at Ajmer remains a symbol of communal harmony and spiritual devotion.
• **Music and Qawwali**: Sufi mysticism gave birth to devotional music forms like Qawwali, while classical Indian music absorbed Persian influences, leading to the creation of new ragas and instruments (sitar, tabla).
• **Development of Languages**: The mingling of Persian, Arabic, and Turkic words with local Prakrit languages gave birth to new vibrant languages like Urdu and Hindustani.
• **Literature in Vernaculars**: Epic literature in regional languages flourished, such as Tulsidas’s 'Ramcharitmanas' in Awadhi and the compositions of Marathi saints in 'Abhangs'.
• **Festivals and Fairs**: Cultural synthesis was visible in the joint celebration of festivals, fairs, and culinary traditions that blended indigenous Indian spices with Middle Eastern cooking styles.
• **Legacy of Synthesis**: The spiritual and cultural movements of the medieval era forged a resilient foundation of tolerance, pluralism, and composite culture in Indian society.

• **Regional Kingdoms of South India**: While northern India witnessed the rise and fall of various sultanates, South India was dominated by powerful, culturally rich, and maritime-oriented empires.
• **The Chola Empire**: One of the greatest ruling dynasties of South India, the Chola Empire reached the pinnacle of its glory under rulers like Rajaraja I and his son Rajendra I.
• **Naval Supremacy**: The Cholas maintained a formidable naval fleet that enabled them to conquer Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and establish maritime trade dominance across Southeast Asia (Sumatra, Java, Malaya).
• **Chola Local Self-Government**: The Cholas are globally celebrated for pioneering a sophisticated system of local self-government, particularly through village assemblies known as 'Ur', 'Sabha', and 'Nagaram'.
• **Uttiramerur Inscriptions**: Famous inscriptions found at Uttiramerur provide detailed descriptions of the democratic election procedures, qualifications, and duties of members forming the village committees.
• **Temple as Economic Hubs**: Chola temples were not merely places of worship but served as vast economic centers that owned land, employed hundreds of priests, artisans, and musicians, and financed irrigation works.
• **Bronze Casting**: The Cholas excelled in metallurgy, producing exquisite bronze sculptures of Nataraja (Lord Shiva as the cosmic dancer) using the intricate lost-wax casting technique.
• **The Vijayanagara Empire**: Founded in 1336 CE by Harihara and Bukka on the southern banks of the Tungabhadra River, Vijayanagara became a bastion of Hindu culture and economic prosperity.
• **Krishnadevaraya**: The most illustrious ruler of the Tuluva dynasty of Vijayanagara, renowned for his military conquests, patronage of Telugu literature (*Ashtadiggajas*), and architectural additions.
• **Hampi**: The magnificent capital city of Vijayanagara, whose ruins today stand as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, showcasing stupendous stone chariots, pillared halls, and intricate temples.
• **The Bahamani Kingdom**: Founded in 1347 CE by Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah after revolting against the Delhi Sultanate, forming a major Muslim power in the Deccan region.
• **Deccan Culture**: The Bahamani kingdom, and its later successor Sultanates (Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar), fostered a unique Deccan culture characterized by grand domes, fortifications, and patronage of miniature painting.
• **Trade in South India**: Southern ports such as Calicut, Cochin, Masulipatnam, and Quilon were bustling hubs connecting global maritime trade routes linking China, the Middle East, and Europe.
• **Artistic Patronage**: Southern dynasties generously patronized classical Carnatic music, Bharatanatyam dance forms, and monumental temple architecture that still define South Indian heritage.
• **Historical Significance**: The study of medieval South India highlights sophisticated administrative decentralization, economic resilience, and extraordinary cultural efflorescence.

• **The Mughal Empire and Administration**: The establishment of the Mughal Empire in 1526 CE ushered in a period of unprecedented political centralization, administrative efficiency, and cultural splendor.
• **Akbar the Great**: Regarded as the true architect of the Mughal Empire, Akbar consolidated his vast territories through strategic marital alliances, military conquests, and a policy of religious tolerance.
• **Mansabdari System**: Introduced by Akbar, this unique administrative and military grading system determined the rank (*mansab*), salary, and military obligations of imperial officials (*mansabdars*).
• **Zabt System (Land Revenue)**: Developed by Raja Todar Mal, the Zabt system involved precise measurement of land, classification based on fertility, and fixation of state demand in cash.
• **Sulh-i-Kul**: Akbar’s visionary policy of 'universal peace' and religious tolerance, which abolished the discriminatory tax (*Jizya*) on non-Muslims and promoted inter-faith dialogue at the 'Ibadat Khana'.
• **Decline of Tolerance**: Later Mughal emperors, particularly Aurangzeb, shifted toward orthodox religious policies, reintroducing Jizya and alienating regional allies like the Rajputs, Sikhs, and Marathas.
• **Mughal Art and Painting**: Mughal emperors established specialized royal workshops (*karkhanas*) where master painters created breathtaking illustrated manuscripts and miniature albums.
• **Architectural Grandeur**: Mughal architecture reached its absolute peak under Shah Jahan, who utilized white marble, intricate inlay work (*Pietra Dura*), and symmetrical gardens (*Charbagh*).
• **Decline of the Mughal Empire**: Following the death of Aurangzeb in 1707 CE, the Mughal Empire suffered from weak successors, endless wars of succession, financial bankruptcy, and vast territorial overexpansion.
• **Rise of Successor States**: As imperial Mughal authority weakened, powerful regional states such as Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad, and the Maratha Confederacy broke away to carve out independent domains.
• **The Maratha Power**: Under the brilliant military leadership of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, the Marathas emerged as a formidable force, challenging Mughal hegemony and establishing 'Swarajya'.
• **Administrative Innovations of Shivaji**: Shivaji established a centralized administration assisted by a council of eight ministers known as the 'Ashtapradhan', while relying on guerrilla warfare tactics.
• **Chauth and Sardeshmukhi**: The Marathas levied taxes known as *Chauth* (25% of revenue) and *Sardeshmukhi* (additional 10%) on neighboring territories in exchange for protection from raids.
• **Pan-Indian Impact**: The constant friction between the Mughals, Marathas, Rajputs, and emerging European trading companies reshaped the geopolitical landscape of 18th-century India.
• **Threshold of Modernity**: The political fragmentation of the 18th century and the decline of the Mughal Empire created a power vacuum that was ultimately exploited by British colonial expansion.

• **Historiographical Analysis and Conclusion**: Studying the medieval period requires critical evaluation of historical evidence, unbiased analysis of societal shifts, and appreciation of our rich heritage.
• **Continuity and Change**: History is never a sudden break; the medieval period demonstrates profound continuity in agrarian practices alongside massive innovations in governance, urban culture, and technology.
• **Technological Innovations**: The medieval era introduced significant technological advancements to India, including the Persian wheel for irrigation, spinning wheels (*charkha*), firearms, gunpowder, and paper-making.
• **Culinary Evolution**: Indian cuisine was dramatically enriched during the medieval period through the introduction of culinary techniques, spices, dry fruits, and dishes brought by Central Asian and Persian influences.
• **Textile Revolutions**: The widespread use of the spinning wheel revolutionized textile production, making India the textile capital of the world and fueling global commercial demand.
• **Urban-Rural Interdependence**: The growth of garrison towns and imperial capitals created strong economic links, drawing surplus agricultural produce from villages to feed urban populations and artisans.
• **Educational Centers**: Medieval centers of learning flourished in institutions attached to mosques, temples, and monastic complexes, imparting education in grammar, theology, mathematics, and astronomy.
• **Preservation of Heritage**: Historical monuments, coins, manuscripts, and inscriptions act as silent witnesses to the triumphs, struggles, and creativity of our ancestors across the medieval millennium.

• **Global Connections**: Medieval India was never isolated; it maintained active maritime and overland trade routes, diplomatic missions, and intellectual exchanges with the wider world.
