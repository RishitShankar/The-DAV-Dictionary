• **Geometry: Introduction**: Geometry is the branch of mathematics that deals with the study of shapes, sizes, positions, and properties of space.
• **Point**: A point is an exact location in space, represented by a fine dot made with a sharp pencil.
• **Point Characteristics**: A point has no length, no breadth, and no thickness; it is dimensionless.
• **Naming Points**: Points are usually named using capital letters of the English alphabet, such as Point A, Point B, or Point P.
• **Line**: A line is a straight path that extends infinitely in both directions without any endpoints.
• **Line Representation**: A line is represented by drawing a straight line with arrows at both ends to indicate it goes on forever.
• **Naming Lines**: A line passing through points A and B is denoted as line AB or line BA, often written with a double-headed arrow symbol above the letters.
• **Line Segment**: A line segment is a part of a line that has two fixed endpoints, meaning it has a definite length.
• **Measuring Line Segments**: Because a line segment has a fixed length, it can be measured using a ruler in centimeters or millimeters.
• **Naming Line Segments**: A line segment with endpoints P and Q is written as segment PQ or segment QP.
• **Ray**: A ray is a part of a line that has one fixed starting point and extends infinitely in only one direction.
• **Ray Representation**: A ray is drawn with a dot at the starting point and an arrow at the other end to show the direction of extension.
• **Ray Examples**: A beam of light from a torch or the rays of the sun are real-life examples of rays.
• **Naming Rays**: A ray starting at point A and passing through point B is denoted as ray AB.
• **Distinction**: A line segment has two endpoints, a line has no endpoints, and a ray has only one endpoint.
• **Drawing Lines**: To draw a straight line, one must use a ruler or a straight-edge tool to ensure precision.

• **Plane Surfaces**: A plane surface is a flat surface that extends infinitely in all directions, such as the surface of a tabletop or a sheet of paper.
• **Curved Surfaces**: A curved surface is a surface that is not flat, such as the surface of a ball, a globe, or an egg.
• **Solid Shapes**: Solid shapes, also known as 3D (three-dimensional) shapes, occupy space and have length, breadth, and height.
• **Cube**: A cube is a solid shape where all six faces are identical squares.
• **Cube Properties**: A cube has 6 faces, 12 edges, and 8 corners (vertices).
• **Real-life Cube**: A standard dice used in board games is the most common example of a cube.
• **Cuboid**: A cuboid is a solid shape where the faces are rectangles; it is also known as a rectangular prism.
• **Cuboid Properties**: Like a cube, a cuboid has 6 faces, 12 edges, and 8 corners.
• **Real-life Cuboid**: A brick, a shoebox, or a duster are classic examples of cuboid-shaped objects.
• **Cylinder**: A cylinder is a solid shape with two circular flat faces and one curved surface.
• **Cylinder Edges**: A cylinder has 2 curved edges and no corners (vertices).
• **Real-life Cylinder**: A cold drink can, a pipe, or a pencil are examples of cylinders.
• **Sphere**: A sphere is a perfectly round solid shape with no flat faces, no edges, and no corners.
• **Sphere Property**: Every point on the surface of a sphere is at the same distance from its center.
• **Real-life Sphere**: A football, a cricket ball, or a globe are examples of spheres.
• **Cone**: A cone is a solid shape with one flat circular base and one curved surface that tapers to a point called the apex.

---PAGE-BREAK---

• **Cone Properties**: A cone has 1 flat face, 1 curved face, 1 circular edge, and 1 vertex (the apex).
• **Real-life Cone**: An ice cream cone or a birthday party cap are common examples of cones.
• **Faces**: A face is a flat or curved surface of a solid shape.
• **Edges**: An edge is a line segment where two faces of a solid shape meet.
• **Vertices**: A vertex (plural: vertices) is a point where three or more edges of a solid shape meet, commonly referred to as a corner.
• **Plane Figures**: Plane figures are 2D (two-dimensional) shapes that have only length and breadth, such as squares, rectangles, triangles, and circles.
• **Rectangle**: A rectangle is a plane figure with four sides and four corners.
• **Rectangle Property**: In a rectangle, the opposite sides are equal in length.
• **Rectangle Angles**: All four corners of a rectangle are right angles (90 degrees).
• **Square**: A square is a special type of rectangle where all four sides are equal in length.
• **Square Property**: Like a rectangle, a square has four corners, and all its angles are right angles.
• **Triangle**: A triangle is a plane figure bounded by three line segments.
• **Triangle Properties**: A triangle has 3 sides, 3 vertices, and 3 angles.
• **Circle**: A circle is a closed plane figure formed by a curved line.
• **Circle Property**: A circle has no sides and no corners; it is defined by its center point and radius.
• **Radius**: The radius is the distance from the center of the circle to any point on its boundary.

---PAGE-BREAK---

• **Diameter**: The diameter is a line segment passing through the center of the circle, with both endpoints on the circle's boundary.
• **Diameter Relationship**: The diameter is always twice the length of the radius (Diameter = 2 × Radius).
• **Curved Lines**: A curved line is a line that changes its direction constantly and is not straight.
• **Straight Lines**: A straight line is the shortest distance between two points and does not change direction.
• **Horizontal Lines**: A horizontal line is a straight line that runs from left to right, parallel to the horizon.
• **Vertical Lines**: A vertical line is a straight line that runs from top to bottom, perpendicular to the horizon.
• **Slanting Lines**: A slanting line is a straight line that is neither horizontal nor vertical; it is tilted.
• **Drawing Shapes**: Using a ruler, one can draw straight-sided shapes like squares, rectangles, and triangles.
• **Freehand Drawing**: Curved shapes like circles are often drawn using a compass or by tracing round objects.
• **Tangram**: A tangram is a dissection puzzle consisting of seven flat shapes, called tans, which are put together to form shapes.
• **Tangram Pieces**: The seven pieces of a tangram include two large triangles, one medium triangle, two small triangles, one square, and one parallelogram.
• **Geometry in Nature**: Geometric patterns are found everywhere in nature, such as the symmetry of a butterfly or the hexagonal shape of a honeycomb.
• **Symmetry**: Symmetry occurs when a shape can be divided into two identical halves that mirror each other.
• **Line of Symmetry**: The line of symmetry is the imaginary line along which a figure can be folded so that the two halves match perfectly.
• **Asymmetrical Shapes**: Shapes that do not have any line of symmetry are called asymmetrical.
• **Geometric Patterns**: Patterns created by repeating geometric shapes are called tessellations or tiling patterns.

• **Solid vs. Plane**: Solid shapes occupy space (3D), while plane shapes are flat (2D).
• **Faces of a Cube**: All 6 faces of a cube are squares of the same size.
• **Faces of a Cuboid**: A cuboid has 6 faces, which are usually rectangles, though some may be squares.
• **Edges of a Cube/Cuboid**: Both cubes and cuboids have 12 edges.
• **Vertices of a Cube/Cuboid**: Both cubes and cuboids have 8 vertices.
• **Curved Surface of Cylinder**: The curved surface of a cylinder, when flattened, forms a rectangle.
• **Base of a Cone**: The base of a cone is a circle.
• **Apex of a Cone**: The apex is the pointed top of the cone, which is not a corner in the same sense as a cube's vertex.
• **Circle Boundary**: The boundary of a circle is called its circumference.
• **Center of a Circle**: The center is the fixed point inside the circle from which all points on the circumference are equidistant.
• **Drawing a Circle**: To draw a perfect circle, a compass is used with one leg acting as the center point and the other as the drawing lead.
• **Relationship of Sides**: In a rectangle, the two longer sides are called length, and the two shorter sides are called breadth.
• **Triangle Types**: Triangles can be classified based on side lengths (equilateral, isosceles, scalene) or angles, though primary study focuses on the basic 3-sided property.
• **Polygon**: A polygon is a closed plane figure made up of three or more line segments.
• **Triangle as Polygon**: A triangle is the simplest polygon because it is the shape with the fewest possible sides.
• **Quadrilateral**: A quadrilateral is a polygon with four sides, such as a square, rectangle, rhombus, or trapezoid.

• **Vertex Definition**: A vertex is the point where two lines or edges meet.
• **Edge Definition**: An edge is the boundary where two faces of a solid meet.
• **Face Definition**: A face is the flat or curved surface of a solid object.
• **Solid Shape Identification**: To identify a solid, count its faces, edges, and vertices.
• **Cube Identification**: If a solid has 6 square faces, 12 equal edges, and 8 vertices, it is a cube.
• **Cuboid Identification**: If a solid has 6 rectangular faces, 12 edges, and 8 vertices, it is a cuboid.
• **Cylinder Identification**: If a solid has 2 circular faces and 1 curved surface, it is a cylinder.
• **Sphere Identification**: If a solid has only 1 curved surface and no edges or vertices, it is a sphere.
• **Cone Identification**: If a solid has 1 circular base, 1 curved surface, and 1 apex, it is a cone.
• **Plane Figure Identification**: Plane figures are identified by the number of sides and corners they possess.
• **Triangle Identification**: A figure with 3 sides and 3 corners is a triangle.
• **Rectangle Identification**: A figure with 4 sides, 4 corners, and opposite sides equal is a rectangle.
• **Square Identification**: A figure with 4 equal sides and 4 corners is a square.
• **Circle Identification**: A figure with a continuous curved line and no corners is a circle.
• **Geometric Tools**: Essential tools for geometry include a ruler, a pencil, an eraser, and a compass.
• **Precision**: Using sharp pencils is crucial in geometry to ensure that lines are thin and accurate.

• **Straight Line Drawing**: Always use a ruler to draw straight lines to ensure they are not wavy.
• **Measuring Segments**: When measuring a line segment, align the zero mark of the ruler with one endpoint.
• **Reading Measurements**: Read the mark on the ruler that aligns with the second endpoint to determine the length.
• **Units of Measurement**: Standard units for measuring line segments in geometry are centimeters (cm) and millimeters (mm).
• **Conversion**: 1 centimeter (cm) is equal to 10 millimeters (mm).
• **Drawing a Rectangle**: To draw a rectangle, draw two pairs of parallel lines of different lengths and join the ends.
• **Drawing a Square**: To draw a square, draw four lines of equal length at right angles to each other.
• **Drawing a Triangle**: Connect three points that are not in a straight line using a ruler to form a triangle.
• **Drawing a Circle**: Place the needle point of the compass at the center and rotate the pencil point around it to form the circumference.
• **Compass Safety**: Always handle the sharp needle point of the compass with care to avoid injury.
• **Geometric Patterns in Art**: Many traditional designs, such as Rangoli or floor tiles, use geometric shapes to create symmetry.
• **Symmetry in Letters**: Some English letters like A, M, T, U, V, W, and Y have a vertical line of symmetry.
• **Symmetry in Numbers**: The number 8 has both vertical and horizontal lines of symmetry.
• **Real-world Geometry**: Architects use geometric principles to design buildings, ensuring they are stable and aesthetically pleasing.
• **Engineering Geometry**: Engineers use geometric shapes to create strong structures like bridges and towers.
• **Importance of Geometry**: Geometry helps in understanding the physical world, measuring distances, and creating structural designs.

• **Parallel Lines**: Parallel lines are two or more lines that are always the same distance apart and never meet, no matter how far they are extended.
• **Intersecting Lines**: Intersecting lines are lines that cross each other at a single common point.
• **Point of Intersection**: The point where two lines cross is called the point of intersection.
• **Perpendicular Lines**: Perpendicular lines are a special type of intersecting lines that meet at a 90-degree angle (a right angle).
• **Right Angle**: A right angle is an angle of 90 degrees, often found at the corners of squares and rectangles.
• **Acute Angle**: An acute angle is an angle that is smaller than a right angle (less than 90 degrees).
• **Obtuse Angle**: An obtuse angle is an angle that is larger than a right angle but smaller than a straight line (between 90 and 180 degrees).
• **Angle Definition**: An angle is formed when two rays meet at a common endpoint called the vertex.
• **Arms of an Angle**: The two rays that form an angle are called the arms of the angle.
• **Naming Angles**: An angle is named using three letters, with the vertex letter in the middle (e.g., angle ABC, where B is the vertex).
• **Protractor**: A protractor is a semi-circular tool used to measure and draw angles in degrees.
• **Degrees**: Angles are measured in units called degrees, represented by the symbol (°).
• **Full Circle**: A full circle represents 360 degrees.
• **Straight Angle**: A straight line forms an angle of 180 degrees.
• **Zero Angle**: When two rays overlap, the angle between them is 0 degrees.
• **Geometry Vocabulary**: Mastering terms like vertex, edge, face, and angle is fundamental to understanding higher-level mathematics.

• **Shapes in Daily Life**: Look around the classroom to identify cuboid-shaped books, cylindrical pens, and spherical balls.
• **Surface Area Concept**: While not explicitly calculated in Class 3, the concept of a "face" introduces the idea of surface area.
• **Volume Concept**: The space occupied by a solid shape introduces the basic concept of volume.
• **2D vs 3D Visualization**: Being able to visualize a 3D object from a 2D drawing is a key geometric skill.
• **Nets of Solids**: A net is a 2D pattern that can be folded to form a 3D shape; for example, a cross-shaped net can be folded into a cube.
• **Tessellation**: A tessellation is a pattern of shapes that fit together without any gaps or overlaps.
• **Common Tessellating Shapes**: Squares, rectangles, and hexagons are common shapes used in tiling floors.
• **Symmetry Testing**: To test for symmetry, fold a paper cutout of a shape; if the edges align perfectly, the fold line is a line of symmetry.
• **Reflection**: Symmetry is often referred to as reflectional symmetry because one half is a reflection of the other.
• **Rotation**: Rotational symmetry occurs when a shape looks the same after being rotated by a certain angle.
• **Geometric Logic**: Geometry teaches logical thinking by requiring students to identify properties and classify shapes based on those properties.
• **Spatial Awareness**: Studying geometry improves spatial awareness, which is the ability to understand the position and size of objects in space.
• **Measurement Accuracy**: Always ensure the ruler is placed flat against the paper to avoid parallax errors when measuring.
• **Compass Handling**: Tighten the screw on the compass to ensure the pencil does not slip while drawing a circle.
• **Geometric Creativity**: Use different shapes to create complex drawings, such as a house made of a square and a triangle.
• **Summary of Geometry**: Geometry is the language of shapes, providing the tools to describe, measure, and create the world around us.
