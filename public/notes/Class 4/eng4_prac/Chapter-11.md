• **Definition of Interrogatives**: Interrogatives are words used to frame questions to seek information, clarification, or confirmation about a subject, action, or state of being.
• **Function of Interrogatives**: They serve as the primary linguistic tools for initiating inquiry and facilitating communication in both spoken and written English.
• **The 'Wh-' Family**: Most interrogative words begin with the letters 'Wh-', which is why they are collectively referred to as 'Wh-words' or 'Wh-questions'.
• **Role of 'Who'**: The interrogative 'Who' is used exclusively to inquire about the identity of a person or people.
• **Example of 'Who'**: 'Who is the class monitor?' focuses on identifying a specific individual within a group.
• **Role of 'Whom'**: The interrogative 'Whom' is used to ask about a person when they are the object of a verb or preposition.
• **Role of 'Whose'**: The interrogative 'Whose' is used to inquire about possession or ownership of an object or quality.
• **Example of 'Whose'**: 'Whose book is lying on the desk?' seeks to identify the owner of the specific item.
• **Role of 'What'**: The interrogative 'What' is used to ask for information about things, actions, or ideas.
• **Example of 'What'**: 'What are you reading?' asks for the specific title or content of a book.
• **Role of 'Which'**: The interrogative 'Which' is used when there is a limited choice or a specific set of options to select from.
• **Distinction between 'What' and 'Which'**: 'What' is used for general inquiries, whereas 'Which' implies a selection from a defined group.
• **Role of 'Where'**: The interrogative 'Where' is used to inquire about the location, place, or position of an entity.
• **Example of 'Where'**: 'Where is the school library?' seeks the geographical location of a facility.
• **Role of 'When'**: The interrogative 'When' is used to ask about time, duration, or the specific moment an event occurs.
• **Example of 'When'**: 'When does the school assembly begin?' seeks the temporal schedule of an event.
• **Role of 'Why'**: The interrogative 'Why' is used to inquire about the reason, cause, or purpose behind an action or situation.
• **Example of 'Why'**: 'Why are you late?' asks for the justification or cause of a delay.

• **Role of 'How'**: The interrogative 'How' is used to ask about the manner, method, or way in which something is done.
• **'How' for Condition**: 'How' is also used to inquire about the state of health or the condition of a person or thing.
• **Example of 'How' (Manner)**: 'How do you solve this math problem?' asks for the process or steps involved.
• **Example of 'How' (Condition)**: 'How are you feeling today?' asks about the current state of well-being.
• **Interrogative Adverbs**: Words like 'Where', 'When', 'Why', and 'How' are classified as interrogative adverbs because they modify verbs by asking about circumstances.
• **Interrogative Pronouns**: Words like 'Who', 'Whom', 'Whose', 'What', and 'Which' function as interrogative pronouns because they replace nouns in a sentence.
• **Sentence Structure**: Interrogative sentences typically place the interrogative word at the very beginning of the sentence.
• **Inversion Rule**: In many interrogative sentences, the auxiliary verb (is, are, was, were, do, does) is placed before the subject.
• **Punctuation Requirement**: Every interrogative sentence must conclude with a question mark (?) to signal the end of the inquiry.
• **Capitalization**: The first letter of the interrogative word must always be capitalized when it starts a sentence.
• **Subject-Verb Agreement**: Even in questions, the verb must agree in number and person with the subject that follows it.
• **'How' with Adjectives**: 'How' can be combined with adjectives to form specific questions, such as 'How tall', 'How old', or 'How fast'.
• **'How' with Adverbs**: 'How' can be combined with adverbs, such as 'How often' or 'How quickly', to inquire about frequency or speed.
• **'How much'**: Used specifically to ask about the quantity of uncountable nouns.
• **'How many'**: Used specifically to ask about the quantity of countable nouns.
• **Example of 'How much'**: 'How much water is in the bottle?' focuses on an uncountable substance.
• **Example of 'How many'**: 'How many students are in the class?' focuses on countable individuals.
• **Importance of Context**: The choice of the correct interrogative word depends entirely on the context of the information being sought.

• **The 'Yes/No' Question Mechanism**: These are interrogative sentences that do not start with 'Wh-' words but start with auxiliary verbs.
• **Auxiliary Verbs**: These include 'is', 'am', 'are', 'was', 'were', 'do', 'does', 'did', 'has', 'have', 'had', 'can', 'will', and 'shall'.
• **Nature of 'Yes/No' Questions**: They are designed to elicit a confirmation or denial rather than a descriptive explanation.
• **Example of 'Yes/No'**: 'Are you going to school?' can only be answered with 'Yes' or 'No'.
• **Transformation**: A statement like 'He is happy' can be transformed into a question by inverting the subject and verb: 'Is he happy?'
• **Use of 'Do/Does'**: When a sentence has no auxiliary verb, 'do' or 'does' is added to the beginning to form a question.
• **Example of 'Do/Does'**: 'She likes apples' becomes 'Does she like apples?'
• **Tense Consistency**: When using 'does' or 'did', the main verb in the question must return to its base form.
• **Example of Tense Change**: 'He played football' becomes 'Did he play football?' (Note: 'played' changes to 'play').
• **Tag Questions**: These are short questions added to the end of a statement to seek confirmation or agreement.
• **Structure of Tag Questions**: They consist of an auxiliary verb and a pronoun, often in the negative form if the statement is positive.
• **Example of Tag**: 'It is a sunny day, isn't it?'
• **Negative Tag Rule**: If the main sentence is negative, the tag question must be positive.
• **Example of Negative Tag**: 'You are not tired, are you?'
• **Importance of Intonation**: In spoken English, the tone of voice rises at the end of a question to indicate an inquiry.
• **Clarity in Inquiry**: Interrogatives help in narrowing down broad topics into specific data points.
• **Logical Sequencing**: Interrogatives allow for a structured flow of conversation, moving from general to specific information.
• **Educational Application**: Teachers use interrogatives to assess student comprehension and encourage critical thinking.

• **'Whose' vs 'Who's'**: A common error is confusing 'Whose' (possessive) with 'Who's' (contraction of 'Who is').
• **'Whose' Usage**: 'Whose bag is this?' (Asking about ownership).
• **'Who's' Usage**: 'Who's coming to the party?' (Asking who is coming).
• **'Which' vs 'What' Nuance**: 'Which' implies a choice from a set; 'What' is open-ended.
• **Example of 'Which'**: 'Which color do you prefer, red or blue?' (Limited choice).
• **Example of 'What'**: 'What is your favorite color?' (Open-ended).
• **'Why' and Reasoning**: 'Why' questions often require a conjunction like 'because' in the response to explain the cause.
• **'How' and Process**: 'How' questions often require a sequence of steps or a description of a method.
• **Interrogative Pronoun vs Adjective**: An interrogative word is an adjective if it is followed immediately by a noun.
• **Example of Adjective**: 'Which book is yours?' ('Which' modifies 'book').
• **Example of Pronoun**: 'Which is your book?' ('Which' stands alone as the subject).
• **'Whom' in Formal English**: While 'Who' is often used colloquially for objects, 'Whom' is the grammatically correct form for objects in formal writing.
• **'Whom' Example**: 'Whom did you meet at the park?'
• **'Where' and Prepositions**: 'Where' questions often elicit answers containing prepositions of place (in, on, at, under).
• **'When' and Time Expressions**: 'When' questions often elicit answers containing time expressions (at 5 o'clock, tomorrow, next week).
• **'How' and Adverbs of Manner**: 'How' questions often elicit answers containing adverbs of manner (slowly, carefully, happily).
• **Questioning Tone**: The structure of the sentence itself acts as a marker for the listener to provide information.
• **Direct Questions**: These are questions that ask for information directly, ending with a question mark.

• **Indirect Questions**: These are questions embedded within a statement, often used for politeness.
• **Indirect Structure**: 'Could you tell me where the library is?' (Note: The word order is subject-verb, not verb-subject).
• **Politeness Marker**: Indirect questions are considered more formal and polite than direct questions.
• **'What' for Definition**: 'What' is frequently used to ask for definitions or explanations of terms.
• **'What' Example**: 'What is photosynthesis?'
• **'Where' for Direction**: 'Where' can also be used to ask about the destination of a movement.
• **'Where' Example**: 'Where are you going?'
• **'When' for Occasions**: 'When' can be used to ask about the timing of specific events or occurrences.
• **'When' Example**: 'When is your birthday?'
• **'Why' for Motivation**: 'Why' is used to explore the intent or motivation behind an action.
• **'Why' Example**: 'Why did you choose this subject?'
• **'How' for Degree**: 'How' can be used to ask about the extent or degree of something.
• **'How' Example**: 'How much do you like this game?'
• **Interrogative Determiners**: 'Which', 'What', and 'Whose' can function as determiners when they precede a noun.
• **Determiner Function**: They specify or limit the noun they precede.
• **Example of Determiner**: 'What time is it?' ('What' determines 'time').
• **Distinction from Pronouns**: When they stand alone, they are pronouns; when they modify a noun, they are determiners.
• **Consistency in Usage**: Using the correct interrogative ensures that the listener understands exactly what information is being requested.
• **Common Mistakes**: Using 'Who' instead of 'Whom' in formal contexts is a frequent grammatical error.
• **Correction Strategy**: If you can answer with 'him', use 'whom'; if you can answer with 'he', use 'who'.

• **The Role of 'Does'**: 'Does' is used for the singular third person (he, she, it).
• **The Role of 'Did'**: 'Did' is used for all persons in the past tense.
• **'Do' in Questions**: 'Do you like ice cream?'
• **'Does' in Questions**: 'Does he play cricket?'

• **'Had' in Questions**: 'Had they left before you arrived?'
• **'Can' in Questions**: 'Can you swim?' (Ability).

• **'Shall' in Questions**: 'Shall we dance?' (Suggestion).

• **'Must' in Questions**: 'Must I do this?' (Obligation).
• **'May' in Questions**: 'May I come in?' (Permission).

• **Interrogatives in Narrative**: Authors use interrogatives to build suspense or reveal information gradually.
• **Interrogatives in Dialogue**: They make conversations realistic and dynamic.
• **'Wh-' Word Order**: Always maintain the standard order: [Wh-word] + [Auxiliary] + [Subject] + [Main Verb].
• **Exception to Order**: If the 'Wh-' word is the subject, the order is [Wh-word] + [Verb] + [Object].
• **Example of Subject-Wh**: 'Who broke the vase?' (No auxiliary needed).
• **Example of Object-Wh**: 'Whom did you see?' (Auxiliary 'did' is required).
• **Avoiding Redundancy**: Do not use both a subject and an interrogative pronoun if the interrogative is the subject.
• **Incorrect**: 'Who he is?' (Correct: 'Who is he?').
• **Correcting Word Order**: Ensure the auxiliary verb precedes the subject in standard 'Wh-' questions.
• **'What' vs 'Which' in Selection**: 'Which' is used when the options are known to both speaker and listener.
• **'What' in General**: 'What' is used when the options are unknown or infinite.
• **'How' vs 'Why'**: 'How' focuses on the process; 'Why' focuses on the motivation.
• **'Where' vs 'When'**: 'Where' focuses on space; 'When' focuses on time.
• **'Whose' vs 'Who's'**: Always check if the word is a possessive pronoun or a contraction.
• **'Whom' vs 'Who'**: Use 'Who' for subjects and 'Whom' for objects.
• **Punctuation Precision**: Never omit the question mark at the end of an interrogative sentence.
• **Capitalization Rule**: Always capitalize the first letter of the interrogative word.
• **Sentence Variety**: Using different interrogatives makes writing more engaging and informative.
• **Clarity Check**: Read the question aloud to ensure it sounds natural and correctly addresses the intended information.
