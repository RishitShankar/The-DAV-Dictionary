• **Definition of a Sentence:** A sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense and expresses a complete thought.
• **Core Components:** Every complete sentence is fundamentally composed of two essential parts: the Subject and the Predicate.
• **The Subject:** The Subject is the part of the sentence that names the person, animal, place, or thing we are talking about.
• **The Predicate:** The Predicate is the part of the sentence that tells us something about the Subject.
• **Structural Rule:** A sentence is incomplete if it lacks either a Subject or a Predicate.
• **Identifying the Subject:** To find the subject, ask the question "Who?" or "What?" before the verb in the sentence.
• **Identifying the Predicate:** The predicate always contains the verb or the action word of the sentence.
• **Example Analysis:** In the sentence "The dog barks," "The dog" is the Subject, and "barks" is the Predicate.
• **Subject Placement:** In most standard English sentences, the Subject usually comes before the Predicate.
• **Predicate Composition:** The predicate includes the verb and all the words that follow it to complete the meaning.
• **Grammatical Agreement:** The subject and the verb in the predicate must agree in number (singular or plural).
• **Simple Subjects:** A simple subject is the main noun or pronoun that the sentence is about.
• **Complete Subjects:** A complete subject includes the simple subject plus all the modifiers or adjectives attached to it.
• **Simple Predicates:** A simple predicate is the main verb or verb phrase of the sentence.
• **Complete Predicates:** A complete predicate includes the verb and all the words that describe the action or state of being.
• **Sentence Unity:** The subject and predicate work together to create a logical unit of communication.
• **Basic Structure:** Subject + Predicate = Complete Sentence.

• **Nouns as Subjects:** A noun (a naming word) is the most common type of word used as a subject.
• **Pronouns as Subjects:** Pronouns like I, you, he, she, it, we, and they can also function as the subject of a sentence.
• **Proper Nouns:** Specific names of people or places, such as "Rahul" or "Delhi," frequently serve as subjects.
• **Common Nouns:** General names like "teacher," "city," or "book" act as subjects when they perform an action.
• **Subject-Verb Relationship:** The subject is the "doer" of the action in an action-oriented sentence.
• **State of Being:** In sentences without an action verb, the subject is the person or thing being described.
• **Example of State:** In "The sky is blue," "The sky" is the subject being described by the predicate "is blue."
• **The Role of the Verb:** The verb is the heart of the predicate and determines the tense of the sentence.
• **Action Verbs:** These verbs describe physical or mental actions performed by the subject (e.g., jump, think, eat).
• **Linking Verbs:** These verbs connect the subject to a description (e.g., is, am, are, was, were).
• **Subject Identification:** Always look for the person or thing performing the action to locate the subject.
• **Predicate Identification:** Always look for what is happening or what is being said about the subject to locate the predicate.
• **Sentence Complexity:** Even in long sentences, the division between subject and predicate remains constant.
• **Subject Expansion:** Adjectives can be added to the subject to make it more descriptive without changing its role.
• **Predicate Expansion:** Adverbs can be added to the predicate to describe how, when, or where the action happens.
• **Clarity in Writing:** Understanding these two parts helps in constructing clear and grammatically correct sentences.
• **Grammar Foundation:** Mastering subject-predicate division is the first step toward advanced sentence diagramming.

• **Subject-Predicate Split:** Every sentence can be split into two distinct parts at the point where the verb begins.
• **The "Who" Test:** If you are unsure about the subject, ask "Who is doing the action?" to isolate the subject.
• **The "What" Test:** If the subject is an object, ask "What is the sentence about?" to identify the subject.
• **The "What happened" Test:** To find the predicate, ask "What is being said about the subject?"
• **Subject Position:** While usually at the start, the subject can sometimes appear after the predicate in specific sentence structures.
• **Inverted Sentences:** In some poetic or emphatic sentences, the predicate may precede the subject.
• **Example of Inversion:** "Down the hill ran the boy." Here, "the boy" is the subject, and "Down the hill ran" is the predicate.
• **Standard Order:** For Class 3 level, focus primarily on the Subject-first, Predicate-second structure.
• **Subject-Predicate Balance:** A sentence must have a balance between the actor (subject) and the action (predicate).
• **Noun Phrases:** A subject can be a single word or a group of words known as a noun phrase.
• **Verb Phrases:** A predicate can be a single word or a group of words known as a verb phrase.
• **Subjective Case:** Pronouns used as subjects must be in the subjective case (e.g., "He" instead of "Him").
• **Predicate Agreement:** The predicate must change its verb form if the subject changes from singular to plural.
• **Singular Subject:** A singular subject requires a singular verb in the predicate (e.g., "The cat sleeps").
• **Plural Subject:** A plural subject requires a plural verb in the predicate (e.g., "The cats sleep").
• **Visualizing the Split:** Imagine a vertical line separating the subject from the rest of the sentence.
• **Practice Strategy:** Use a pencil to draw a line between the subject and the predicate to visualize the structure.
