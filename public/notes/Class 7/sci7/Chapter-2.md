• **Chapter Overview**: Welcome to The DAV Dictionary academic study notes for Class 7 Science, Chapter 2, titled "Nutrition in Living Organisms—Animals and Man", designed for comprehensive academic mastery.
• **Definition of Nutrition**: Nutrition is defined as a complex biological process that involves the intake of food by an organism and its subsequent utilization by the body for various vital activities.
• **Mode of Nutrition in Animals**: Unlike plants which are autotrophic, all animals exhibit a **heterotrophic mode of nutrition** as they cannot synthesize their own food and depend directly or indirectly on plants.
• **Holozoic Nutrition**: The vast majority of animals, including human beings, exhibit **holozoic nutrition**, which involves the ingestion of solid or liquid organic food followed by internal digestion, absorption, and assimilation.
• **Classification of Animals Based on Food Habits**: Animals are fundamentally categorized into herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, and parasites based on their specific dietary preferences and food sources.
• **Herbivores**: Animals such as cows, goats, horses, and rabbits that feed exclusively on plants, leaves, grass, and plant products are scientifically termed **herbivores**.
• **Carnivores**: Animals such as lions, tigers, wolves, and snakes that feed exclusively on the flesh and body parts of other animals are scientifically classified as **carnivores**.
• **Omnivores**: Animals such as human beings, bears, crows, and pigs that consume both plant matter and animal flesh are categorized as **omnivores**.
• **Scavengers and Decomposers**: Specialized organisms like vultures and hyenas act as scavengers feeding on dead remains, while microorganisms act as decomposers, completing the ecological nutrient cycle.
• **Significance of Food**: Food is fundamentally essential for living organisms because it provides energy for metabolic activities, raw materials for growth, and substances for repairing worn-out tissues.
• **Components of Food**: The food we consume contains major nutrients namely carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, roughage, and water, each playing a distinct physiological role.
• **Macronutrients vs Micronutrients**: Carbohydrates, proteins, and fats are required in large quantities as macronutrients, whereas vitamins and minerals are required in trace amounts as micronutrients.
• **Need for Digestion**: Complex food substances like starch, large proteins, and complex fats cannot be utilized directly by body cells and must be broken down into simpler, soluble forms through **digestion**.
• **Definition of Digestion**: Digestion is the physiological and biochemical process of breaking down complex, insoluble food molecules into simple, diffusible, and assimilable molecules.
• **Digestive System Organization**: In multicellular animals, especially humans, nutrition is carried out through an elaborate and highly specialized anatomical structure known as the **alimentary canal** along with associated glands.

• **Overview of the Human Digestive System**: The human digestive system comprises a continuous muscular tube called the **alimentary canal** (or digestive tract) and several vital digestive glands.
• **Path of the Alimentary Canal**: The human alimentary canal extends from the mouth to the anus, traversing the buccal cavity, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine.
• **Associated Digestive Glands**: The primary glands associated with the human digestive tract that secrete digestive enzymes include the **salivary glands**, the **liver**, and the **pancreas**.
• **The Ingestion Process**: The process of taking food into the body through the mouth is known as **ingestion**, marking the very first step of human digestion.
• **Anatomy of the Buccal Cavity**: The buccal cavity, or oral cavity, houses the teeth, tongue, and openings of the salivary glands, serving as the physical processing chamber for food.
• **Role of Teeth**: Teeth are hard, calcified structures embedded in the jaw sockets that mechanically break down large food particles into smaller pieces through cutting, tearing, and grinding.
• **Types of Teeth in Humans**: Humans develop two sets of teeth during their lifetime: temporary milk teeth during infancy followed by permanent teeth in adulthood.
• **Four Types of Permanent Teeth**: The permanent human dentition is specialized into four distinct types based on shape and function: **incisors, canines, premolars, and molars**.
• **Incisors**: Located at the front of the jaws, the chisel-shaped **incisors** are specially adapted for cutting, biting, and slicing food items.
• **Canines**: Flanking the incisors are the sharp, pointed **canines**, which are exceptionally well-developed in carnivores for piercing and tearing flesh.
• **Premolars**: Situated behind the canines, the broad-surfaced **premolars** assist in crushing, grinding, and chewing food thoroughly.
• **Molars**: Located at the rearmost part of the jaw, the large **molars** possess multiple cusps designed for fine grinding and pulverizing food before swallowing.
• **Dental Formula Concept**: The arrangement of teeth in humans is represented systematically by a dental formula that indicates the number of each tooth type in half of the upper and lower jaws.
• **Tooth Decay and Dental Caries**: Poor oral hygiene leads to the accumulation of bacteria that ferment food sugars, producing acids that erode tooth enamel, causing painful cavities known as **dental caries**.
• **Prevention of Dental Caries**: Regular brushing after meals, flossing, avoiding excessive sugary treats, and routine dental check-ups prevent plaque buildup and tooth decay.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Role of the Tongue**: The tongue is a muscular, sensory organ attached to the floor of the buccal cavity, vital for tasting, chewing, swallowing, and speaking.
• **Taste Buds**: The upper surface of the tongue is covered with numerous microscopic elevations called **papillae**, which house specialized chemical receptors known as **taste buds**.
• **Regions of Taste**: Different areas of the human tongue are sensitive to specific primary tastes, namely sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami.
• **Role of Tongue in Swallowing**: During chewing, the tongue thoroughly mixes chewed food with saliva and rolls it into a slippery mass called a **bolus**, pushing it backward into the pharynx for swallowing.
• **Salivary Glands**: Humans possess three pairs of major **salivary glands**—parotid, submandibular, and sublingual—which continuously pour watery saliva into the buccal cavity.
• **Composition of Saliva**: Saliva is a watery, slightly acidic fluid composed of 99% water, mineral ions, mucus, and a crucial digestive enzyme called **salivary amylase** (or ptyalin).
• **Action of Salivary Amylase**: Salivary amylase initiates the chemical digestion of carbohydrates in the mouth by converting complex **starch into simple sugars** (maltose).
• **Partial Digestion in Mouth**: Because food stays in the mouth for a very short duration, carbohydrate digestion is only partial at this stage, leaving proteins and fats untouched.
• **The Pharynx and Oesophagus**: After swallowing, the food bolus passes through the pharynx into a long, muscular, collapsible tube called the **oesophagus** (food pipe).
• **Peristalsis**: The movement of food down the oesophagus does not rely on gravity alone; instead, rhythmic, wave-like muscular contractions and relaxations of the gut wall known as **peristalsis** push the food forward.
• **Structure of the Stomach**: The stomach is a thick-walled, J-shaped muscular bag located in the upper left abdomen, acting as a temporary food storage and primary mixing chamber.
• **Gastric Glands**: The inner mucosal lining of the stomach contains millions of microscopic **gastric glands** that secrete **gastric juice**.
• **Components of Gastric Juice**: Gastric juice is a potent digestive fluid containing **hydrochloric acid (HCl)**, digestive enzymes like **pepsin**, and protective **mucus**.
• **Role of Hydrochloric Acid**: HCl creates an acidic medium required for pepsin activation, kills ingested harmful bacteria, and helps break down tough connective tissues in food.
• **Role of Mucus in Stomach**: The thick layer of **mucus** secreted by the stomach lining protects the delicate gastric walls from the corrosive action of its own hydrochloric acid and pepsin.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Protein Digestion in the Stomach**: The gastric enzyme **pepsin** chemically digests complex proteins into simpler polypeptides in the acidic environment of the stomach.
• **Chyme Formation**: Through vigorous churning and muscular contractions of the stomach walls, food is thoroughly mixed with gastric juices to form a semi-liquid, acidic paste called **chyme**.
• **Pyloric Sphincter**: The exit of the stomach is guarded by a muscular valve called the **pyloric sphincter**, which regulates the controlled release of small amounts of chyme into the small intestine.
• **Introduction to the Small Intestine**: The **small intestine** is the longest part of the alimentary canal, measuring about 6 to 7.5 meters in adults, and is coiled compactly within the abdominal cavity.
• **Regions of the Small Intestine**: Anatomically, the small intestine is subdivided into three consecutive regions: the **duodenum**, the **jejunum**, and the **ileum**.
• **Role of the Duodenum**: The first part of the small intestine, the duodenum, receives secretions from two major accessory glands: the **liver** and the **pancreas**.
• **The Liver (Anatomy & Role)**: The liver is the largest internal gland in the human body, reddish-brown in color, located in the upper right side of the abdomen just below the diaphragm.
• **Bile Secretion**: The liver continuously secretes an alkaline, greenish-yellow fluid called **bile**, which is temporarily stored in a small pear-shaped sac called the **gall bladder**.
• **Emulsification of Fats**: Bile does not contain any digestive enzymes; instead, it acts on large fat droplets, breaking them down into microscopic fat droplets in a process called **emulsification**, greatly increasing the surface area for enzyme action.
• **The Pancreas (Anatomy & Role)**: The pancreas is a soft, cream-colored, leaf-like gland located just below the stomach that secretes **pancreatic juice** into the duodenum via the pancreatic duct.
• **Pancreatic Enzymes**: Pancreatic juice contains powerful enzymes including **pancreatic amylase** (for carbohydrates), **trypsin** (for proteins), and **lipase** (for emulsified fats).
• **Intestinal Juice (Succus Entericus)**: The walls of the small intestine secrete an alkaline fluid called **intestinal juice**, which completes the final phase of chemical digestion.
• **Final Breakdown of Carbohydrates**: Carbohydrates are completely broken down into the simplest monosaccharide sugar, **glucose**, by the collective action of intestinal and pancreatic enzymes.
• **Final Breakdown of Proteins**: Proteins are fully digested and broken down into their fundamental building blocks, **amino acids**, by trypsin and peptidases.
• **Final Breakdown of Fats**: Emulsified fats are completely broken down into **fatty acids and glycerol** through the action of intestinal and pancreatic lipases.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Completion of Digestion**: With the action of intestinal juices in the ileum, the digestion of all major food components is successfully completed, yielding simple, soluble nutrients.
• **Definition of Absorption**: Absorption is the physiological process by which digested, simple nutrients pass through the walls of the small intestine and enter the blood and lymph circulation.
• **Adaptations of Small Intestine for Absorption**: The inner lining of the small intestine is specially adapted for maximum absorption by possessing an immensely large surface area.
• **Villi (Villus Structure)**: The inner wall of the small intestine is folded into numerous finger-like projections called **villi** (singular: **villus**), which tremendously increase the absorptive surface area.
• **Microvilli**: The epithelial cells covering each villus possess microscopic brush-border projections called **microvilli**, further multiplying the surface area available for nutrient uptake.
• **Blood Capillaries in Villi**: Each villus is richly supplied with a dense network of microscopic blood vessels called **blood capillaries** and a central lymph vessel called a **lacteal**.
• **Absorption of Glucose and Amino Acids**: Water-soluble nutrients like simple sugars (glucose), amino acids, vitamins, and minerals are absorbed directly into the **blood capillaries** inside the villi.
• **Absorption of Fatty Acids and Glycerol**: Insoluble fatty acids and glycerol, being unable to enter blood capillaries directly, are first reassembled and absorbed into the **lacteals** (lymph vessels).
• **Definition of Assimilation**: The process by which absorbed nutrients are transported via the blood to all body cells and utilized for energy production, growth, and tissue repair is called **assimilation**.
• **Utilization of Glucose**: Inside body cells, absorbed glucose is oxidized during cellular respiration to release chemical energy, while excess glucose is stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles.
• **Utilization of Amino Acids**: Absorbed amino acids are utilized by cells to synthesize new structural proteins required for growth, cellular repair, enzyme production, and hormone synthesis.
• **Utilization of Fatty Acids and Fats**: Fatty acids and glycerol are utilized to build cellular membranes and are stored in adipose tissue beneath the skin as a reserve energy source and thermal insulator.
• **Definition of Egestion (Defaecation)**: The process by which undigested and unabsorbed waste food material is expelled from the body through the anus is termed **egestion** or **defaecation**.
• **Transition to the Large Intestine**: Whatever food remains undigested and unabsorbed passes from the small intestine into the wider, shorter tube known as the **large intestine**.
• **Regions of the Large Intestine**: The large intestine consists anatomically of the **caecum**, the **colon** (ascending, transverse, descending), and the **rectum**.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Function of the Large Intestine**: The primary function of the large intestine is not digestion, but the **absorption of water and essential mineral salts** from the undigested waste residue.
• **Formation of Faeces**: As water is continuously absorbed along the colon, the remaining semi-solid waste residue becomes solid and is known as **faeces**.
• **Role of the Rectum**: Solid faecal matter is temporarily stored in the terminal muscular sac of the large intestine known as the **rectum**.
• **The Anus and Defaecation**: Periodically, the faeces are eliminated from the body through the muscular opening at the end of the alimentary canal called the **anus**, controlled by anal sphincters.
• **Gut Microbiome / Symbiotic Bacteria**: The human large intestine houses trillions of harmless, beneficial bacteria (such as *Escherichia coli*) that synthesize certain vitamins (like Vitamin K) in exchange for shelter and nutrients.
• **Introduction to Herbivore Digestion**: Herbivorous animals have specialized digestive tracts because plant-based food is rich in a complex carbohydrate called **cellulose**, which is very difficult to digest.
• **Ruminants (Cud-Chewing Animals)**: Plant-eating mammals such as cows, sheep, buffaloes, goats, and deer are known as **ruminants** because they quickly swallow plant matter and later bring it back to the mouth to chew it thoroughly.
• **The Ruminant Stomach**: The stomach of a ruminant is uniquely divided into four distinct chambers: the **rumen**, the **reticulum**, the **omasum**, and the **abomasum**.
• **Role of the Rumen**: The rumen is the largest first chamber of a ruminant's stomach where plant food is stored and partially digested by the action of specialized bacteria and protozoa that break down **cellulose**.
• **Formation of the Cud**: In the rumen, food is partially broken down into a semi-liquid state known as **cud**, which is then sent back up to the mouth in small batches for thorough re-chewing.
• **Rumination**: The process of bringing swallowed food back up into the mouth to chew it a second time for better mechanical breakdown is called **rumination**.
• **Passage Through Omasum and Abomasum**: After re-chewing, the food bypasses the rumen and reticulum, passes through the omasum for water absorption, and finally enters the **abomasum** (true stomach) for enzymatic digestion.
• **Why Humans Cannot Digest Cellulose**: Human beings, unlike ruminants, lack the specialized cellulose-digesting bacteria and multi-chambered stomachs required to break down plant cellulose, though it acts as vital dietary fiber.
• **Roughage (Dietary Fiber)**: Plant cell walls rich in cellulose provide **roughage**, which does not yield nutrients but adds bulk to food, stimulates peristalsis, and prevents constipation in humans.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Introduction to Amoeba Nutrition**: Unicellular microscopic organisms like **Amoeba** possess a very simple, yet efficient mechanism of holozoic nutrition without having any specialized digestive organs.
• **Structure of Amoeba**: Amoeba is a single-celled freshwater protozoan that constantly changes its shape by extending temporary, finger-like cytoplasmic projections called **pseudopodia** (false feet).
• **Ingestion in Amoeba**: When an Amoeba detects a food particle (such as microscopic algae or bacteria) in its vicinity, it extends **pseudopodia** around the food particle to engulf it completely.
• **Formation of Food Vacuole**: Upon engulfment, the cell membrane fuses around the food particle, forming a temporary fluid-filled bubble inside the cytoplasm called a **food vacuole**.
• **Digestion in Amoeba**: Digestive enzymes secreted from the surrounding cytoplasm into the food vacuole break down complex food substances into simpler, soluble molecules.
• **Absorption in Amoeba**: The digested, soluble nutrients diffuse directly out of the food vacuole into the surrounding cytoplasm of the Amoeba cell.
• **Assimilation in Amoeba**: The absorbed nutrients are utilized by the single cell for growth, energy generation, and basic cellular metabolism, representing **assimilation**.
• **Egestion in Amoeba**: Unlike humans who have an anus, an Amoeba expels undigested waste by bringing its food vacuole to the cell membrane, which ruptures and discharges the waste outside the cell.
• **Introduction to Hydra Nutrition**: **Hydra** is a small, simple multicellular freshwater coelenterate that exhibits holozoic nutrition using specialized anatomical features.
• **Structure of Hydra**: Hydra possesses a tubular body surrounded by a ring of mobile, thread-like tentacles around its mouth, which it uses for capturing prey.
• **Tentacles and Nematocysts**: The tentacles of Hydra are armed with stinging cells called **nematocysts** that paralyze small aquatic prey such as water fleas (Daphnia) upon contact.
• **Ingestion in Hydra**: Using its tentacles, the paralyzed prey is pulled toward the oral opening and pushed into the central hollow digestive cavity known as the **coelenteron** (gastrovascular cavity).
• **Digestion in Hydra**: Gland cells lining the gastrovascular cavity secrete digestive enzymes that partially break down the food extracellularly within the cavity.
• **Phagocytosis in Hydra**: Specialized cells lining the cavity then engulf smaller food particles (intracellular digestion), ensuring complete breakdown of the prey.
• **Egestion in Hydra**: Since Hydra lacks a true anus, indigestible waste is expelled back out through the same oral opening by muscular contraction of the body wall.

• **Nutritional Deficiencies**: A lack of proper balance in nutrient intake leads to various deficiency disorders, highlighting the immense importance of a balanced diet containing all essential food components.
• **Kwashiorkor**: A severe protein deficiency disease common in toddlers characterized by retarded growth, swelling of the face and limbs, discolored hair, and a protruding belly.
• **Marasmus**: A condition caused by simultaneous deficiency of both proteins and total calories, leading to extreme emaciation, wasted muscles, wrinkled skin, and severe weakness in infants.
• **Vitamin A Deficiency**: Lack of Vitamin A in the diet leads to **night blindness** (inability to see in dim light) and dry, scaly skin.
• **Vitamin B Deficiency**: Deficiency of Vitamin B complex (such as Vitamin B1 or thiamine) causes **beriberi**, characterized by extreme muscle weakness and fatigue.
• **Vitamin C Deficiency**: Lack of Vitamin C causes **scurvy**, symptoms of which include bleeding gums, loose teeth, delayed wound healing, and joint pains.
• **Vitamin D Deficiency**: Vitamin D deficiency impairs calcium absorption, resulting in **rickets** in children, characterized by soft, weak, and deformed bow legs.
• **Calcium Deficiency**: Inadequate dietary calcium leads to weak bones and teeth, increased bone fracture risk, and a condition known as **osteoporosis** in adults.
• **Iron Deficiency**: Lack of iron in the diet reduces haemoglobin synthesis in red blood cells, causing **anaemia**, marked by chronic fatigue, weakness, and paleness.
• **Iodine Deficiency**: Insufficient dietary iodine leads to improper functioning of the thyroid gland, resulting in an enlargement of the gland in the neck known as **goitre** and mental retardation in children.
• **Balanced Diet Concept**: A **balanced diet** contains adequate amounts of all essential nutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals), roughage, and water in correct proportions to meet body requirements.
• **Factors Affecting Balanced Diet**: The exact composition of a balanced diet varies depending on an individual's age, sex, body weight, and physical activity level (e.g., laborers require more energy-rich food).
• **Water as a Nutrient**: Water constitutes about 65-70% of human body weight and is vital as a universal solvent, medium for metabolic reactions, and regulator of body temperature.
• **Dehydration**: Excessive loss of water and essential salts from the body due to vomiting or diarrhea leads to **dehydration**, which can be treated by administering Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS).
• **Role of Hygiene in Nutrition**: Maintaining strict personal and food hygiene—such as washing hands before eating, washing fruits and vegetables, and keeping food covered—prevents water-borne and food-borne infections.

• **Summary of Digestion Stages**: Human nutrition involves five sequential physiological steps: **ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, and egestion**.
• **Enzymes as Biological Catalysts**: Digestive enzymes are specialized protein molecules that speed up chemical reactions involved in breaking down complex food molecules without themselves being consumed.
• **Specificity of Enzymes**: Every digestive enzyme is highly specific in its action; for instance, salivary amylase acts only on starch, pepsin acts only on proteins, and lipase acts only on fats.
• **Optimum pH Requirements**: Different regions of the alimentary canal require specific pH conditions for optimal enzyme activity, such as strongly acidic in the stomach and alkaline in the small intestine.
• **Role of Sphincter Muscles**: Circular muscular rings called sphincters (such as cardiac sphincter and pyloric sphincter) act as biological valves that regulate the one-way flow of food through the alimentary canal.
• **Adaptation Across Species**: The structural complexity of an animal's digestive system directly reflects its dietary habits, ranging from simple intracellular vacuoles in unicellular organisms to complex multi-chambered stomachs in ruminants.
• **Interdependence in Nature**: Animals directly or indirectly depend on green plants for their nutritional needs, establishing the fundamental food chain and energy flow across global ecosystems.
• **Significance of Liver Functions**: Beyond bile production for fat emulsification, the liver acts as a vital metabolic clearinghouse that regulates blood sugar, stores vitamins, and detoxifies harmful substances.
• **Role of Blood Circulation**: The cardiovascular system plays an indispensable role in nutrition by transporting absorbed food molecules from the small intestine to every living cell in the body.
• **Cellular Respiration Link**: The ultimate fate of ingested food is its oxidation inside body cells during respiration to liberate ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate), the usable currency of cellular energy.
• **Health and Nutrition Interconnection**: Proper nutritional habits directly correlate with overall physical health, robust immune function, disease resistance, and mental well-being in humans.
• **Junk Food Warnings**: Excessive consumption of commercially processed junk foods rich in empty calories, trans fats, and refined sugars leads to obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.
• **Malnutrition Definition**: **Malnutrition** refers to a pathological state resulting from a deficiency, excess, or imbalance of nutrient intake, severely affecting normal growth and bodily functions.
• **Importance of Dietary Fiber**: Even though roughage cannot be digested by human enzymes, consuming adequate fiber from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains is essential for maintaining a healthy digestive tract.

• **Key Terms Review - Alimentary Canal**: The long, muscular, continuous tubular passage extending from the mouth to the anus through which food passes during digestion.
• **Key Terms Review - Ingestion**: The mechanical act of taking solid or liquid food into the body through the mouth cavity.
• **Key Terms Review - Digestion**: The enzymatic breakdown of complex, insoluble food molecules into simple, soluble, and diffusible molecules.
• **Key Terms Review - Absorption**: The passage of digested soluble nutrient molecules through the intestinal wall into the blood and lymph circulation.
• **Key Terms Review - Assimilation**: The biological incorporation and utilization of absorbed nutrients by body cells for energy, growth, and tissue repair.
• **Key Terms Review - Egestion**: The elimination of undigested, unabsorbed solid waste food material from the body via the anus.
• **Key Terms Review - Peristalsis**: The rhythmic, wave-like contractions and relaxations of the muscular walls of the alimentary canal that propel food forward.
• **Key Terms Review - Villi**: Tiny, finger-like projections lining the inner wall of the small intestine that immensely maximize nutrient absorption surface area.
• **Key Terms Review - Pseudopodia**: Temporary, cytoplasmic extensions formed by unicellular organisms like Amoeba used for locomotion and engulfing food particles.
• **Key Terms Review - Ruminants**: Specialized herbivorous mammals like cows and sheep that regurgitate swallowed plant food and re-chew it as cud.
• **Key Terms Review - Rumination**: The behavioral process of re-chewing regurgitated plant matter to mechanically break down tough cellulose fibers.
• **Key Terms Review - Emulsification**: The physical process of breaking large fat globules down into microscopic droplets by bile salts to enhance enzyme digestion.
• **Key Terms Review - Balanced Diet**: A diet containing all essential nutrient groups, roughage, and water in precise quantities required for optimal health.
• **Key Terms Review - Malnutrition**: A physiological state of ill-health caused by prolonged deficiency, excess, or imbalance of dietary nutrients.
