• **Chapter Overview**: Welcome to the DAV Dictionary Academic Notes for Class 5 My Living World, Chapter 2: "Plants," designed to provide exhaustive conceptual clarity.
• **Core Significance of Plants**: Plants are essential living organisms that form the foundation of terrestrial ecosystems and sustain life on Earth.
• **Primary Classification**: Plants are broadly classified based on their habitat, structure, reproduction methods, and life cycles.
• **Green Pigment Chlorophyll**: Most plants appear green due to the presence of chlorophyll, a specialized pigment essential for trapping solar energy.
• **Autotrophic Nature**: Plants are autotrophs, meaning they synthesize their own organic food using inorganic raw materials through photosynthesis.
• **Primary Plant Parts**: A typical flowering plant is divided into two main systems: the underground root system and the aerial shoot system.
• **The Root System**: The root system anchors the plant firmly in the soil and absorbs vital water and dissolved mineral nutrients.
• **The Shoot System**: The shoot system grows above the ground and includes stems, branches, leaves, flowers, buds, and fruits.
• **Diversity in Plant Life**: Plants range in size from microscopic floating aquatic duckweeds to massive towering trees like the giant sequoia.
• **Ecological Producers**: In any ecological food chain, plants occupy the base as primary producers, converting solar energy into chemical energy.
• **Oxygen Providers**: Through the process of photosynthesis, plants release oxygen gas into the atmosphere, which is essential for aerobic respiration in animals.
• **Carbon Dioxide Regulation**: Plants help regulate atmospheric carbon dioxide levels by absorbing this greenhouse gas during food production.
• **Soil Conservation**: Plant roots bind soil particles together tightly, preventing soil erosion caused by heavy winds and flowing water.
• **Human Dependency**: Human beings depend entirely on plants for food, clothing fibers, medicines, timber, paper, and industrial raw materials.
• **Scope of Chapter 2**: This chapter deeply explores the internal and external structures of plants, focusing heavily on leaves, food making, and seed germination.

• **Definition of Roots**: Roots are the underground vegetative organs of a vascular plant that grow downward into the soil in response to gravity (geotropism).
• **Primary Function of Anchorage**: The primary mechanical function of roots is to firmly anchor the heavy plant body deep within the soil substrate.
• **Absorption of Water**: Roots possess specialized absorptive surfaces that take up capillary water from the soil spaces via osmotic pressure.
• **Mineral Uptake**: Dissolved mineral ions such as nitrates, phosphates, and potassium are absorbed by the roots along with water.
• **Taproot System Definition**: A taproot system features one thick, primary main root that grows straight down from the radicle, with smaller lateral roots branching off.
• **Examples of Taproots**: Common plants possessing taproot systems include beans, peas, carrots, radishes, turnips, and mustard plants.
• **Fibrous Root System Definition**: A fibrous root system consists of a dense cluster of thin, moderately branching roots arising from the base of the stem.
• **Absence of Main Root**: In fibrous roots, there is no single dominant primary root; all roots are roughly equal in size and thickness.
• **Examples of Fibrous Roots**: Grasses, wheat, rice, maize, onions, and sugarcane are classic examples of plants with fibrous root systems.
• **Root Modification for Storage**: Certain plants modify their roots to store large quantities of reserve food material to survive unfavorable environmental conditions.
• **Examples of Storage Roots**: Carrot, radish, beet, and sweet potato are swollen roots packed with stored starch and nutrients.
• **Specialized Aerial Roots**: Some plants develop specialized aerial roots that hang in the air or penetrate deep into swampy mud for gaseous exchange.
• **Root Hairs**: Minute, delicate, hair-like outgrowths of epidermal cells on roots increase the surface area for water and mineral absorption exponentially.
• **Transport Mechanism**: Absorbed water and minerals move upward from the roots into the stem via specialized vascular tissues called xylem.
• **Soil Aeration Requirement**: Roots require oxygen trapped in soil spaces for cellular respiration; waterlogged soil suffocates roots and kills plants.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Definition of Stem**: The stem is the primary vertical aerial axis of a vascular plant that supports leaves, flowers, and fruits, connecting roots to leaves.
• **Node and Internode Structure**: Stems are characterized by nodes (points where leaves attach) and internodes (the regions of the stem between successive nodes).
• **Buds on Stems**: Terminal buds at the tip of the stem facilitate upward growth, while lateral axillary buds give rise to branches or flowers.
• **Vascular Transport Role**: The stem acts as a two-way highway containing xylem (transporting water/minerals upward) and phloem (transporting food downward).
• **Structural Support**: Stems provide mechanical rigidity, holding leaves up toward sunlight to maximize photosynthetic efficiency.
• **Herbaceous Stems**: Herbaceous stems are soft, green, flexible, and non-woody, characteristic of seasonal plants like mint, spinach, and grass.
• **Woody Stems (Trunks)**: Woody stems are hard, rigid, brown, and covered in thick bark, characteristic of perennial trees like mango, neem, and oak.
• **Stem Modifications for Storage**: Certain underground or modified stems store abundant food reserves to tide over dry or cold seasons.
• **Potato as a Stem**: A potato is not a root; it is a modified underground stem tuber containing dormant buds known as "eyes."
• **Ginger and Onion Modifications**: Ginger is a fleshy underground stem called a rhizome, while onions possess compressed underground stem bases surrounded by fleshy scale leaves.
• **Stems Modifying for Support**: Weak-stemmed climbing plants develop specialized spiral structures called tendrils to climb up supports.
• **Photosynthetic Stems**: In certain desert plants with reduced leaves, such as cacti, the green stem takes over the function of photosynthesis.
• **Water Storage in Stems**: Succulent stems of desert cacti store large volumes of water within their spongy tissues to survive prolonged droughts.
• **Conduction Speed**: The rate of sap conduction through stems is regulated by transpirational pull generated by the leaves above.
• **Economic Value of Stems**: Stems provide us with timber for construction, bamboo for scaffolding, sugarcane juice, and medicinal extracts like cinnamon bark.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **The Leaf as Kitchen**: Leaves are universally recognized as the food factories or kitchens of the plant kingdom due to their role in photosynthesis.
• **General Anatomy of a Leaf**: A typical leaf consists of a flat, broad blade called the lamina and a stalk called the petiole that attaches it to the stem.
• **Leaf Base and Stipules**: The base of the petiole connects to the stem node, sometimes accompanied by small, leaf-like appendages called stipules.
• **Midrib and Veins**: Running through the center of the lamina is a prominent midrib, branching out into a fine network of veins that transport materials.
• **Leaf Venation Definition**: The arrangement of veins and veinlets on the lamina of a leaf is termed venation.
• **Reticulate Venation**: A net-like arrangement of veins spreading across the lamina, characteristic of dicotyledonous plants like hibiscus, rose, and mango.
• **Parallel Venation**: An arrangement where veins run parallel to each other from the leaf base to the apex, typical of monocot grasses, wheat, and banana.
• **Stomata Definition**: Microscopic pore structures found predominantly on the lower epidermis of leaves, responsible for gas exchange.
• **Guard Cells**: Each stoma is flanked by two specialized kidney-shaped guard cells that regulate the opening and closing of the pore.
• **Gaseous Exchange via Stomata**: Carbon dioxide enters leaves through stomata for photosynthesis, and oxygen and water vapor exit through them.
• **Transpiration Process**: The continuous loss of water vapor from aerial plant parts, mainly through leaf stomata, is called transpiration.
• **Cooling Effect of Transpiration**: Transpiration creates a cooling effect for the plant and generates suction pull to draw water up from roots.
• **Pigment Distribution**: Chlorophyll pigments are housed inside specialized organelles called chloroplasts located within the mesophyll cells of the leaf.
• **Leaf Fall (Auscultation)**: Deciduous trees shed all their leaves simultaneously during autumn to conserve water and energy during harsh winters.
• **Economic Importance of Leaves**: Leaves provide human beings with medicines (neem, tulsi), beverages (tea), spices (curry leaves, mint), and animal fodder.

• **Photosynthesis Etymology**: The term "photosynthesis" derives from Greek words: "photo" meaning light, and "synthesis" meaning putting together.
• **Definition of Photosynthesis**: The physicochemical process by which green plants synthesize organic glucose from carbon dioxide and water using solar energy.
• **Essential Ingredients Required**: Photosynthesis requires four vital raw materials: carbon dioxide, water, sunlight, and chlorophyll pigment.
• **Source of Carbon Dioxide**: Plants absorb carbon dioxide gas directly from the surrounding atmosphere through their leaf stomata.
• **Source of Water**: Water is absorbed from the soil by roots and transported upward through xylem vessels into the photosynthetic leaf cells.
• **Role of Sunlight**: Sunlight provides the pure radiant energy required to split water molecules and drive chemical bond formation in glucose.
• **Role of Chlorophyll**: Chlorophyll acts as a biological catalyst that traps and absorbs photon energy from sunlight efficiently.
• **Chemical Equation**: Carbon Dioxide + Water $\xrightarrow{\text{Sunlight & Chlorophyll}}$ Glucose (Food) + Oxygen Gas.
• **Primary Product Synthesized**: The immediate organic food produced during photosynthesis is simple sugar (glucose), which feeds the plant.
• **Byproduct Released**: Oxygen gas is released as a valuable byproduct of photosynthesis, exiting the plant via stomata into the atmosphere.
• **Storage Form of Food**: Plants convert excess manufactured glucose into complex insoluble carbohydrates called starch for safe storage in leaves, stems, or roots.
• **Global Significance**: Photosynthesis is arguably the most critical biological process on Earth, supporting virtually all life forms directly or indirectly.
• **Energy Flow Foundation**: It bridges the gap between solar energy and biological life, acting as the primary entry point of energy into food webs.
• **Starch Test Demonstration**: Iodine solution can be used in a laboratory setting to test for the presence of starch in leaves, turning blue-black if positive.
• **De-starch Procedure**: Before conducting a starch test, a potted plant is kept in a dark room for 48 hours to exhaust all stored leaf starch reserves.

• **Interdependence in Nature**: Plants and animals maintain a delicate balance in nature through a continuous, interdependent exchange of gases.
• **Animal Dependency on Plants**: Animals consume plants (or plant-eating herbivores) for organic nutrition and inhale oxygen released by plants for respiration.
• **Plant Dependency on Animals**: Plants utilize carbon dioxide exhaled by animals and humans during respiratory processes for conducting photosynthesis.
• **Balanced Atmospheric Composition**: This continuous reciprocal exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide maintains stable atmospheric proportions essential for survival.
• **Carbon Cycle Role**: Plants act as primary sinks in the global carbon cycle, sequestering vast amounts of atmospheric carbon into biomass.
• **Oxygen Cycle Maintenance**: Marine phytoplankton and terrestrial green forests together generate almost all the breathable oxygen found in the biosphere.
• **Deforestation Impacts**: Large-scale indiscriminate felling of trees disrupts this natural gas equilibrium, leading to elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide levels.
• **Global Warming Consequences**: Excessive carbon dioxide traps solar heat within the atmosphere, driving global climate change, glacial melt, and severe weather anomalies.
• **Afforestation Campaigns**: Planting new trees (afforestation) is critically promoted worldwide to restore ecological equilibrium and sequester carbon effectively.
• **Urban Green Spaces**: Parks, botanical gardens, and roadside trees act as green lungs in polluted metropolitan cities, filtering particulates and producing oxygen.
• **Plant Adaptations for Survival**: Plants modify their physical structures to survive extreme climatic pressures in deserts, aquatic zones, and mountains.
• **Parasitic Plants**: Some plants, like Cuscuta (dodder), lack chlorophyll and steal ready-made food from host plants using specialized roots called haustoria.
• **Insectivorous Plants**: Pitcher plants and Venus flytraps grow in nitrogen-deficient soils and capture insects to supplement their nutritional requirements.
• **Saprophytic Plants**: Fungi and certain non-green plants feed on dead and decaying organic matter, recycling nutrients back into the forest soil.
• **Symbiotic Relationships**: Lichens exhibit a symbiotic association between algae (food producers) and fungi (water absorbers), displaying mutualistic survival.

• **Reproduction in Plants**: Plants propagate and multiply their numbers through two major modes of reproduction: asexual reproduction and sexual reproduction.
• **Asexual Reproduction Definition**: Reproduction that occurs without the fusion of gametes, involving only a single parent plant to produce genetically identical offspring.
• **Vegetative Propagation**: A form of asexual reproduction where new plants grow from vegetative plant parts such as stems, roots, or leaves without seeds.
• **Propagation via Stem Cuttings**: Gardeners cut pieces of stems from parent plants (like rose, hibiscus, money plant) and plant them in soil to grow new roots.
• **Propagation via Underground Stems**: Potato tubers sprout new shoots from their "eyes," giving rise to independent clone plants effortlessly.
• **Propagation via Runner Stems**: Creeping horizontal stems (runners or stolons) like grass and strawberries spread across the soil and root at nodes.
• **Propagation via Leaves**: Certain succulent plants like Bryophyllum produce tiny adventitious plantlets along the margins of their fleshy leaves.
• **Advantages of Vegetative Propagation**: Plants grow much faster, bear flowers and fruits earlier, and produce exact genetic copies of desirable parent plants.
• **Sexual Reproduction in Plants**: Reproduction involving the fusion of male and female reproductive cells (gametes) culminating in seed and fruit formation.
• **The Flower as Reproductive Organ**: The flower is the specialized reproductive shoot of a flowering plant (angiosperm) containing male and/or female organs.
• **Calyx and Corolla**: Non-essential floral whorls include the sepals (calyx, protecting the bud) and petals (corolla, attracting pollinators with bright colors).
• **Androecium (Stamen)**: The male reproductive organ of a flower, consisting of a slender stalk (filament) and a pollen-sac-bearing head (anther).
• **Pollen Grains**: Microscopic powdery granules produced inside anthers that contain the male gametes necessary for plant fertilization.
• **Gynoecium (Pistil/Carpel)**: The female reproductive organ consisting of three parts: the basal ovary, the elongated style, and the terminal stigma.
• **Ovules inside Ovary**: The swollen ovary contains tiny female ovules, which enclose the female egg cell waiting to be fertilized by pollen.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Pollination Definition**: The transfer of mature pollen grains from the anther of a stamen to the receptive stigma of a flower is known as pollination.
• **Self-Pollination**: The transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same individual plant.
• **Cross-Pollination**: The transfer of pollen grains from the anther of one flower to the stigma of a flower on another plant of the same species.
• **Agents of Pollination**: Since plants are rooted in place, they rely on external agents such as insects, birds, wind, and water to transfer pollen.
• **Entomophilous Flowers**: Flowers pollinated by insects are brightly colored, highly fragrant, and produce sticky pollen and sweet nectar to attract bees and butterflies.
• **Anemophilous Flowers**: Flowers pollinated by wind possess small, inconspicuous petals, produce massive quantities of lightweight pollen, and feature feathery stigmas.
• **Fertilization Process**: Following pollination, a pollen grain germinates on the stigma, growing a pollen tube down the style to reach the ovary.
• **Zygote Formation**: The male gamete travels down the pollen tube and fuses with the female egg cell inside the ovule, forming a single-celled zygote.
• **Post-Fertilization Changes**: After fertilization, petals, sepals, stamens, and style wither and fall off the flower as they are no longer required.
• **Transformation of Ovary**: The fertilized ovary undergoes extensive cell division and enlargement, ripening into a protective fruit.
• **Transformation of Ovules**: Inside the maturing fruit, the fertilized ovules transform into hard, dormant seeds containing embryonic plant life.
• **Seed Anatomy**: A standard seed consists of a tough protective seed coat (testa), stored food tissue (cotyledons), and a tiny plant embryo.
• **Radicle and Plumule**: The plant embryo inside a seed comprises the radicle (future root system) and the plumule (future shoot system).
• **Seed Dormancy**: Seeds remain in a state of suspended metabolic activity (dormancy) until environmental conditions become favorable for growth.
• **Importance of Dispersal**: Spreading seeds away from the parent plant prevents overcrowding, intense competition for light and water, and soil exhaustion.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Seed Dispersal Definition**: The natural mechanism by which seeds are transported away from the parent plant to new locations for germination.
• **Dispersal by Wind (Anemochory)**: Light, winged, or hairy seeds (like dandelion, cotton, maple, drumstick) are easily carried over long distances by air currents.
• **Dispersal by Water (Hydrochory)**: Plants growing near water bodies produce buoyant fruits and seeds (like coconut husk and lotus) that float and travel downstream.
• **Dispersal by Animals (Zoochory)**: Animals ingest fleshy fruits and excrete indigestible seeds far away, or hook-bearing seeds attach to animal fur.
• **Examples of Hooked Seeds**: Xanthium and spear grass possess barbed hooks that latch onto passing animal fur or human clothing for hitchhiking dispersal.
• **Dispersal by Explosion (Autochory)**: Certain seed pods (like balsam, pea, and touch-me-not) dry out under tension and burst open violently, scattering seeds.
• **Seed Germination Definition**: The gradual reactivation of metabolic processes in a dormant seed, leading to the emergence of a seedling.
• **Essential Factors for Germination**: Successful seed germination strictly requires three primary environmental factors: adequate water, appropriate warmth, and oxygen.
• **Role of Water in Germination**: Water softens the tough seed coat, activates dormant enzymes, and triggers biochemical reactions inside the cotyledons.
• **Role of Oxygen in Germination**: Oxygen is required by the rapidly dividing embryonic cells to carry out aerobic cellular respiration and release energy.
• **Role of Temperature in Germination**: Moderate warmth provides the optimal thermal activation energy required for enzyme activity during early seedling growth.
• **Steps in Germination**: First, the seed absorbs water and swells; second, the seed coat bursts open; third, the radicle emerges downward to anchor the plant.
• **Subsequent Shoot Growth**: Following root emergence, the plumule grows upward toward the light source, unfurling the first green embryonic leaves.
• **Transition to Autotrophy**: Once the initial stored food in cotyledons is exhausted, the seedling unfurls true leaves, starts photosynthesis, and becomes independent.
• **Agricultural Significance**: Understanding germination mechanics helps farmers optimize sowing depth, soil moisture, and crop spacing for maximum agricultural yields.
