• **Introduction to Geometry**: The word geometry originates from the Greek words 'geo' meaning earth and 'metron' meaning measurement, forming the foundational branch of mathematics that deals with shapes, sizes, relative positions of figures, and properties of space.
• **Basic Building Blocks**: All geometrical figures, shapes, designs, and complex structures are ultimately composed of three undefined fundamental terms: point, line, and plane.
• **Concept of a Point**: A point is an exact location in space that has no length, no breadth, no thickness, and no volume, possessing only position.
• **Representation of a Point**: A point is typically represented on paper by a sharp pencil mark and is denoted using capital English alphabet letters such as $A$, $B$, $P$, or $Q$.
• **Collinear Points**: Three or more points lying on the exact same straight line are defined as collinear points, sharing a linear relationship in a two-dimensional space.
• **Non-Collinear Points**: Points that do not lie on the same single straight line are referred to as non-collinear points, which are essential for forming multi-sided polygons.
• **Concept of a Line**: A line is a straight one-dimensional geometric figure that extends infinitely in both opposite directions without any endpoints.
• **Naming a Line**: A line can be designated either by a small cursive letter like $l$, $m$, or $n$, or by naming any two distinct points lying upon it, such as line $AB$ ($\overleftrightarrow{AB}$).
• **Line Segment Definition**: A line segment is a finite portion of a straight line that has two distinct endpoints and possesses a measurable, fixed length.
• **Representation of a Line Segment**: A line segment with endpoints $A$ and $B$ is symbolically represented as $\overline{AB}$, where the bar signifies its bounded nature.
• **Measurement of Line Segments**: Unlike an infinite line, a line segment can be accurately measured using a standard ruler, divider, or metric scale.
• **Ray Definition**: A ray is a portion of a line that originates from a single fixed starting point (endpoint) and extends infinitely in one specific direction.
• **Representation of a Ray**: A ray starting at origin $A$ and passing through point $B$ is denoted symbolically as $\vec{AB}$, where the arrow indicates the single direction of infinite extension.
• **Distinction Between Line, Segment, and Ray**: A line has zero endpoints and extends both ways; a line segment has two endpoints and is finite; a ray has one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction.
• **Intersecting Lines**: Two or more lines that cross or meet each other at a single common point are known as intersecting lines, with the meeting point called the point of intersection.
• **Concurrent Lines**: When three or more lines pass through the exact same single common point, they are specifically termed concurrent lines.
• **Parallel Lines**: Two lines in a plane that never meet or intersect, no matter how far they are extended, and maintain a constant perpendicular distance from each other, are parallel lines.
• **Symbol for Parallel Lines**: The geometric relationship of parallelism between line $l$ and line $m$ is mathematically written as $l \parallel m$.

• **Introduction to Planes**: A plane is a flat, two-dimensional surface that extends infinitely in all directions, containing an infinite number of lines and points.
• **Real-World Examples of Planes**: Smooth surfaces such as the top of a flat table, the surface of a calm pool of water, or a whiteboard serve as physical models of planes.
• **Naming a Plane**: A plane is usually named using a single capital letter such as Plane $P$ or by specifying three non-collinear points lying on it, like Plane $ABC$.
• **Dimension Concept**: In geometry, dimensions refer to the independent directions required to specify a point's location within a space.
• **Zero-Dimensional Figures**: A geometric point is classified as a zero-dimensional figure because it has length, width, and height equal to zero.
• **One-Dimensional Figures**: Lines, line segments, and rays are classified as one-dimensional figures because they possess only length and lack width or thickness.
• **Two-Dimensional Figures**: Planes and flat flat shapes like rectangles, squares, and circles are two-dimensional figures possessing both length and breadth.
• **Three-Dimensional Figures**: Solid shapes such as cubes, spheres, cylinders, and cones are three-dimensional figures because they possess length, breadth, and height/depth.
• **Half-Plane**: Any single straight line drawn within a plane divides that plane into two distinct regions, each of which is referred to as a half-plane.
• **Incidence Properties of Points and Lines**: Through a single given point, an infinite number of different straight lines can be drawn in various directions.
• **Unique Line Axiom**: Through two distinct, given points, exactly one and only one straight line can be drawn, establishing the principle of linear determination.
• **Intersection of Two Lines**: Two distinct lines in a plane can intersect at most at one single common point, and they can never intersect at more than one point.
• **Intersection of a Line and a Plane**: A straight line can intersect a plane at a single point, lie entirely within the plane, or be completely parallel to the plane.
• **Intersection of Two Planes**: Two distinct planes, when they intersect, always meet along a straight line rather than at a single point.
• **Coplanar Objects**: Lines, segments, or points that lie within the exact same single plane are termed coplanar elements.
• **Non-Coplanar Objects**: Elements that do not lie within the same plane, such as skew lines in three-dimensional space, are called non-coplanar.
• **Spatial Geometry Foundation**: Understanding points, lines, and planes provides the foundational mental model required for all advanced branches of mathematics and engineering drawing.

• **Introduction to Curves**: In geometry, a curve is any continuous line drawn on a surface without lifting the pencil, and it does not have to be bent or curved in everyday language.
• **Simple Curve**: A simple curve is defined as a curve that changes direction but never crosses, intersects, or overlaps itself at any point along its length.
• **Non-Simple Curve**: A curve that crosses or intersects itself at one or more points during its continuous path is categorized as a non-simple curve.
• **Open Curve**: An open curve is a curve in which the starting point and the ending point are completely separate and distinct from one another.
• **Closed Curve**: A closed curve is a continuous curve that starts and ends at the exact same point, enclosing a specific area or space without any breaks.
• **Simple Closed Curve**: A simple closed curve is a closed curve that does not cross itself anywhere, forming a clean loop such as a circle or oval.
• **Interior of a Closed Curve**: The interior refers to the entire inner region or set of points enclosed strictly within the boundaries of a closed curve.
• **Exterior of a Closed Curve**: The exterior refers to the vast region or set of points situated completely outside the boundaries of a closed curve.
• **Boundary of a Closed Curve**: The boundary consists of the exact set of points that form the continuous line or perimeter separating the interior from the exterior.
• **Region of a Curve**: The union of the interior of a closed curve and its boundary constitutes the complete closed region.
• **Polygon Definition**: A polygon is a special type of simple closed curve made up entirely of three or more straight line segments joined end-to-end.
• **Sides of a Polygon**: The individual straight line segments that form the outer framework of a polygon are called its sides or edges.
• **Vertices of a Polygon**: The points where two adjacent sides of a polygon meet are called vertices (singular: vertex).
• **Adjacent Sides**: Two sides of a polygon that share a common vertex endpoint are referred to as adjacent sides.
• **Diagonals of a Polygon**: A diagonal is a straight line segment that connects any two non-adjacent vertices of a polygon.
• **Classification of Polygons by Sides**: Polygons are named and classified based on the total number of sides and angles they possess, starting from three sides.
• **Triangle as a Polygon**: A polygon with three sides and three vertices is called a triangle, which is the simplest possible closed rectilinear polygon.
• **Quadrilateral as a Polygon**: A four-sided polygon with four vertices and four angles is known as a quadrilateral.

• **Introduction to Angles**: An angle is formed when two distinct rays share a common initial starting point or endpoint.
• **Vertex of an Angle**: The common initial endpoint shared by the two rays forming an angle is called the vertex of that angle.
• **Arms of the Angle**: The two rays that extend from the vertex to form the sides of the angle are referred to as its arms or sides.
• **Naming an Angle**: An angle is denoted using the symbol $\angle$ followed by three letters (with the vertex letter always in the middle, e.g., $\angle ABC$) or just the vertex letter ($\angle B$).
• **Interior of an Angle**: The interior of an angle consists of all the points located in the open space lying between the two arms of the angle extending infinitely.
• **Exterior of an Angle**: The exterior of an angle comprises all the points located outside the space enclosed by the two extending arms.
• **Angle Region**: The collection of points that make up the angle itself, combined with all the points in its interior, forms the angular region.
• **Measuring Angles**: Angles are measured in degrees ($^\circ$) using a geometric instrument known as a protractor.
• **Acute Angle**: An acute angle is an angle whose measure is strictly greater than $0^\circ$ and strictly less than $90^\circ$.
• **Right Angle**: A right angle is an angle whose measure is exactly equal to $90^\circ$, representing perpendicular directions.
• **Obtuse Angle**: An obtuse angle is an angle whose measure is strictly greater than $90^\circ$ and strictly less than $180^\circ$.
• **Straight Angle**: A straight angle is an angle whose measure is exactly equal to $180^\circ$, where the two arms point in exact opposite directions, forming a straight line.
• **Reflex Angle**: A reflex angle is an angle whose measure is strictly greater than $180^\circ$ and strictly less than $360^\circ$.
• **Complete Angle**: A complete angle (or full turn) is an angle whose measure is exactly equal to $360^\circ$, returning full circle to the starting direction.
• **Zero Angle**: A zero angle occurs when the two arms coincide completely, resulting in a measure of $0^\circ$.
• **Adjacent Angles**: Two angles are called adjacent if they have a common vertex, a common arm, and their interiors do not overlap.
• **Complementary Angles**: Two angles whose sum of measures is exactly equal to $90^\circ$ are termed complementary angles.
• **Supplementary Angles**: Two angles whose sum of measures is exactly equal to $180^\circ$ are termed supplementary angles.

• **Introduction to Triangles**: A triangle is a three-sided polygon and is the most fundamental closed shape in Euclidean geometry.
• **Elements of a Triangle**: Every triangle possesses three sides, three interior angles, and three vertices, giving a total of six main parts.
• **Classification by Sides - Scalene**: A scalene triangle is a triangle in which all three sides are of completely different lengths and all angles have different measures.
• **Classification by Sides - Isosceles**: An isosceles triangle is a triangle that has at least two sides of equal length and two angles of equal measure opposite those sides.
• **Classification by Sides - Equilateral**: An equilateral triangle is a regular polygon with three sides of equal length and three interior angles each measuring $60^\circ$.
• **Classification by Angles - Acute-Angled**: An acute-angled triangle is a triangle in which all three interior angles are acute (less than $90^\circ$).
• **Classification by Angles - Right-Angled**: A right-angled triangle is a triangle in which one of the interior angles measures exactly $90^\circ$.
• **Hypotenuse**: In a right-angled triangle, the side directly opposite the right angle is called the hypotenuse, which is always the longest side of the triangle.
• **Classification by Angles - Obtuse-Angled**: An obtuse-angled triangle is a triangle that contains one interior angle measuring greater than $90^\circ$.
• **Angle Sum Property of a Triangle**: The sum of all three interior angles of any triangle in a flat plane is always exactly equal to $180^\circ$.
• **Exterior Angle of a Triangle**: If any side of a triangle is produced, the angle formed outside the triangle with the adjacent side is an exterior angle.
• **Perimeter of a Triangle**: The perimeter of a triangle is calculated by finding the total sum of the lengths of all its three sides.
• **Altitude of a Triangle**: An altitude (or height) is a perpendicular line segment drawn from any vertex of a triangle to the opposite side (or its extension).
• **Median of a Triangle**: A median is a line segment joining any vertex of a triangle to the midpoint of the opposite side, dividing the triangle into two parts of equal area.
• **Centroid**: The point where all three medians of a triangle intersect concurrently is known as the centroid of the triangle.
• **Orthocenter**: The point of concurrency of the three altitudes of a triangle is called the orthocenter.
• **Triangle Inequality Theorem**: The sum of the lengths of any two sides of a triangle must always be strictly greater than the length of the third remaining side.

• **Introduction to Quadrilaterals**: A quadrilateral is a closed two-dimensional geometric polygon bounded by four straight sides and having four vertices and four interior angles.
• **Angle Sum Property of Quadrilaterals**: The sum of all four interior angles of any quadrilateral is always equal to $360^\circ$.
• **Types of Quadrilaterals**: Quadrilaterals are classified into various special types based on the parallelism, equality, and relationships of their sides and angles.
• **Trapezium Definition**: A trapezium is a quadrilateral in which at least one pair of opposite sides is parallel to each other.
• **Parallelogram Definition**: A parallelogram is a quadrilateral in which both pairs of opposite sides are parallel and equal in length.
• **Properties of a Parallelogram**: In a parallelogram, opposite sides are equal, opposite angles are equal, and adjacent angles are supplementary.
• **Rectangle Definition**: A rectangle is a special type of parallelogram in which all four interior angles are right angles ($90^\circ$).
• **Properties of a Rectangle**: In a rectangle, opposite sides are equal and parallel, diagonals are equal in length, and every angle measures $90^\circ$.
• **Square Definition**: A square is a regular quadrilateral in which all four sides are equal in length and all four interior angles are right angles ($90^\circ$).
• **Properties of a Square**: A square possesses all the properties of a parallelogram, rectangle, and rhombus, with diagonals that bisect each other at right angles.
• **Rhombus Definition**: A rhombus is a parallelogram in which all four sides are of equal length, though its interior angles are not necessarily right angles.
• **Properties of a Rhombus**: The diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at right angles ($90^\circ$), and its opposite angles are equal.
• **Kite Definition**: A kite is a quadrilateral with two distinct pairs of adjacent sides that are equal in length to each other.
• **Diagonals of a Kite**: In a kite, the longer diagonal bisects the shorter diagonal perpendicularly, and the opposite angles between unequal sides are equal.
• **Perimeter of Quadrilaterals**: The perimeter of any quadrilateral is calculated by finding the total arithmetic sum of the lengths of all its four outer sides.
• **Area Concept**: The area of a quadrilateral measures the total two-dimensional space enclosed within its four boundary sides, expressed in square units.
• **Hierarchical Relationship**: Squares, rectangles, and rhombuses are all specialized subsets of the broader category known as parallelograms.

• **Introduction to Circles**: A circle is a simple closed curve consisting of all points in a plane that are at a constant fixed distance from a single fixed center point.
• **Center of a Circle**: The fixed point located at the exact middle of a circle, from which all boundary points are equidistant, is called the center.
• **Radius of a Circle**: A radius is a straight line segment joining the center of the circle to any point on its boundary (plural: radii).
• **Chord of a Circle**: A chord is a straight line segment whose two endpoints both lie on the circumference of the circle.
• **Diameter of a Circle**: A diameter is a special chord that passes directly through the center of the circle, dividing it into two equal halves.
• **Relationship Between Radius and Diameter**: The diameter ($d$) of a circle is always exactly twice the length of its radius ($r$), expressed mathematically as $d = 2r$.
• **Circumference of a Circle**: The circumference is the total length of the boundary or perimeter of a circle, calculated using the mathematical constant $\pi$ (pi).
• **Arc of a Circle**: An arc is any connected, continuous portion of the circumference of a circle.
• **Semi-Circle**: A semi-circle is an arc that represents exactly half of a circle, formed when a diameter divides the circular boundary.
• **Minor and Major Arcs**: An arc smaller than a semi-circle is called a minor arc, while an arc larger than a semi-circle is called a major arc.
• **Interior of a Circle**: The interior of a circle consists of all the points in the plane whose distance from the center is strictly less than the radius.
• **Exterior of a Circle**: The exterior of a circle comprises all the points in the plane whose distance from the center is strictly greater than the radius.
• **Circular Region (Disk)**: The union of the circle's interior and its boundary forms the solid circular region or disk.
• **Sector of a Circle**: A sector is the region enclosed by two radii and the arc intercepted between them, resembling a slice of pie.
• **Segment of a Circle**: A segment of a circle is the region bounded by a chord and the arc lying between its endpoints.
• **Concentric Circles**: Two or more circles that share the exact same center point but have different radii are called concentric circles.
• **Symmetry of a Circle**: A circle possesses an infinite number of lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry about its center.

• **Introduction to Geometric Instruments**: Accurate construction and measurement of geometric figures require specialized mathematical instruments from a standard geometry box.
• **The Ruler (Scale):** A ruler is a straight-edged instrument marked with centimeters and millimeters, used primarily for drawing straight lines and measuring line segments.
• **The Compass**: A compass is a V-shaped drawing instrument with a metal point on one leg and a pencil holder on the other, used to draw circles and arcs.
• **The Divider**: A divider has two hinged metal points and is used for comparing lengths and accurately transferring measurements between a line and a ruler.
• **The Set-Squares**: A pair of triangular plastic templates (one 30°-60°-90° and one 45°-45°-90°) used to draw parallel and perpendicular lines.
• **The Protractor**: A semi-circular transparent instrument graduated from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$, used for measuring and drawing angles with precision.
• **Construction of a Line Segment**: To construct a line segment of a given length, use a ruler and a sharp pencil, marking start and end points accurately.
• **Copying a Line Segment**: A line segment can be copied accurately using a divider to pick up the length and transferring it to a new location.
• **Construction of a Perpendicular Bisector**: A perpendicular bisector is a line that divides a given line segment into two equal halves at a $90^\circ$ angle.
• **Method of Perpendicular Bisector**: Using a compass with a radius greater than half the segment length, intersecting arcs are drawn from both endpoints.
• **Construction of Angles Using Protractor**: An angle of any specific degree measure is constructed by drawing a base ray, placing the protractor center on the vertex, and marking the target.
• **Construction of Standard Angles**: Standard angles such as $60^\circ$, $120^\circ$, $90^\circ$, and $45^\circ$ can be constructed precisely using only a ruler and a compass without a protractor.
• **Constructing a $60^\circ$ Angle**: Draw a ray, use a compass to draw an arc from the vertex, and with the same radius, cut the arc to intersect it, joining the vertex.
• **Constructing a $90^\circ$ Angle**: Construct a perpendicular line at the endpoint of a ray or at a point on a line using compass arc intersections.
• **Angle Bisector Construction**: An angle bisector is a ray that divides a given angle into two equal smaller angles, constructed using intersecting compass arcs.
• **Construction of Parallel Lines**: Parallel lines are constructed using a ruler and set-squares by maintaining a constant perpendicular distance along a transversal.
• **Geometrical Accuracy**: Maintaining a sharp pencil tip and firm instrument placement is essential for achieving error-free geometric constructions.

• **Introduction to Symmetry**: Symmetry is a balanced and proportionate similarity found in geometric shapes, living organisms, and everyday objects.
• **Line Symmetry (Reflection Symmetry):** A figure has line symmetry if it can be folded along a straight line so that the two halves coincide completely and perfectly.
• **Line of Symmetry**: The fold line or mirror line that divides a symmetric figure into two identical, matching halves is called the line of symmetry.
• **Symmetry in a Line Segment**: A straight line segment has exactly one line of symmetry, which is its perpendicular bisector, along with its own line.
• **Symmetry in an Angle**: An angle with equal arms or an angle bisector line has one line of symmetry running directly along its angle bisector ray.
• **Symmetry in Scalene Triangles**: A scalene triangle has zero lines of symmetry because all its sides and angles are of different sizes.
• **Symmetry in Isosceles Triangles**: An isosceles triangle has exactly one line of symmetry passing from the vertex between the equal sides to the midpoint of the base.
• **Symmetry in Equilateral Triangles**: An equilateral triangle possesses exactly three lines of symmetry, each passing through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.
• **Symmetry in Rectangles**: A rectangle has exactly two lines of symmetry, passing through the midpoints of opposite sides (horizontal and vertical).
• **Symmetry in Squares**: A square is a highly symmetric figure possessing exactly four lines of symmetry: two along the median lines and two along the diagonals.
• **Symmetry in Circles**: A circle has an infinite number of lines of symmetry, because every single straight line passing through its center is a line of symmetry.
• **Rotational Symmetry**: A figure has rotational symmetry if it fits onto itself more than once during a complete $360^\circ$ rotation around its center.
• **Center of Rotation**: The fixed point around which a geometric figure is rotated to test for rotational symmetry is called the center of rotation.
• **Angle of Rotation**: The minimum angle through which a figure must be rotated to coincide with its original position is termed the angle of rotation.
• **Order of Rotational Symmetry**: The total number of times a figure matches its original appearance during a full $360^\circ$ rotation is the order of symmetry.
• **Order of Symmetry for a Square**: A square has a rotational symmetry order of 4, matching itself at $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, $270^\circ$, and $360^\circ$.
• **Importance of Symmetry**: Symmetry concepts are widely applied in art, architecture, manufacturing, nature, and molecular chemistry for structural balance and aesthetics.

• **Introduction to Practical Applications**: Geometrical concepts learned in Class 6 form the visual and structural language used in architecture, cartography, and engineering.

• **Role of Triangles in Structures**: Because triangles are rigid and cannot be deformed without changing side lengths, they provide maximum structural stability in bridges and roofs.
• **Tessellations (Tilings):** A tessellation is a pattern of repeating geometric shapes that covers a plane completely without any gaps or overlapping regions.
• **Regular Tessellations**: Tessellations formed using a single type of regular polygon, such as equilateral triangles, squares, or regular hexagons.
• **Art and Rangoli Designs**: Traditional Indian art forms like Rangoli and Kolam extensively use geometric lines, curves, points, and reflective symmetry.
• **Measurement Precision**: Mastery of units of length, angle degrees, and area calculations ensures precision in carpentry, tailoring, and land surveying.
• **Optical Illusions**: Misinterpretations of geometric lines and angles often create fascinating visual and optical illusions in art and design.
• **Computer Graphics and Design**: Digital animation, video games, and CAD software construct 2D and 3D objects using mathematical points, vectors, and polygons.
• **Nature's Geometry**: Hexagonal honeycombs, spiral snail shells, and radial flower petals display complex natural geometry and mathematical optimization.
• **Summary of Points**: Points indicate exact positions and serve as the zero-dimensional origin of all geometric study.
• **Summary of Lines and Rays**: Lines, segments, and rays provide linear pathways and measurement standards across one-dimensional space.
• **Summary of Angles**: Acute, right, obtuse, and straight angles quantify rotational separation and angular orientation.
• **Summary of Polygons**: Triangles, quadrilaterals, and polygons enclose two-dimensional areas with straight boundary segments.
