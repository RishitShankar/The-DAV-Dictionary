• **Subtraction**: The mathematical process of taking one number away from another to find the difference.
• **Difference**: The final result obtained after performing the subtraction operation.
• **Minus Sign (-)**: The specific symbol used to represent the operation of subtraction in mathematics.
• **Minuend**: The larger number from which another number is being subtracted.
• **Subtrahend**: The smaller number that is being taken away from the minuend.
• **Equation**: A mathematical statement showing that two expressions are equal, such as 8 - 3 = 5.
• **Basic Concept**: Subtraction is essentially the opposite or inverse operation of addition.
• **Counting Backwards**: A fundamental mental strategy where one starts at the minuend and counts back by the value of the subtrahend.
• **Number Line**: A visual tool consisting of a horizontal line with numbers marked at equal intervals used to demonstrate subtraction by moving to the left.
• **Leftward Movement**: On a number line, moving to the left signifies a decrease in value, which is the direction for subtraction.
• **Single-Digit Subtraction**: The process of subtracting numbers from 0 to 9, which forms the foundation for all complex subtraction.
• **Zero Property**: Any number minus zero remains the same number (e.g., 7 - 0 = 7).
• **Identity Property**: Subtracting a number from itself always results in zero (e.g., 5 - 5 = 0).
• **One Property**: Subtracting one from any number results in the predecessor or the number just before it.
• **Comparison**: Subtraction is used to determine "how many more" or "how many less" one group has compared to another.

• **Two-Digit Subtraction**: The process of subtracting numbers between 10 and 99, involving the separation of tens and ones places.
• **Place Value**: The system where the position of a digit determines its value (Ones place and Tens place).
• **Column Subtraction**: A method of arranging numbers vertically so that ones are aligned with ones and tens with tens.
• **Right-to-Left Rule**: In multi-digit subtraction, always begin the calculation from the ones column before moving to the tens column.
• **Ones Column**: The first position to the right where the subtraction of individual units occurs.
• **Tens Column**: The second position to the left where the subtraction of groups of ten occurs.
• **No-Regrouping Subtraction**: A scenario where each digit in the subtrahend is smaller than or equal to the corresponding digit in the minuend.
• **Simple Subtraction**: When the ones digit of the minuend is greater than the ones digit of the subtrahend, no special techniques are required.
• **Alignment**: Proper vertical alignment is critical to prevent errors in place value calculation.
• **Visual Representation**: Using base-ten blocks (rods for tens, units for ones) to physically demonstrate the removal of values.
• **Expanded Form**: Breaking down numbers into tens and ones (e.g., 45 = 40 + 5) to simplify the subtraction process.
• **Mental Math**: The ability to perform subtraction without paper or pencil by breaking numbers into manageable parts.
• **Subtraction Facts**: Memorized pairs of numbers that help in quick recall of basic subtraction results.
• **Decomposition**: Breaking a number into smaller parts to make the subtraction process easier to visualize.
• **Accuracy**: Checking the work by ensuring the digits are correctly placed in their respective columns.

• **Regrouping (Borrowing)**: A technique used when the digit in the ones place of the minuend is smaller than the digit in the ones place of the subtrahend.
• **The Need for Borrowing**: Occurs when there are not enough ones to subtract, requiring the conversion of a ten into ten ones.
• **Ten-to-One Conversion**: One ten is "broken" or "regrouped" into ten individual ones to increase the value in the ones column.
• **Renaming**: The process of changing the form of a number (e.g., 42 becomes 3 tens and 12 ones) to facilitate subtraction.
• **Borrowing from Tens**: Taking one unit from the tens column and adding it to the ones column to make the subtraction possible.
• **Strikethrough Method**: A visual notation where the original tens digit is crossed out and replaced with a smaller value after borrowing.
• **Updating the Minuend**: After borrowing, the ones digit becomes a two-digit number (e.g., 12), and the tens digit decreases by one.
• **Step-by-Step Execution**: First, check the ones column; if the top number is smaller, borrow from the tens column.
• **Completing the Ones**: Subtract the subtrahend ones from the newly formed ones value.
• **Completing the Tens**: Subtract the subtrahend tens from the remaining tens value.
• **Verification**: Ensuring that the tens digit was correctly reduced by one after the borrowing process.
• **Common Error**: Forgetting to reduce the tens digit after borrowing from it is a frequent mistake in early learning.
• **Place Value Awareness**: Understanding that 1 ten is equal to 10 ones is the core logic behind regrouping.
• **Practice of Regrouping**: Essential for solving subtraction problems involving numbers like 52 - 27.
• **Logical Sequence**: Always complete the ones column regrouping before attempting to subtract the tens column.

• **Word Problems**: Real-life scenarios that require the application of subtraction to find a solution.
• **Key Phrases**: Identifying words like "left," "remaining," "difference," "how many more," and "taken away" to signal subtraction.
• **Problem Analysis**: Reading the problem carefully to identify the minuend (total) and the subtrahend (amount removed).
• **Mathematical Modeling**: Converting a word problem into a formal subtraction equation.
• **Contextual Understanding**: Recognizing that subtraction is used to find what is left after a portion is removed.
• **Multi-Step Thinking**: Sometimes word problems require identifying the total first before performing the subtraction.
• **Data Extraction**: Pulling the relevant numbers from the narrative of the word problem.
• **Logical Deduction**: Determining if the situation describes a decrease in quantity, which confirms the need for subtraction.
• **Labeling**: Including units (e.g., apples, pencils, books) in the final answer to provide context.
• **Story Problems**: Narratives that help students relate abstract numbers to tangible objects in their daily lives.
• **Critical Thinking**: Evaluating whether the answer makes sense in the context of the problem (e.g., the answer should be smaller than the starting number).
• **Comparison Problems**: Using subtraction to find the difference in age, height, or quantity between two objects or people.
• **Subtraction in Daily Life**: Applying subtraction to shopping, sharing items, or measuring time.
• **Problem Solving Strategy**: Read, Identify, Set up, Solve, and Check.
• **Clarity in Presentation**: Writing the final answer clearly after performing the calculation.

• **Subtraction with Zero**: Special rules apply when the minuend contains a zero in the ones or tens place.
• **Zero in the Ones Place**: If the ones digit of the minuend is zero, you must regroup from the tens place.
• **Example (50 - 23)**: Since 0 is less than 3, borrow 1 ten from 50, making it 4 tens and 10 ones.
• **Regrouping with Zero**: The zero becomes 10, allowing the subtraction to proceed normally.
• **Tens Place Adjustment**: The tens digit of the minuend decreases by one because one ten was moved to the ones place.
• **Zero as a Placeholder**: Understanding that zero holds the place value even if it has no value itself.
• **Subtraction from Multiples of Ten**: Numbers like 10, 20, 30, etc., require regrouping when subtracting any non-zero digit.
• **Consistency**: The rules of regrouping remain the same regardless of whether the minuend has a zero or not.
• **Visual Aids**: Using place value charts to track the movement of tens into the ones column.
• **Mental Check**: Always verify that the tens digit was reduced after borrowing from a zero-containing number.
• **Common Pitfall**: Assuming that 0 - 3 = 3; students must be taught that 0 - 3 requires borrowing.
• **Conceptual Mastery**: Understanding that borrowing is just rearranging the same total value into different place values.
• **Foundation for Larger Numbers**: Mastering subtraction with zero in Class 2 prepares students for 3-digit subtraction in higher grades.
• **Precision**: Ensuring the numbers are aligned correctly so that the borrowing process is visible and logical.
• **Self-Correction**: If the result of the ones column is negative, it is a sign that regrouping was missed.

• **Checking Subtraction**: A method to ensure the accuracy of the calculated difference.
• **Inverse Operation**: Addition is the inverse of subtraction, meaning it can be used to verify the result.
• **The Check Formula**: (Difference + Subtrahend = Minuend).
• **Verification Process**: Add the result (difference) to the number that was subtracted (subtrahend).
• **Result Confirmation**: If the sum of the difference and the subtrahend equals the original minuend, the subtraction is correct.
• **Error Detection**: If the sum does not match the minuend, there is an error in the subtraction calculation.
• **Importance of Checking**: Encourages students to be responsible for their own work and reduces calculation errors.
• **Confidence Building**: Knowing a reliable way to check answers increases student confidence in mathematics.
• **Systematic Approach**: Checking should be a standard final step for every subtraction problem solved.
• **Addition Skills**: Checking subtraction requires strong addition skills, reinforcing both concepts simultaneously.
• **Mental Verification**: For simple problems, students can mentally add the difference and subtrahend to check.
• **Written Verification**: For complex problems, writing the addition equation below the subtraction equation is recommended.
• **Habit Formation**: Developing the habit of checking work is a key academic skill for primary education.
• **Accuracy Focus**: The goal is to achieve 100% accuracy through systematic verification.
• **Teacher-Student Interaction**: Using the check method allows teachers to identify exactly where a student made a mistake.

• **Subtraction Properties**: Fundamental rules that govern how subtraction behaves in mathematics.
• **Property 1 (Zero)**: Any number minus zero equals the number itself (n - 0 = n).
• **Property 2 (Identity)**: Any number minus itself equals zero (n - n = 0).
• **Property 3 (Order Matters)**: Unlike addition, the order of numbers in subtraction cannot be changed (a - b is not the same as b - a).
• **Commutative Property Absence**: Subtraction is not commutative; switching the order changes the result or makes it impossible in whole numbers.
• **Non-Negative Constraint**: In Class 2, we only subtract smaller numbers from larger numbers to ensure the result is a whole number.
• **Difference Magnitude**: The difference is always smaller than or equal to the minuend.
• **Relationship with Addition**: Subtraction and addition are "fact families" (e.g., 5 + 3 = 8, 8 - 3 = 5, 8 - 5 = 3).
• **Fact Families**: A group of three numbers that can form two addition and two subtraction equations.
• **Understanding Relationships**: Recognizing that subtraction is the process of finding the missing part of a whole.
• **Whole-Part Model**: The minuend is the "whole," and the subtrahend and difference are the "parts."
• **Conceptual Depth**: Understanding these properties helps students solve problems faster and with better logic.
• **Mathematical Language**: Using terms like "minuend" and "subtrahend" correctly reinforces these properties.
• **Logical Reasoning**: Properties help students predict the outcome of a subtraction problem before calculating.
• **Foundation**: These properties serve as the building blocks for algebra in later years.

• **Mental Subtraction Strategies**: Techniques to solve subtraction problems quickly without written work.
• **Counting Back**: Starting at the minuend and counting back the number of units in the subtrahend.
• **Bridging to Ten**: Subtracting to reach the nearest ten, then subtracting the remainder.
• **Compensation**: Adjusting numbers to make them easier to subtract (e.g., 15 - 9 is the same as 16 - 10).
• **Decomposition Strategy**: Breaking the subtrahend into tens and ones (e.g., 45 - 12 = 45 - 10 - 2).
• **Doubles Strategy**: Using known doubles (e.g., 8 - 4 = 4) to solve related subtraction problems.
• **Near Doubles**: Using the knowledge of doubles to solve problems like 9 - 4 (since 8 - 4 = 4, 9 - 4 must be 5).
• **Number Bonds**: Using knowledge of pairs that make 10 to subtract quickly (e.g., 10 - 7 = 3).
• **Efficiency**: Mental math strategies save time and improve overall numerical fluency.
• **Practice**: Regular practice of mental strategies leads to automaticity in basic subtraction facts.
• **Flexibility**: Encouraging students to find the strategy that works best for their individual thinking style.
• **Speed**: Mental math is essential for competitive mathematics and daily life situations.
• **Confidence**: Being able to calculate mentally makes students feel more capable in mathematics.
• **Accuracy in Mental Math**: Requires a strong grasp of place value and number relationships.
• **Application**: Mental strategies are best applied to 1-digit and simple 2-digit subtraction problems.
