• **Introduction to Chapter 12: "The First Cities"**: This chapter explores the fascinating journey of human civilization from small farming and herding communities to the establishment of the world's earliest urban centers, specifically focusing on the Indus Valley Civilization.
• **Definition of a Civilization**: A civilization is an advanced state of human social development and organization, characterized by complex urban planning, technological advancements, social stratification, writing systems, and institutionalized governance.
• **Significance of Urbanization**: The transition from rural village life to urban city life represents a major milestone in human history, marked by specialized labor, monumental architecture, and long-distance trade networks.
• **The Discovery of the Indus Valley Civilization**: In the 1920s, archaeologists discovered the remains of ancient cities in the Indus River valley, completely rewriting the ancient history of the Indian subcontinent.
• **Harappa as the First Site**: Harappa, located in present-day Pakistan, was the first site to be discovered and excavated, which is why the Indus Valley Civilization is also widely known as the Harappan Civilization.
• **Geographical Extent**: The Harappan Civilization was one of the largest ancient civilizations, spanning across parts of modern-day Pakistan, northwestern India, and eastern Afghanistan.
• **Major Geographical Boundaries**: The civilization covered an area much larger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia, extending from Jammu in the north to Gujarat in the south, and from Balochistan in the west to western Uttar Pradesh in the east.
• **Major Rivers of the Region**: The civilization flourished primarily around the Indus River and its numerous tributaries, which provided vital resources for survival, agriculture, and transportation.
• **The Drying Up of Saraswati**: Archaeological and geological studies indicate that many settlements also thrived along the banks of the now-dry Saraswati-Ghaggar river system.
• **Dating the Civilization**: Radiocarbon dating and archaeological stratigraphy place the mature phase of the Indus Valley Civilization roughly between 2500 BCE and 1900 BCE.
• **Pre-Harappan and Post-Harappan Phases**: The civilization evolved through distinct phases, beginning with early farming settlements (Early Harappan), moving into the highly urbanized phase (Mature Harappan), and finally experiencing a decline (Late Harappan).
• **Climatic and Environmental Conditions**: During the height of the civilization, the region received more rainfall and had dense forests, supporting abundant wildlife, timber resources, and extensive agriculture.
• **Role of Floods**: The annual flooding of the Indus River deposited fertile silt on the plains, which greatly enhanced agricultural productivity and sustained large urban populations.
• **Strategic Location of Settlements**: Cities were strategically established near river basins to ensure a steady water supply, easy irrigation, and access to trade routes via waterways.
• **Uniformity Across Sites**: One of the most remarkable features of the Indus Valley Civilization is the striking uniformity in town planning, weights, measures, pottery, and brick sizes across hundreds of miles.

• **Mastery in Town Planning**: Harappan cities exhibited an exceptional level of urban planning, engineering skill, and civic organization that was unmatched in the ancient world.
• **The Two-Part City Structure**: Most major Harappan cities were typically divided into two distinct parts: a raised western area known as the Citadel and a larger lower eastern residential area.
• **The Citadel**: The Citadel was built on an elevated platform of mud bricks, was smaller in area, and likely housed the ruling elite, administrative buildings, or important religious structures.
• **Fortification of Cities**: Both the Citadel and the lower town areas were often surrounded by massive defensive walls made of baked bricks to protect against floods and external threats.
• **Grid Iron Town Planning**: The streets of Harappan cities were laid out in a strict grid pattern, intersecting at right angles and dividing the city into neat rectangular or square blocks.
• **Width and Orientation of Streets**: Main streets were exceptionally wide, running straight from north to south and east to west, designed to allow easy movement of traffic and natural cleansing by the wind.
• **Advanced Drainage System**: The covered, underground drainage system of the Indus cities is considered one of the greatest engineering achievements of the ancient world.
• **House Drains Connecting to Street Drains**: Kitchens and bathrooms in individual houses had drains that emptied into small sumps, which then connected to larger covered brick drains running alongside the main streets.
• **Inspection Manholes**: Street drains were fitted with removable brick slabs at regular intervals, allowing municipal authorities to inspect, clean, and clear blockages.
• **Prevention of Waterlogging**: The meticulous planning of the drainage system ensured that wastewater did not stagnate in residential areas, promoting high standards of public hygiene and health.
• **Building Materials**: Harappan architects predominantly used kiln-baked bricks of a standardized size and ratio (typically 4:2:1), which were much stronger and more durable than sun-dried mud bricks.
• **Interlocking Brick Masonry**: Bricks were laid in an interlocking pattern, known as English bond construction, which provided incredible structural strength to withstand earthquakes and floods.
• **Multi-Room Residential Houses**: Houses in the lower town varied in size, featuring multiple rooms built around a central courtyard, which served as the hub for domestic activities like cooking and weaving.
• **Privacy in Architecture**: Houses were designed with doors and windows opening into interior courtyards and side alleys rather than directly onto the main streets, ensuring privacy and reducing noise.
• **Absence of Palaces and Temples**: Unlike contemporary civilizations in Egypt and Mesopotamia, Harappan cities did not feature grand palaces, royal tombs, or massive monumental temples, suggesting a unique egalitarian or mercantile form of governance.

• **Mohenjo-daro ("The Mound of the Dead")**: Mohenjo-daro is one of the largest and best-preserved urban sites of the Indus Valley Civilization, located in the Larkana district of Sindh, Pakistan.
• **Discovery of Mohenjo-daro**: The site was excavated in 1922 by the pioneering archaeologist Rakhaldas Bandyopadhyay under the supervision of John Marshall.
• **The Great Bath**: The Great Bath is the most famous architectural structure in Mohenjo-daro, located within the Citadel area, measuring approximately 39 feet long, 23 feet wide, and 8 feet deep.
• **Construction of the Great Bath**: Built with finely fitted bricks, the tank was made watertight by applying a thick layer of bitumen (tar) between the inner brick walls and an outer layer of mud brick.
• **Water Supply and Drainage for the Great Bath**: Water was supplied to the tank from a large dedicated well nearby, and used water was emptied through a massive vaulted drain into the city's main drainage network.
• **Purpose of the Great Bath**: Historians and archaeologists believe the Great Bath was used for special ritualistic or ceremonial bathing by the elite or the entire community during important religious festivals.
• **Dressing Rooms Surrounding the Great Bath**: A series of small rooms and changing chambers surrounded the pool, with staircases leading down into the water from the northern and southern ends.
• **The Great Granary**: Situated near the Great Bath in Mohenjo-daro, the Great Granary was a massive warehouse structure used for storing surplus agricultural grains like wheat and barley.
• **Ventilation System in Granaries**: The granary was cleverly engineered with raised brick platforms and air ducts beneath the floorboards to keep the stored grains dry, well-ventilated, and safe from pests and moisture.
• **Significance of Granaries**: The presence of large granaries in multiple Harappan cities indicates a centralized system of collecting, storing, and redistributing agricultural produce during times of drought or famine.
• **The Assembly Hall**: Mohenjo-daro featured a large pillared hall with thick walls and twenty brick piers arranged in four rows, which may have served as a meeting place for civic leaders or a cultural assembly hall.
• **Domestic Wells**: Almost every house in Mohenjo-daro had its own private brick-lined well, ensuring an independent and reliable supply of clean drinking water for every family.
• **Street Lighting and Waste Bins**: Archaeological evidence suggests that brick bins for collecting street refuse were placed at strategic locations, reflecting advanced municipal cleanliness practices.
• **Decline of Mohenjo-daro**: The city experienced multiple floods of the Indus River, causing silt deposition, economic decline, and eventual abandonment around 1900 BCE.
• **UNESCO World Heritage Status**: Due to its exceptional archaeological importance and well-preserved urban layout, the ruins of Mohenjo-daro were designated as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980.

• **Harappa (The Type Site)**: Harappa, located in the Sahiwal district of Punjab, Pakistan, along the dry bed of the Ravi River, gave its name to the entire Indus civilization.
• **Destruction of Harappan Bricks**: In the 19th century, British engineers laying railway tracks used millions of baked bricks from the ancient ruins of Harappa as ballast, destroying a large portion of the site before systematic excavation began.
• **The Six Granaries of Harappa**: Harappa featured a row of six massive granaries built on a raised brick platform near the river bank, facilitating the easy loading and unloading of grain transported by boats.
• **Workmen's Quarters**: Excavations near the granaries revealed rows of small, uniform single-room circular working platforms and small two-room quarters, believed to have housed laborers or agricultural workers.
• **Dholavira (The Island City)**: Dholavira, located on Khadir Beyt in the Rann of Kutch, Gujarat, is one of the largest and most spectacular Harappan sites in India.
• **Unique Three-Part Division**: Unlike most other cities divided into two parts, Dholavira was uniquely divided into three principal sections: the Citadel, the Middle Town, and the Lower Town, each heavily fortified.
• **Massive Stone Architecture**: While most Harappan cities used baked bricks, Dholavira extensively utilized finely dressed sandstone blocks for building its massive walls, gateways, and monumental structures.
• **Sophisticated Water Management in Dholavira**: Located in an arid region with scanty rainfall, Dholavira's inhabitants engineered an ingenious system of massive stone-cut reservoirs and dams to harvest, store, and channel rainwater.
• **The Dholavira Signboard**: Archaeologists discovered a massive inscription board featuring ten large signs of the Indus script inlaid in white gypsum, found near the northern gateway of the city.
• **Lothal (The Port City)**: Lothal, situated near the Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat, was a major coastal trading outpost and bustling port city of the Indus Valley Civilization.
• **The Dockyard at Lothal**: Lothal is globally famous for its massive brick-built dockyard, measuring about 216 meters long and 37 meters wide, connected by a channel to the Bhogavo River and the Arabian Sea.
• **Function of the Lothal Dockyard**: The dockyard allowed sea-going ships and merchant vessels to enter the port during high tide for loading and unloading goods, facilitating thriving maritime trade with Mesopotamia and Oman.
• **The Bead-Making Factory**: Lothal was a major industrial center specializing in the mass production of precious and semi-precious stone beads, such as agate, jasper, chalcedony, and lapis lazuli.
• **Fire Altars at Lothal**: Excavations at Lothal uncovered brick structures identified as fire altars, suggesting that the inhabitants practiced fire worship or performed specific domestic or community rituals.
• **Burial Practices at Lothal**: Lothal also yielded unique double burials, where two skeletons were placed in a single grave, sparking interesting historical debates about social customs of the time.

• **Kalibangan (The Black Bangles)**: Kalibangan, located in Hanumangarh district, Rajasthan, along the banks of the dried-up Ghaggar River, is another major provincial capital of the Harappan Civilization.
• **Etymology of Kalibangan**: The name Kalibangan translates to "black bangles," derived from the vast quantities of broken terracotta bangles found littered across the surface of the mounds.
* **Ploughed Agricultural Field**: One of the most significant discoveries at Kalibangan is the world's earliest excavated ploughed field, dating back to the pre-Harappan phase.
• **Pattern of Ploughing**: The field showed a grid of intersecting furrows set at right angles to each other, indicating that farmers simultaneously grew two different crops (such as mustard and gram) in the same field.
• **Fire Altars at Kalibangan**: Kalibangan featured raised brick platforms containing fire altars, which were likely used for performing religious rituals and animal or grain sacrifices by the ruling elite.
• **Banawali**: Located in Fatehabad district, Haryana, Banawali is an important Harappan site that shows evidence of both pre-Harappan and mature Harappan cultures.
• **Oval-Shaped Town Plan**: Unlike the strict rectangular grid plans of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, Banawali had an oval-shaped layout enclosed within thick defensive walls.
• **Superb Quality Terracotta Plough**: Excavations at Banawali yielded a well-preserved toy model of a plough made of terracotta, proving that actual wooden ploughs were widely used in agriculture.
• **Rakhigarhi**: Rakhigarhi, located in Hisar district, Haryana, is currently recognized as the largest Harappan site in the world, spanning over 350 hectares.
• **Significance of Rakhigarhi**: Recent extensive excavations at Rakhigarhi have provided crucial insights into the genetic makeup, dietary habits, and social organization of the Indus Valley people.
• **Surkotada**: Located in Gujarat, Surkotada is a fortified Harappan citadel site that is particularly famous for yielding the earliest actual skeletal remains of horses in the Indian subcontinent.
• **Chanhudaro**: Chanhudaro, located in Sindh, Pakistan, was a specialized craft-production center that lacked a fortified citadel, focusing entirely on shell-working, seal-making, and bead manufacturing.
• **Kot Diji**: Kot Diji, situated in Sindh, Pakistan, represents an important site that provides a clear stratigraphic transition from the early farming cultures to the mature urban Harappan civilization.
• **Rupnagar (Ropar)**: Located in Punjab, India, Ropar was the first Harappan site to be excavated in independent India, showing continuous habitation from the Harappan era down to medieval times.
• **Alamgirpur**: Situated in Meerut district, Uttar Pradesh, Alamgirpur marks the easternmost boundary of the Indus Valley Civilization, yielding pottery, terracotta items, and impression seals.

• **Agricultural Foundation**: Agriculture was the primary economic backbone of the Indus Valley Civilization, providing food security to sustain large urban populations living in non-farming cities.
• **Major Crops Cultivated**: Harappan farmers grew a wide variety of crops, including wheat, barley, pulses, peas, lentils, sesame, linseed, and various types of millets.
• **Introduction of Rice**: Archaeological evidence from sites in Gujarat (like Lothal and Rangpur) indicates that rice was also cultivated in the later phases of the civilization.
• **Cotton Cultivation**: The Indus people were among the very first in the world to cultivate cotton for textile production, with woven cotton fragments recovered at Mohenjo-daro.
• **Irrigation Techniques**: Due to erratic rainfall, farmers utilized canal irrigation drawing water from rivers, as well as wells and water reservoirs to water their fields during dry spells.
• **Domestication of Animals**: Animal husbandry played a vital role in the rural and urban economy, providing milk, meat, wool, leather, and draft power for farming and transport.
• **Livestock Kept**: The Harappans domesticated cattle (zebu oxen), buffaloes, sheep, goats, pigs, and humped bulls, which appear frequently on soapstone seals.
• **Working Animals**: Oxen and bulls were extensively used to pull heavy carts and draw wooden ploughs through the fields, acting as the primary beasts of burden.
• **Knowledge of the Horse**: While domestic horses were likely introduced or known in limited numbers at sites like Surkotada, the principal draft and riding animals were humped bulls, elephants, and camels.
• **Hunting and Gathering**: Wild animals such as deer, wild boar, fish, and birds were hunted or caught to supplement the daily diet of the urban and rural populations.
• **Surplus Production**: High agricultural and pastoral productivity created a massive food surplus, which was stored in state granaries to feed city dwellers, craftspeople, and traders who did not grow their own food.
• **Division of Labor**: The surplus food economy allowed society to diversify into specialized professions, freeing individuals from farming to become full-time artisans, builders, traders, and administrators.
• **Metallurgy and Metalworking**: Harappan craftspeople mastered metallurgy, extracting copper from Rajasthan and Oman, and alloying it with tin (imported from Afghanistan or Central India) to produce strong bronze tools and weapons.
• **Flint Blade Technology**: Despite knowing metals, the Harappans continued to produce sharp stone blades made from chert (flint), mass-produced using specialized core-reduction techniques.
• **Pottery Making**: Potter's wheels were widely used to manufacture fine red-and-black painted pottery decorated with geometric patterns, pipal leaves, fish scales, and animal motifs.

• **Thriving Trade Network**: The Indus Valley Civilization maintained a vast and highly organized commercial network that facilitated internal trade within the subcontinent and external trade with distant foreign lands.
• **Internal Trade Routes**: Raw materials and finished goods were transported across vast distances using bullock carts over land routes and flat-bottomed river boats along major waterways like the Indus.
• **Procurement of Raw Materials**: Since major cities lacked local mineral resources, traders imported raw materials from distant regions, such as copper from Khetri in Rajasthan and Oman.
• **Precious Stones and Gems**: Carnelian was sourced from Gujarat, lapis lazuli from Badakhshan in Afghanistan, turquoise from Central Asia, and shell from the coastal waters of Saurashtra and south India.
• **Foreign Trade with Mesopotamia**: Archaeological evidence, including Indus seals found in Sumerian cities (Mesopotamia) and Mesopotamian texts mentioning "Meluhha," proves active maritime trade between the two civilizations.
• **Identification of Meluhha**: Historians generally identify "Meluhha" in ancient Mesopotamian records as the geographical region of the Indus Valley Civilization.
• **Exports to Mesopotamia**: The Harappans exported luxury and utilitarian goods, including timber, ivory, pearls, carnelian beads, cotton textiles, and agricultural produce to the Middle East.
• **Imports from Mesopotamia and Gulf**: In return, the Indus cities imported silver, gold, tin, and certain specialized manufactured goods from Mesopotamian and Persian Gulf merchants.
• **Standardized Weights and Measures**: To ensure fair trade and taxation, the Harappans developed a highly accurate, standardized system of weights and measures made of chert, jasper, and gneiss.
• **Binary and Decimal Weight System**: The weights followed a binary system for smaller quantities (1, 2, 4, 8, 16 up to 64) and a decimal system for larger weights, demonstrating advanced mathematical knowledge.
• **Measuring Sticks**: Archaeologists have discovered calibrated measuring sticks made of shell and bronze, indicating that linear measurement was strictly standardized across the civilization.
• **The Indus Script**: The Harappans developed a distinct writing system consisting of pictographic and logographic signs, inscribed primarily on soapstone seals, copper tablets, and pottery.
• **Nature of the Script**: The Indus script remains undeciphered to this day because inscriptions are extremely short (averaging only 4 to 5 signs) and there is no bilingual inscription (like the Rosetta Stone) to help translate it.
• **Direction of Writing**: The script was written primarily from right to left in the first line, and then left to right in the second line (a writing style known as *boustrophedon*).
• **Significance of Seals**: Thousands of steatite (soapstone) seals have been recovered, featuring masterfully carved animal figures (such as the unicorn, humped bull, tiger, and elephant) accompanied by lines of the undeciphered script, used primarily as stamps for trade and administrative ownership.

• **Social Structure and Hierarchy**: Although Harappan cities lacked grand palaces, archaeological evidence reveals a stratified society composed of different social classes based on occupation, wealth, and administrative status.
• **The Ruling Elite**: A class of powerful rulers, priests, or wealthy merchants likely governed the major cities, residing in the fortified Citadel areas and overseeing urban planning, trade, and defense.
• **The Middle Class**: The middle social tier consisted of skilled artisans, scribes, traders, architects, and teachers who lived in the spacious brick houses of the lower town.
• **Laborers and Farmers**: The lower stratum comprised agricultural workers, builders, potters, and manual laborers who lived in modest quarters and sustained the economic infrastructure of the empire.
• **Role of Women in Society**: Terracotta figurines of mother goddesses and various ornaments suggest that women held an important and respected place within the family and religious life.
• **Art and Craft Production**: Harappan craftspeople were exceptionally skilled, producing exquisite jewelry made of gold, silver, copper, terracotta, faience, and semi-precious stones.
• **The "Dancing Girl" of Mohenjo-daro**: One of the most famous masterpieces of Harappan metal casting is the bronze statue of a young dancing girl, showcasing advanced lost-wax (*cire perdue*) casting techniques.
• **The "Priest-King" Statue**: A small limestone bust depicting a bearded nobleman wearing an ornamented headband and a cloak with trefoil patterns is often referred to by archaeologists as the "Priest-King."
• **Terracotta Figurines**: Artisans mass-produced numerous terracotta toys, animal figures, carts with moving wheels, and mother goddess figurines, reflecting both daily life and religious beliefs.
• **Faience Technology**: The Harappans invented an early form of glazed artificial ceramic material known as faience, used to make shiny beads, bangles, pendants, and tiny vessels.
• **Religious Beliefs and Practices**: Based on terracotta figurines and seal motifs, religion played a significant role in daily life, though it lacked the massive temples seen in Egypt or Mesopotamia.
• **Worship of Nature**: The Indus people worshipped nature, evidenced by depictions of sacred pipal trees, flowing water, and animals, reflecting a deep spiritual connection to the environment.
• **The Mother Goddess**: Numerous terracotta female figurines adorned with elaborate headdresses point to the widespread worship of a Mother Goddess associated with fertility, nature, and prosperity.
• **Proto-Shiva (Pashupati Seal)**: A famous seal depicts a seated, three-faced yogic figure surrounded by animals (tiger, elephant, rhino, buffalo, and deer), identified by archaeologists as "Pashupati" (Lord of Animals), an early prototype of Lord Shiva.
• **Sacred Animals and Symbols**: The unicorn and the humped bull were revered mythical and real animals frequently featured on seals, alongside sacred motifs like the swastika and intersecting circles.

• **The Mystery of the Decline**: Around 1900 BCE, the magnificent Indus Valley Civilization began a steady, irreversible decline, leading to the gradual abandonment of its sprawling urban centers.
• **Transformation into Rural Life**: Urban life collapsed, standardized weights, the script, and long-distance trade disappeared, and people migrated to smaller, rural farming settlements in the east and south.
• **Multiple Theories of Decline**: Historians and scientists agree that there was no single cause for the collapse; rather, a combination of environmental, ecological, and socio-economic factors brought down the civilization.
• **Ecological Degradation and Deforestation**: Massive clearing of forests to bake millions of bricks and fuel copper smelting furnaces led to severe ecological degradation, soil erosion, and climate drying.
• **River Floods and Course Changes**: Catastrophic floods of the Indus and its tributaries repeatedly destroyed cities like Mohenjo-daro, while tectonic shifts caused rivers like the Saraswati to dry up or change their courses.
• **Climate Change and Aridity**: A major shift in climate caused a significant decrease in monsoon rainfall, turning fertile regions into arid deserts and making sustained agriculture impossible.
• **Epidemics and Diseases**: Overcrowded urban centers with complex drainage systems may have suffered from devastating waterborne epidemics or infectious diseases that decimated populations.
• **External Invasions**: Earlier historians hypothesized that invading Indo-Aryan tribes (Aryans) destroyed Harappan cities, a theory largely discounted today due to lack of archaeological evidence of large-scale warfare.
• **Economic Collapse**: The disruption of international trade with Mesopotamia and internal supply chains choked the urban economy, forcing city dwellers to disperse into village communities.
• **Legacy of the Indus Valley Civilization**: Despite its collapse, the foundational elements of Harappan culture deeply influenced subsequent Indian civilizations, shaping the cultural heritage of the subcontinent.
• **Agricultural Continuity**: Farming techniques, crop selection (wheat, barley, cotton), and rural irrigation methods developed by the Harappans were successfully adopted by later Vedic societies.
• **Urban Planning and Sanitation**: The concepts of grid-iron street layouts, domestic bathing platforms, and covered brick drainage systems laid the earliest foundations of urban sanitation in India.
• **Artistic and Craft Traditions**: Traditional Indian jewelry-making, bead crafting, terracotta pottery, and metal-casting techniques trace their direct roots back to Harappan workshops.
• **Religious and Cultural Survivals**: Practices such as yoga, meditation, worship of nature, sacred trees (pipal), and reverence for animal and mother goddess figures survived into later Hindu and Indian traditions.
• **Historical Importance**: The discovery of the Indus Valley Civilization pushed back the documented history of India by thousands of years, proving that South Asia was home to one of the world's oldest, most advanced urban civilizations.
