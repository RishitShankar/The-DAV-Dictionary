• **Definition of a Factor**: A **factor** of a natural number is defined as an integer that divides that number evenly, leaving a remainder of zero.
• **Alternative Terminology**: In classical arithmetic, factors are also frequently referred to as **divisors** or **submultiples** of the given number.
• **Finiteness of Factors**: Unlike multiples, the total number of factors for any given finite non-zero natural number is always **finite** and countable.
• **Smallest Factor**: The number **1 (one)** is universally the smallest factor of every single natural number in existence.
• **Greatest Factor**: The **number itself** is always the greatest possible factor of any given non-zero natural number.
• **Boundaries of Factors**: Every factor of a given non-zero natural number is either less than or equal to the number itself; no factor can be greater than the number.
• **Property of 1**: The number 1 possesses a unique property in factor theory: it has exactly one factor, which is 1 itself, making it neither prime nor composite.
• **Finding Factors by Multiplication**: Every factor other than 1 and the number itself always occurs in pairs whose product equals the given number (e.g., for 12: $1 \times 12, 2 \times 6, 3 \times 4$).
• **Systematic Listing**: When listing factors, students should always write them in ascending order to prevent omissions and verify completeness using factor pairs.
• **Example - Factors of 18**: The complete set of factors for 18 is $\{1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18\}$, verified by pairing $(1, 18)$, $(2, 9)$, and $(3, 6)$.
• **Example - Factors of 24**: The complete set of factors for 24 is $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24\}$, showing that larger numbers possess richer factor structures.
• **Divisibility Condition**: If $a \times b = c$, then both $a$ and $b$ are factors of $c$, establishing a dual relationship between multiplication equations and factor sets.
• **Non-zero Restriction**: Factors are defined strictly for non-zero integers; division by zero is mathematically undefined and has no place in factor theory.
• **Proper Factors**: A **proper factor** of a number is any factor of that number except the number itself, a concept foundational to understanding perfect numbers.
• **Practical Application**: Factors are used in real-world scenarios requiring equal sharing, partitioning items into identical groups, or arranging objects in rectangular grids.

• **Definition of a Multiple**: A **multiple** of a number is the product obtained when that number is multiplied by any integer, whole number, or counting number ($1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$).
• **Infiniteness of Multiples**: Unlike factors, the number of multiples for any given non-zero natural number is **infinite** and endless, continuing indefinitely.
• **Smallest Multiple**: The **number itself** (multiplied by 1) is always the smallest and first multiple of any given natural number.
• **Absence of Greatest Multiple**: Because the sequence of natural numbers is infinite, it is impossible to determine or state the **greatest multiple** of any number.
• **Relationship to the Number**: Every multiple of a given non-zero natural number is either greater than or equal to the number itself; no non-zero multiple is smaller than the number.
• **Generation Method**: Multiples of a number $n$ are generated by the sequence $\{n \times 1, n \times 2, n \times 3, n \times 4, \dots, n \times k\}$, where $k$ represents any natural number.
• **Example - Multiples of 5**: The first five multiples of 5 are $5, 10, 15, 20,$ and $25$, generated by multiplying 5 by $1, 2, 3, 4,$ and $5$ respectively.
• **Example - Multiples of 7**: The first six multiples of 7 are $7, 14, 21, 28, 35,$ and $42$, illustrating the standard multiplication table sequence.
• **Multiple vs. Factor Distinction**: Students must strictly differentiate that factors divide a number completely, whereas multiples are divided completely by the number.
• **Zero as a Multiple**: While strictly speaking, $n \times 0 = 0$, DAV curriculum typically focuses on positive natural number multiples ($n \times 1, n \times 2, \dots$) for Class 6 level.
• **Properties of Multiples**: Every number is a multiple of itself (since $n \times 1 = n$) and every number is a multiple of 1 (since $1 \times n = n$).
• **Common Multiples Introduction**: When comparing two or more numbers, multiples that appear in the lists of all the given numbers are called **common multiples**.
• **Application in LCM**: Understanding common multiples is the essential stepping stone to calculating the Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) of numbers.
• **Divisibility Check for Multiples**: A number $M$ is a multiple of $n$ if and only if $M$ is completely divisible by $n$ with zero remainder.
• **Structural Symmetry**: While factors form a bounded, descending or matching set, multiples form an unbounded, expanding sequence extending toward infinity.

• **Classification of Numbers**: Numbers can be classified into distinct categories based on the count and properties of their factors, notably even, odd, prime, and composite numbers.
• **Even Numbers**: An **even number** is any integer that is a multiple of 2, meaning it is completely divisible by 2 and ends in digits $0, 2, 4, 6,$ or $8$.
• **Odd Numbers**: An **odd number** is any integer that is not a multiple of 2, meaning it leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by 2 and ends in $1, 3, 5, 7,$ or $9$.
• **Prime Numbers**: A **prime number** is a natural number greater than 1 that has exactly two distinct factors: **1 and the number itself**.
• **Composite Numbers**: A **composite number** is a natural number greater than 1 that has **more than two factors**, meaning it can be divided evenly by numbers other than 1 and itself.
• **List of First Few Primes**: The prime numbers up to 30 are $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23,$ and $29$.
• **Unique Even Prime**: The number **2** is the only even prime number; all other even numbers are composite because they are divisible by 2.
• **Smallest Prime Number**: The number **2** is the smallest prime number in the number system; 1 is neither prime nor composite.
• **Smallest Composite Number**: The number **4** is the smallest composite number, as its factors are $1, 2,$ and $4$.
• **Twin Primes**: **Twin primes** are pairs of prime numbers that have a difference of exactly 2 (e.g., $(3, 5), (5, 7), (11, 13), (17, 19)$).
• **Prime Numbers up to 100**: There are exactly **25 prime numbers** between 1 and 100, a key factual point frequently tested in DAV examinations.
• **Sieve of Eratosthenes**: The ancient mathematical method used to find all prime numbers up to any given limit by systematically crossing out multiples of primes is the **Sieve of Eratosthenes**.
• **Co-Prime Numbers**: Two numbers are said to be **co-prime** (or relatively prime) if their only common factor is **1**, regardless of whether the individual numbers themselves are prime or composite.
• **Examples of Co-Primes**: The pairs $(8, 15)$ are co-prime because their factors are $\text{Factors}(8)=\{1, 2, 4, 8\}$ and $\text{Factors}(15)=\{1, 3, 5, 15\}$, sharing only 1.
• **Fundamental Property of Primes**: Every composite number can be broken down uniquely into a product of prime numbers, forming the bedrock of number theory.

• **Concept of Divisibility Tests**: **Divisibility tests** are quick arithmetic shortcuts used to determine whether a given number is divisible by another number without performing the actual long division process.
• **Divisibility by 2**: A number is divisible by 2 if its units digit (ones place) is an even number, specifically **0, 2, 4, 6, or 8**.
• **Divisibility by 3**: A number is divisible by 3 if the **sum of all its digits** is a multiple of 3 or divisible by 3.
• **Example for Divisibility by 3**: Consider the number 543; sum of digits = $5 + 4 + 3 = 12$. Since 12 is divisible by 3, 543 is completely divisible by 3.
• **Divisibility by 4**: A number is divisible by 4 if the number formed by its **last two digits** (tens and ones places) is divisible by 4, or if the last two digits are both zeros ($00$).
• **Divisibility by 5**: A number is divisible by 5 if its units digit (ones place) is either **0 or 5**.
• **Divisibility by 6**: A number is divisible by 6 if it is simultaneously divisible by **both 2 and 3** (since $2 \times 3 = 6$ and their factors are co-prime).
• **Divisibility by 8**: A number is divisible by 8 if the number formed by its **last three digits** is divisible by 8, or if its last three digits are all zeros ($000$).
• **Divisibility by 9**: A number is divisible by 9 if the **sum of all its digits** is a multiple of 9 or divisible by 9.
• **Divisibility by 10**: A number is divisible by 10 if its units digit is **0**.
• **Divisibility by 11**: A number is divisible by 11 if the difference between the sum of the digits at odd places (from the right) and the sum of the digits at even places (from the right) is **0 or a multiple of 11**.
• **Example for Divisibility by 11**: For 1331, odd place sum = $1 + 3 = 4$, even place sum = $3 + 1 = 4$; difference = $4 - 4 = 0$, hence 1331 is divisible by 11.
• **Divisibility by Composite Factors**: A number is divisible by a composite number if it is divisible by each of its respective co-prime factor components (e.g., test 12 by checking divisibility by 3 and 4).
• **Efficiency in Problem Solving**: Mastering divisibility tests saves significant time during prime factorization, simplification of fractions, and finding HCF/LCM.
• **Universal Rule**: Divisibility rules apply to whole numbers of any magnitude, provided the positional place-value system is properly adhered to during digit summation or extraction.

• **Definition of Prime Factorization**: **Prime factorization** is the process of expressing a given composite number as the product of its prime factors.
• **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**: Every composite number can be factored into prime numbers in a way that is unique, except for the order in which the prime factors are written.
• **Methods of Prime Factorization**: There are two primary methods taught in Class 6 DAV mathematics to find prime factorizations: the **Factor Tree Method** and the **Short Division Method** (Division by Prime Numbers).
• **Factor Tree Method - Concept**: In a **factor tree**, a composite number is continuously split into pairs of factors until all terminal branches end in prime numbers.
• **Factor Tree - Branching Process**: For instance, 36 can split into $4 \times 9$, where 4 splits into $2 \times 2$ and 9 splits into $3 \times 3$, resulting in prime factors $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3$.
• **Uniqueness of Factor Trees**: Regardless of which factor pairs are chosen initially in a factor tree, the final set of prime factors at the leaves of the tree remains identical.
• **Short Division Method - Concept**: In the **short division method**, the composite number is divided repeatedly by the smallest possible prime number until the quotient is 1.
• **Execution of Short Division**: To find the prime factorization of 60: divide by 2 (quotient 30), divide by 2 (quotient 15), divide by 3 (quotient 5), divide by 5 (quotient 1).
• **Expressing the Result**: The prime factorization of 60 is written in exponential or product form as $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5$, or compactly as $2^2 \times 3 \times 5$.
• **Ascending Order Convention**: DAV academic standards require students to write prime factorization products with prime numbers arranged in strict ascending order from left to right.
• **Checking Prime Factorization**: A student can verify a prime factorization answer by multiplying all the prime factors together; the resulting product must equal the original composite number.
• **Role in HCF and LCM**: Prime factorization serves as the foundational engine for calculating both the Highest Common Factor and the Lowest Common Multiple efficiently.
• **Application to Large Numbers**: The short division method is especially preferred over factor trees when dealing with larger composite numbers to avoid cluttered visual diagrams.
• **Prerequisite Requirement**: Flawless execution of prime factorization requires instant recall of basic prime numbers ($2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$) and mastery of divisibility tests.
• **Distinction from Factorization**: Standard factorization lists all factors (e.g., factors of 12 are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$), whereas prime factorization isolates strictly the prime building blocks ($2 \times 2 \times 3$).

• **Definition of HCF**: The **Highest Common Factor (HCF)** of two or more given numbers is the greatest number that divides each of the given numbers exactly without leaving any remainder.
• **Alternative Terminology**: In various mathematical contexts and international curricula, HCF is also widely known as the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)** or Greatest Common Measure.
• **Concept of Common Factors**: To find the HCF, one must first list the individual factors of each number, identify all factors common to all lists, and select the largest among them.
• **Method 1 - Listing Factors**: Finding HCF of 12 and 18: $\text{Factors}(12)=\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$, $\text{Factors}(18)=\{1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18\}$. Common factors are $1, 2, 3, 6$. The highest is **6**.
• **Method 2 - Prime Factorization Method**: HCF can be found by writing the prime factorization of each number and taking the product of the **lowest powers of common prime factors**.
• **Execution of Prime Factorization Method**: For numbers 24 and 36: $24 = 2^3 \times 3^1$ and $36 = 2^2 \times 3^2$. Common prime factors are 2 and 3. Lowest powers are $2^2$ and $3^1$, giving $\text{HCF} = 4 \times 3 = 12$.
• **Method 3 - Common Division Method (Short Division)**: Dividing all given numbers simultaneously by common prime factors until no further common prime factor exists, then multiplying the divisors.
• **Properties of HCF**: The HCF of two or more numbers can never be greater than any of the given numbers; at most, it equals the smallest number in the set.
• **HCF of Co-Prime Numbers**: If two numbers are **co-prime** (like 4 and 9), their Highest Common Factor is always **1** because they share no common factor other than 1.
• **HCF of Numbers Where One is a Multiple**: If a smaller number is a factor (or divisor) of a larger number (e.g., 8 and 24), the HCF is always the **smaller number** (in this case, 8).
• **Practical Applications of HCF**: HCF is used in real life for cutting lengths of rope, cloth, or metallic bars into equal pieces of the greatest possible length without wastage.
• **Group Division Word Problems**: HCF solves problems involving distributing maximum items equally among a given number of recipients or packing items into identical maximum-capacity boxes.
• **Relationship with LCM**: For any two positive integers $a$ and $b$, the product of the numbers equals the product of their HCF and LCM: $\text{HCF}(a, b) \times \text{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b$.
• **Unitary Property**: The HCF of any set of numbers containing 1 as one of the elements is always **1**.
• **Rigorous Verification**: Students must verify that their calculated HCF divides every original number in the problem statement without leaving a remainder.

• **Definition of LCM**: The **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM)** of two or more given numbers is the smallest non-zero natural number that is a multiple of each of the given numbers.
• **Concept of Common Multiples**: To find the LCM conceptually, one lists the successive multiples of each number, identifies the multiples common to all lists, and selects the smallest value.
• **Method 1 - Listing Multiples**: Finding LCM of 4 and 6: Multiples of 4 are $4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, \dots$; Multiples of 6 are $6, 12, 18, 24, 30, \dots$. Common multiples include 12, 24, etc. The lowest is **12**.
• **Method 2 - Prime Factorization Method**: LCM can be calculated by finding the prime factorization of each number and multiplying the **highest powers of all prime factors** involved.
• **Execution of Prime Factorization LCM**: For numbers 12 and 18: $12 = 2^2 \times 3^1$ and $18 = 2^1 \times 3^2$. Highest powers are $2^2$ and $3^2$, giving $\text{LCM} = 4 \times 9 = 36$.
• **Method 3 - Common Division Method (Short Division)**: The most efficient method for Class 6 students where all numbers are arranged in a row and divided simultaneously by prime numbers until all quotients become 1.
• **Execution of Common Division Method**: To find LCM of 12, 15, and 20: divide by 2, 2, 3, and 5 sequentially in the common grid until all bottom row entries are 1; multiply all divisors to get 60.
• **Properties of LCM**: The LCM of two or more numbers is never less than any of the given numbers; at minimum, it equals the largest number in the set.
• **LCM of Co-Prime Numbers**: If two numbers are **co-prime**, their Lowest Common Multiple is simply equal to their **product** (e.g., LCM of 5 and 7 is $5 \times 7 = 35$).
• **LCM of Numbers Where One is a Multiple**: If a smaller number is a factor of a larger number (e.g., 6 and 18), the LCM is always the **larger number** (in this case, 18).
• **Practical Applications of LCM**: LCM is used in real-life problems involving events that repeat at different periodic intervals and need to synchronize or ring together (e.g., traffic lights, bell tolls, running laps).
• **Synchronized Event Word Problems**: Questions asking "When will they ring together again?" or "When will they start at the same time?" are classic indicators requiring LCM calculation.
• **Fraction Addition Foundation**: LCM is indispensable for finding the Least Common Denominator (LCD) when adding or subtracting unlike fractions in subsequent mathematical chapters.
• **Boundaries of LCM**: Unlike HCF which bounds downward toward 1, LCM scales upward, often yielding numbers significantly larger than the initial inputs.
• **Verification Check**: A calculated LCM must be fully divisible by each of the original input numbers without leaving any remainder.

• **Inter-relationship Formula**: A fundamental algebraic property connects two natural numbers with their HCF and LCM: $\text{HCF}(a, b) \times \text{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b$.
• **Derivation of Missing Values**: Using the product formula, if any three of the four quantities ($a, b, \text{HCF}, \text{LCM}$) are known, the fourth unknown quantity can be calculated easily.
• **Formula for Unknown Number**: $\text{First Number} = \frac{\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}}{\text{Second Number}}$, and vice versa.
• **Formula for HCF from Product**: $\text{HCF} = \frac{\text{Product of Two Numbers}}{\text{LCM}}$.
• **Formula for LCM from Product**: $\text{LCM} = \frac{\text{Product of Two Numbers}}{\text{HCF}}$.
• **Crucial Limitation**: The relationship $\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = a \times b$ holds **strictly true for pairs of numbers only**; it does not extend directly to three or more numbers.
• **Divisibility Rule by HCF**: Every number in a given set of numbers is always an exact multiple of their HCF, meaning the HCF always divides the LCM completely.
• **Example Application**: If the product of two numbers is 300 and their HCF is 5, their LCM is calculated as $\text{LCM} = 300 \div 5 = 60$.
• **Verification of Calculations**: Students should routinely cross-check their DAV exam answers by verifying that $\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = \text{Product of Numbers}$.
• **Co-Prime Verification**: If two numbers have an HCF of 1, their LCM is automatically equal to their direct product, which validates the co-prime property.
• **Prime Number Product Check**: For any two distinct prime numbers $p$ and $q$, their HCF is 1 and their LCM is $p \times q$, confirming the general formula ($1 \times p \times q = p \times q$).
• **Problem Solving Strategy**: Word problems testing this relationship often mask the numbers by providing their HCF, LCM, and one number; students must isolate the unknown using transposition.
• **Mathematical Reasoning**: This property emerges from the fundamental prime factor compositions of the two numbers, where shared factors make up the HCF and all unique factors make up the LCM.
• **Error Prevention**: A common student error is attempting to apply the product formula to three numbers; teachers emphasize that this is invalid for triplets.
• **Mastery Check**: Comprehensive fluency with this relationship prepares Class 6 students for advanced number theory in middle and high school mathematics.
