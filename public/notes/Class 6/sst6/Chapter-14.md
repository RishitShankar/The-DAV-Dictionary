• **Geographical Scope**: This chapter explores the historical developments in the region south of the Vindhya range, spanning the Deccan plateau and the far south of the Indian peninsula.
• **Chronological Frame**: The study covers the period broadly from the post-Mauryan era down to the early centuries of the Common Era (C.E.), highlighting regional kingdoms and cultures.
• **Natural Barriers**: The Vindhya and Satpura ranges, along with dense forests, historically acted as physical barriers separating North India from the Deccan and South India, fostering unique regional cultures.
• **River Systems**: Major river basins such as the Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery, and Tungabhadra played a foundational role in sustaining early agrarian civilizations in the south.
• **Coastal Advantage**: The long coastline of the Deccan and South India facilitated extensive maritime trade networks with Roman, Southeast Asian, and West Asian civilizations.
• **Primary Sources**: Historians reconstruct the history of this region using a combination of archaeological excavations, numismatics (coins), epigraphy (inscriptions), and ancient literature.
• **Sangam Literature**: The ancient Tamil Sangam corpus provides invaluable sociological, economic, and political data regarding early South Indian society.
• **Megalythic Culture**: Before the rise of recorded dynasties, the Deccan and South India witnessed a widespread Megalithic culture characterized by large stone burials.
• **Iron Age Transition**: Megalithic sites yield abundant iron tools—such as sickles, ploughshares, and weapons—indicating a transition to advanced iron-based agriculture and warfare.
• **Southern Dynasties**: The political landscape of the early historical period was dominated by major powers like the Satavahanas in the Deccan, and the Cheras, Cholas, and Pandyas in the far south.
• **Deccan Crossroads**: The Deccan plateau served as a crucial bridge facilitating cultural, religious, and commercial exchanges between Northern and Southern India.
• **Agriculture Foundation**: Irrigation networks, tanks, and river-fed deltas enabled surplus agricultural production, which in turn sustained urbanization and state formation.
• **Trade Emporiums**: Coastal towns and inland centers emerged as bustling trade emporiums handling spices, precious stones, textiles, and metalware.
• **Cultural Synthesis**: The interaction of indigenous Dravidian traditions with incoming Sanskritic and Buddhist influences shaped a distinct socio-religious fabric in the peninsula.

• **The Satavahana Dynasty**: The Satavahanas were the first major indigenous political power to establish a vast empire in the Deccan region after the decline of the Mauryas.
• **Alternative Name**: In historical texts and Puranas, the Satavahana dynasty is frequently referred to as the **Andhra** dynasty.
• **Core Territory**: The Satavahana dominion initially covered the modern states of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Maharashtra, later expanding into parts of Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh.
• **Founder**: The dynasty was founded by **Simuka**, who overthrew the Kanva power and established centralized rule in the western and central Deccan.
• **Greatest Ruler**: **Gautamiputra Satakarni** is celebrated as the greatest Satavahana monarch, credited with reviving the fortunes of the dynasty and defeating foreign invaders.
• **Nashik Inscription**: Information regarding Gautamiputra Satakarni’s military triumphs, particularly over the Shakas, is proudly preserved in an inscription at the Nashik cave by his mother, Gautami Balashri.
• **Matronymic Titles**: A unique feature of Satavahana rulers is that many took matronymics—names derived from their mothers, such as Gautamiputra (son of Gautami) and Vasisthiputra (son of Vasisthi).
• **Status of Women**: The use of matronymics suggests that women held a relatively high, respected social standing and influence within the royal household.
• **Trade and Economy**: Under the Satavahanas, internal and external trade flourished remarkably, bolstered by deep-water ports on both the western and eastern coasts.
• **Coinage System**: The Satavahanas issued a prolific variety of coins struck in lead, copper, silver, and *potin* (an alloy), which reflect a monetized economy.
• **Lead Coins Preference**: Their widespread use of lead coins is a distinctive feature, reflecting the local availability of lead and extensive mineral exploitation in the Deccan.
• **Guilds (Shrenis)**: Trade and craft production were largely organized into guilds (*shrenis*), which functioned autonomously, trained apprentices, and even acted as banks.
• **Maritime Trade**: Satavahana ports like **Pratishthana** (Paithan, the capital) and Tagara managed lucrative maritime trade routes connecting India with the Roman Empire.
• **Roman Trade Connection**: Quantities of Roman gold and silver coins discovered at Satavahana sites testify to a favorable balance of trade for Indian merchants.
• **Satavahana Administration**: The empire was divided into provinces known as *aharas*, which were overseen by royal officials responsible for tax collection and maintenance of law and order.
• **Rural Administration**: Local administration in villages was managed by headmen (*gulmikas*), who commanded military units and collected rural revenues.

• **Satavahana Religious Policy**: The Satavahanas were staunch patrons of Brahmanical Hinduism, performing Vedic sacrifices like *Ashvamedha* and *Rajasuya* to legitimize their imperial status.
• **Buddhism Patronage**: Despite their Vedic leanings, the Satavahanas and their wealthy subjects extended generous royal patronage to Buddhism, resulting in magnificent architectural monuments.
• **Rock-Cut Architecture**: The Deccan landscape under the Satavahanas witnessed the mass excavation of remarkable rock-cut caves (*chaityas* and *viharas*) out of solid basalt hills.
• **Karle Chaitya**: The **Karle Chaitya** in Maharashtra stands as a magnificent architectural masterpiece from this era, featuring massive pillars, vaulted ceilings, and intricate stone carvings.
• **Ajantha and Ellora Precursors**: Satavahana rock-cut architecture laid the foundational engineering and aesthetic techniques for later masterpieces like Ajanta and Ellora.
• **Amravati Stupa**: The Satavahanas and later Ikshvakus patronized the **Amravati Stupa**, famous for its exquisite white marble sculptural panels depicting the life and previous births of the Buddha.
• **Prakrit Language**: **Prakrit** served as the official administrative and court language of the Satavahanas, reflecting the spoken dialect of the common populace.
• **Sanskrit Promotion**: Towards the later phase of their rule, Sanskrit began to gain prominence in royal inscriptions and courtly literature alongside Prakrit.
• **Gatha Saptasati**: King **Hala**, a Satavahana monarch, compiled the *Gatha Saptasati*, an anthology of 700 exquisite love poems written in Maharashtri Prakrit.
• **Decline of Satavahanas**: By the early 3rd century C.E., the Satavahana Empire fragmented due to internal revolts, the rise of ambitious local governors, and pressure from the Abhiras and Ikshvakus.
• **Legacy in the Deccan**: The Satavahanas left behind an enduring legacy of political unification, economic prosperity, and architectural brilliance in the peninsular region.
• **The Deep South**: While the Satavahanas ruled the Deccan, the far south of the peninsula—spanning modern Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and parts of Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh—was dominated by three crown dynasties.
• **The Muvendar**: In Tamil literature, the three ruling dynasties—Cheras, Cholas, and Pandyas—are collectively referred to as the **Muvendar**, meaning the three crowned kings.
• **Tamilakam**: The ancient geographical region inhabited by Tamil-speaking peoples, stretching from the Tirupati hills down to Cape Comorin (Kanyakumari), was known as **Tamilakam**.
• **Shared Cultural Identity**: Despite frequent warfare among the Muvendar, Tamilakam shared a unified language (Tamil), literary tradition, and religious ethos.
• **Importance of Sangam Age**: The period of rule by the Muvendar, roughly spanning from 300 B.C.E. to 300 C.E., is famously designated in Indian history as the **Sangam Age**.

• **The Sangam Assemblies**: The term **Sangam** refers to historic assemblies or academies of Tamil poets, scholars, and bards patronized by Pandya kings in Madurai.
• **Literary Corpus**: The works approved by these legendary academies constitute the **Sangam Literature**, preserving invaluable historical data about ancient South India.
• **Poetic Themes**: Sangam poems are broadly categorized into **Aham** (internal, dealing with love and subjective human emotions) and **Puram** (external, dealing with warfare, governance, and charity).
• **Major Sangam Texts**: Prominent surviving works include the *Ettuttokai* (Eight Anthologies), *Pattupattu* (Ten Idylls), and the grammatical treatise *Tolkappiyam*.
• **Tolkappiyam Attribution**: Authored by **Tolkappiyar**, the *Tolkappiyam* is the earliest surviving Tamil literary work, setting grammatical norms and shedding light on social institutions.
• **The Chera Dynasty**: The Cheras ruled over the western portion of Tamilakam, comprising modern central and northern Kerala along with parts of western Tamil Nadu.
• **Capital and Ports**: The Cheras established their capital at **Vanchi** (modern Karur or Tiruvanchikulam) and maintained bustling ports such as **Muziris** (Kodungallur).
• **Muziris Significance**: Muziris was a legendary international emporium famed for the export of pepper, beryl, and ivory, drawing fleets of Roman merchant vessels.
• **Cheran Senguttuvan**: **Cheran Senguttuvan** was the most illustrious Chera king, celebrated in the epic *Silappadikaram* for his military prowess and patronage of arts.
• **Pattini Cult**: Cheran Senguttuvan is credited with introducing the **Pattini Cult**—the worship of Kannagi as an embodiment of chastity and wifely devotion.
• **Chera Emblem**: The royal emblem stamped on Chera coins, banners, and seals was the **Bow and Arrow**.
• **The Chola Dynasty**: The ancient Cholas ruled the fertile delta of the Cauvery river valley, known as the **Cholamandalam** (Coromandel coast), with inland centers of power.
• **Chola Capitals**: The Cholas operated from multiple historic centers, prominently including **Uraiyur** (famous for pearl fishery and cotton textiles) and later **Puhar** (Kaveripattinam).
• **Kaveripattinam**: Puhar served as a magnificent port city equipped with dockyards, lighthouses, and warehouses to handle roaring maritime trade.
• **Karikala Chola**: **Karikala** is celebrated as the greatest early Chola king, renowned for his military triumphs and monumental civil engineering projects.
• **Grand Anicut (Kallanai)**: Karikala constructed the **Kallanai** (Grand Anicut) across the river Cauvery—one of the oldest water-regulator structures in the world still in use today.

• **Chola Emblem**: The traditional royal emblem of the early Cholas was the **Tiger**, symbolizing strength, agility, and absolute sovereignty.
• **The Pandya Dynasty**: The Pandyas ruled the southernmost tip of the Indian peninsula, centered around the fertile basin of the Vaigai river.
• **Pandya Capital**: Their capital was **Madurai**, a famous center of Tamil learning, commerce, and the legendary seat of the royal Sangam assemblies.
• **Pandya Ports**: Key maritime outlets for the Pandyas included **Korkai**, which gained international renown for its highly lucrative pearl fisheries and chank shell industry.
• **Patronage of Literature**: The Pandya kings were passionate patrons of Tamil poets and scholars, financing the preservation and compilation of the vast Sangam literary corpus.
• **Pandya Emblem**: The royal emblem of the Pandyas was the **Fish** (or twin fishes), symbolizing abundance, fertility, and mastery over marine resources.
• **Minor Chieftains (Velirs)**: Alongside the Muvendar, Tamilakam was dotted with territories ruled by independent minor chieftains known as **Velirs** (e.g., Pari, Adiyan, Nannan).
• **Patronage of Bards**: The Velirs were legendary for their extreme generosity (*Kodai*) towards wandering poets, bards, and musicians, often gifting entire chariots or lands.
• **Society Structure**: Sangam society was not rigidly divided by the orthodox four-fold Vedic caste system, though occupational stratification and clan loyalties existed.
• **Aintinaikkuvai (Eco-Regions)**: Society and economy in Tamilakam were organized based on ecological zones (*tinai*), matching land types with specific subsistence patterns.
• **Kurinji (Hilly Tracts)**: The *Kurinji* eco-region was associated with hunting, gathering, and honey collection, ruled by Murugan devotees.
• **Mullai (Pastoral Lands)**: The *Mullai* region comprised pastoral grasslands where communities engaged in cattle rearing and dairy farming.
• **Marutam (Agricultural Plains)**: The fertile *Marutam* region supported intensive wet-rice cultivation and permanent sedentary village settlements.
• **Neital (Coastal Areas)**: The *Neital* region along the sea coast was inhabited by fisherfolk, salt-makers, and maritime traders.
• **Palai (Parched Deserts)**: The *Palai* region consisted of arid tracts where inhabitants relied on cattle lifting, hunting, and highway robbery for survival.
• **Social Mobility**: Individuals in early Tamil society could achieve higher social standing through martial valor, poetic excellence, or economic success.

• **Position of Women**: Women in the Sangam era enjoyed considerable freedom, actively participated in public life, and contributed brilliant poetry to the Sangam anthologies.
• **Avvaiyar**: **Avvaiyar** stands out as one of the most revered female poets of the Sangam age, whose wise verses on ethics, statecraft, and human nature remain celebrated today.
• **Hero Stones (Nadukal)**: When warriors died valiantly protecting their cattle or community, memorials called **Hero Stones** (*Nadukal* or *Virakkal*) were erected and worshipped.
• **Economic Life**: The economy of ancient South India was robust, resting on a balanced mix of agriculture, cottage industries, craftsmanship, and domestic and international trade.
• **Agricultural Produce**: Paddy (rice), sugarcane, millets, pepper, cardamom, turmeric, and cotton were cultivated extensively across the river basins and fertile valleys.
• **Textile Production**: Weaving fine cotton fabrics, muslin, and silk was a major cottage industry, with Uraiyur and Madurai serving as prime textile manufacturing hubs.
• **Metal Craftsmanship**: Artisans displayed exceptional skill in metallurgy, crafting iron tools, copper utensils, bronze statues, and gold ornaments.
• **Pearl and Gem Industry**: The Pandya coast around Korkai was globally famous for high-quality pearls, while the interior hills yielded beryl, diamonds, and precious stones.
• **Internal Commerce**: Barter system coexisted with minted currency; weekly markets (*avanam* or *angadi*) facilitated the exchange of goods between villages and towns.
• **External Trade Networks**: Tamilakam maintained thriving maritime trade relations with Southeast Asia (Suvarnabhumi), Sri Lanka, West Asia, and the Roman Empire.
• **Yavanas**: Traders and sailors from the Greco-Roman world were collectively referred to in Tamil literature as **Yavanas**, residing in dedicated coastal quarters.
• **Roman Gold Influx**: Pliny the Elder, a Roman historian, lamented the massive drain of Roman gold into India to pay for luxury goods like silk, spices, and precious stones.
• **Religious Beliefs**: The religious life of early South India featured a blend of indigenous folk deities, ancestor worship, and newly adopted Vedic and Puranic traditions.
• **Lord Murugan**: **Murugan** (also known as Subrahmanya or Skandakumaran) was the most popular primary deity of Tamilakam, associated with hills, youth, and valor.
• **Other Deities**: People also worshipped Mayon (Vishnu), Venthan (Indra), Korravai (goddess of victory/war), and sacred trees or natural springs.
• **Vedic Traditions**: Brahmanical priests gained influence in royal courts, performing Vedic sacrifices, promoting Sanskrit learning, and introducing temple worship traditions.

• **Decline of the Sangam Age**: Around the 3rd to 4th centuries C.E., the classic Sangam Age drew to a close due to political instability, foreign invasions, and economic shifts.
• **Kalabhra Interregnum**: Following the decline of the Muvendar, Tamilakam experienced a turbulent transitional phase dominated by a mysterious group known in history as the **Kalabhras**.
• **Kalabhra Rule**: Ancient literature paints the Kalabhras negatively as aggressive rulers who disrupted traditional chieftains and patronized heterodox sects like Buddhism and Jainism.
• **Land Grants**: During the Kalabhra period, extensive royal and private grants of tax-free land (*brahmadeya* and *devadana*) to religious institutions became increasingly common.
• **Rise of Pallavas**: By the late 6th century C.E., the political vacuum in the northern Tamil country was filled by the emergence of the powerful **Pallava** dynasty.
• **Rise of Pandyas**: Simultaneously, in the deep south, the Pandyas reasserted their imperial authority, overthrowing the Kalabhras and reviving their ancient capital of Madurai.
• **The Pallava Dynasty**: The Pallavas established their grand imperial capital at **Kanchipuram** (Kanchi), which transformed into a world-renowned center of learning, art, and religion.
• **Origin Debates**: Historians debate the origins of the Pallavas, with theories suggesting they were local feudatories of the Satavahanas or indigenous chieftains of Tondaimandalam.
• **Mahendravarman I**: **Mahendravarman I** was a brilliant early Pallava ruler, celebrated as a patron of architecture, playwright, musician, and poet.
• **Rock-Cut Temples**: Mahendravarman I pioneered the excavation of rock-cut cave temples across Tamil Nadu, shifting away from perishable building materials like wood and brick.
• **Matavilasa Prahasana**: Mahendravarman I authored the brilliant Sanskrit satirical farce ***Matavilasa Prahasana***, mocking hypocritical ascetics and corrupt religious practices.
• **Narasimhavarman I**: Son of Mahendravarman I, **Narasimhavarman I** (Mamallan) was the greatest Pallava emperor, renowned for his monumental military victories and rock architecture.
• **Victory over Pulakeshin II**: Narasimhavarman I avenged previous defeats by crushing the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II and capturing the Chalukyan capital of Vatapi, earning the title **Vatapikonda**.
• **Naval Expedition**: He dispatched a formidable naval fleet to Sri Lanka to successfully restore the exiled Sinhalese prince Manavarma to his rightful throne.
• **Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram)**: Narasimhavarman I founded the coastal port city of **Mamallapuram**, transforming it into a legendary stone-masonry laboratory.
• **Monolithic Rathas**: Mamallapuram features the famous **Pancha Rathas**—five monolithic shrines carved entirely out of single granite boulders, shaped like temple chariots.

• **Shore Temple**: The majestic **Shore Temple** at Mamallapuram, built by later Pallava ruler Rajasimha (Narasimhavarman II), stands directly on the seashore as a structural masterpiece.
• **Descent of the Ganges**: The magnificent bas-relief rock carving known as ***Descent of the Ganges*** (or Arjuna's Penance) at Mamallapuram depicts figures of gods, men, and animals in vibrant realism.
• **Pallava Administration**: The Pallava empire was efficiently organized into provinces (*mandalam*), districts (*nadu*), and autonomous villages (*sabha* or *ur*).
• **Village Autonomy**: Pallava inscriptions, particularly the famous **Uttaramerur Inscriptions**, provide detailed accounts of democratic village assemblies and election rules.
• **Kanchi Ghatika**: Kanchipuram hosted a prestigious center of higher education known as a **Ghatika**, where students from across India and abroad studied Vedic texts, logic, medicine, and philosophy.
• **Hieun Tsang's Account**: The famous Chinese Buddhist pilgrim **Hiuen Tsang** visited Kanchi around 640 C.E., praising its pious population, thriving Buddhist monasteries, and intellectual climate.
• **The Chalukyas of Vatapi**: Contemporary with the Pallavas in the northern Deccan were the **Chalukyas of Vatapi** (Badami in modern Karnataka), a major imperial powerhouse.
• **Dynasty Founder**: The Chalukya dynasty was founded by **Pulakeshin I** in the mid-6th century C.E., establishing a stronghold in the rugged region of Badami.
• **Pulakeshin II**: The most celebrated ruler of this dynasty was **Pulakeshin II**, whose reign marked the zenith of Chalukyan imperial expansion and military might.
• **Aihole Inscription**: Details of Pulakeshin II’s reign and his decisive victory over Harshavardhana of Kanauj on the banks of the Narmada are inscribed in the **Aihole Inscription**.
• **Aihole Inscription Author**: The Aihole Prasasti was composed by the court poet **Ravikirti** in Sanskrit using the Kannada script, showcasing high literary craftsmanship.
• **Conflict with Pallavas**: The political history of the Deccan and South India during the 7th and 8th centuries was dominated by the intense, protracted dynastic rivalry between the Chalukyas and Pallavas.
• **Chalukya Architecture**: The Chalukyas were prolific builders who perfected the Nagara and Dravida styles of temple architecture, transforming towns into stone museums.
• **Aihole Temple Cradle**: Aihole, known as the "cradle of Indian temple architecture," features experimental structural temples like the **Lad Khan Temple** and **Meguti Temple**.
• **Badami Cave Temples**: The cliff-sides of Badami contain magnificent rock-cut cave temples dedicated to Hindu deities and Jain *Tirthankaras*, adorned with exquisite relief sculptures.
• **Virupaksha Temple**: The **Virupaksha Temple** at Pattadakal, built by Queen Lokamahadevi to commemorate her husband's victory over the Pallavas, is a pinnacle of early Chalukyan art.

• **Pattadakal Monument Group**: The temple complex at **Pattadakal**, a UNESCO World Heritage site, showcases a harmonious blend of Northern (Nagara) and Southern (Dravida) architectural styles.
• **Chalukya Administration**: Chalukyan governance was decentralized, relying on strong regional feudatories, loyal military commanders, and autonomous village councils.
• **Position of Women in Chalukya Court**: Royal women in the Chalukya administration held high political administrative roles, serving as provincial governors and issuing royal land grants.
• **Decline of Vatapi Chalukyas**: The power of the Vatapi Chalukyas waned after the Pallava king Nripatunga and later Rashtrakuta chieftains launched decisive attacks, leading to the overthrow of Kirtivarman II.
• **Cultural Synthesis in the Peninsula**: The interactions between Deccan and South Indian kingdoms fostered an unprecedented synthesis of architecture, art, religion, and philosophy.
• **Bhakti Movement Roots**: The early historical and post-Sangam periods witnessed the germination of the **Bhakti Movement**, characterized by intense emotional devotion to personal deities.
• **Alvars and Nayanars**: In Tamilakam, saint-poets known as **Alvars** (devotees of Vishnu) and **Nayanars** (devotees of Shiva) popularized bhakti through vernacular Tamil hymns.
• **Popularizing Religion**: By composing hymns in simple spoken Tamil rather than Sanskrit, these saints made religious devotion accessible to all social strata, regardless of caste or gender.
• **Shankara's Advaita Vedanta**: Building upon the philosophical legacy of the Deccan and South, the great philosopher **Adi Shankaracharya** later unified Hindu thought through his doctrine of *Advaita Vedanta*.
• **Spread of Indian Culture Abroad**: The bustling ports of South India served as launching pads for the spread of Indian art, architecture, language, and religion to Southeast Asia.
• **Sanskritization Abroad**: Regions such as Champa (Vietnam), Kambuja (Cambodia), Java, Sumatra, and Bali absorbed Indian administrative models, Sanskrit scripts, and epic traditions.
• **Angkor Wat and Prambanan**: The architectural grandeur seen in monuments like **Angkor Wat** (Cambodia) and **Prambanan** (Indonesia) reflects deep civilizational links rooted in ancient South Indian maritime networks.
• **Coinage Evolution**: Numismatic evidence shows how southern kingdoms adapted Roman, Satavahana, and indigenous motifs to create distinct coin traditions featuring fish, tigers, bows, and royal busts.
• **Epigraphical Wealth**: Inscriptions etched on cave walls, copper plates, and temple pillars remain the most reliable spine of South Indian chronology and administrative history.
• **Preservation of Heritage**: The temples, rock-cut caves, and classical literature generated during these centuries continue to anchor the rich cultural identity of modern South India.
• **Chapter Conclusion**: The early history of the Deccan and South India represents a vibrant epoch of state-building, economic globalization, artistic innovation, and enduring cultural synthesis.

• **Summary of Dynastic Successions**: The historical trajectory of the south unfolded through successive and overlapping phases of regional state formation, avoiding centralized pan-Indian empires.
• **The Satavahana Legacy**: Established the first major imperial framework in the Deccan, bridging the north-south divide and promoting commerce, coinage, and rock-cut architecture.
• **The Sangam Era Peak**: The Cheras, Cholas, and Pandyas defined the classical golden age of Tamil culture, driven by maritime trade, poetry, and flourishing urban centers.
• **The Pallava Zenith**: Transformed Kanchipuram and Mamallapuram into hubs of structural engineering, Sanskrit learning, and overseas expansion into Southeast Asia.
• **The Chalukyan Brilliance**: The Vatapi Chalukyas mastered stone architecture at Aihole, Badami, and Pattadakal, while fiercely contesting regional supremacy with the Pallavas.
• **Economic Continuity**: Across all these dynasties, agriculture driven by river-basin irrigation and maritime commerce formed the twin pillars of sustained financial prosperity.
• **Social Evolution**: Society transitioned from tribal-clan structures and eco-regional livelihood patterns (*tinai*) to complex urban, agrarian, and caste-stratified frameworks.
• **Religious Pluralism**: Buddhism, Jainism, and Brahmanical Hinduism coexisted, competed, and eventually synthesized, laying the groundwork for the popular Bhakti movement.
• **Architectural Evolution**: The architectural medium evolved organically from perishable wood and brick to permanent rock-cut caves and finally to magnificent structural stone temples.
• **Literary Treasures**: The survival
