• **Historical and Foundational Context**: The circle is recognized as one of the oldest and most perfect shapes in geometry, representing symmetry, perfection, and infinite rotational symmetry without any corners or straight edges.
• **Core Definition of a Circle**: A **circle** is formally defined as the locus or set of all points in a plane that are situated at a constant, fixed distance from a single fixed point called the center.
• **The Fixed Point (Center)**: The fixed point from which all points on the boundary of the circle are equidistant is known as the **center** of the circle, usually denoted by capital letters such as $O$.
• **The Constant Distance (Radius)**: The fixed, constant distance measured from the center of the circle to any point lying on its boundary is called the **radius** of the circle.
• **Equidistance Property**: Every single point residing on the circumference of a circle maintains an exact, uniform, and invariant distance from the center, which is the foundational geometric axiom defining circular figures.
• **Plane Figure Classification**: A circle is classified as a two-dimensional closed **plane figure** that divides the plane into distinct regions: interior, exterior, and the boundary itself.
• **Distinction from Discs**: In rigorous mathematical terminology, a **circle** strictly refers only to the curved boundary line, whereas the combination of the boundary and its interior region is collectively referred to as a **circular disc**.
• **Everyday Examples**: Circles manifest ubiquitously in everyday life, visible in objects such as coins, wheels, compact discs, circular rings, buttons, and the faces of analog clocks.
• **Instrumental Construction**: In DAV Class 6 Mathematics, circles are constructed precisely using a mathematical instrument called a **pair of compasses**, ensuring accurate radii and smooth curves.
• **Pencil and Compass Setup**: The process of drawing a circle involves adjusting the sharp metal tip of the compass to a specific measurement on a ruler and rotating the pencil lead smoothly around the fixed pivot point.
• **Independence of Size**: The size of a circle is entirely determined by the length of its radius; larger radii produce larger circles, while smaller radii produce smaller, more compact circles.

• **The Boundary (Circumference)**: The curved, continuous line that forms the outer boundary of a circle is known as its **circumference**, representing the linear perimeter of the circular shape.
• **Interior Region of a Circle**: The **interior** of a circle consists of the complete set of all points that lie strictly inside the boundary line, meaning their distance from the center is strictly less than the radius.
• **Exterior Region of a Circle**: The **exterior** of a circle comprises all points located completely outside the boundary line, characterized by distances from the center that are strictly greater than the radius.
• **The Circular Region**: The union of the circle's interior and its boundary constitutes the **circular region**, forming a solid two-dimensional area on the plane.
• **Boundary as a Median**: The boundary itself acts as a precise topological separator, dividing the infinite plane into the interior domain and the exterior domain without any overlap.
• **Point Classification**: Any given point in a plane relative to a circle can be categorized into exactly one of three states: lying strictly inside, lying strictly outside, or lying precisely on the boundary.
• **Radius Segment Definition**: A **radius** is formally defined as a line segment that has one of its endpoints at the center of the circle and its other endpoint anywhere on the circumference.
• **Plural Form (Radii)**: The plural of radius is **radii**, and all individual radii drawn within the exact same circle are mathematically proven to be equal in length to one another.
• **Infinite Radii Property**: An infinite number of radii can be drawn inside any single circle, radiating outwards from the center in every conceivable direction like spokes on a wheel.
• **Defining the Circle's Scale**: The length of the radius serves as the fundamental scaling factor for calculating all other dimensions of a circle, including its diameter, circumference, and area.
• **Center-Boundary Connection**: The radius acts as the direct bridge connecting the hidden, internal reference point (the center) to the visible, external boundary (the circumference).
• **Measurement Standardization**: In geometrical problems, radii are typically measured in standard metric units such as centimeters ($cm$) or millimeters ($mm$).
• **Symmetrical Radiation**: Because all radii are equal, the center of the circle sits in a state of absolute equilibrium, perfectly balanced with respect to every point on the perimeter.

• **Definition of a Chord**: A **chord** of a circle is defined as a straight line segment whose both endpoints lie anywhere on the circumference of the circle.
• **Chord Characteristics**: Unlike a radius, a chord does not need to pass through the center of the circle; it can span across any two points on the boundary.
• **Infinite Chords**: Just like radii, an infinite number of unique chords can be drawn inside a single circle, each varying in length depending on the position of its endpoints.
• **Shortest and Longest Chords**: Chords located closer to the center are longer in length, whereas chords situated near the outer edges close to the boundary are significantly shorter.
• **Definition of the Diameter**: The **diameter** of a circle is a special chord that passes directly through the center of the circle, connecting two points on the circumference.
• **Longest Chord Property**: The diameter is mathematically proven to be the **longest possible chord** that can ever be drawn within any given circle.
• **Diameter as Double the Radius**: The length of a diameter is always exactly twice the length of the radius ($d = 2 \times r$), because it consists of two collinear radii meeting end-to-end at the center.
• **Radius as Half the Diameter**: Conversely, the length of the radius is always exactly half the length of the diameter ($r = d \div 2$), making calculations between these two dimensions straightforward.
• **Symmetry of the Diameter**: A diameter divides a circle into two identical, symmetrical halves, acting as a natural axis of reflection for the circular shape.
• **Infinite Diameters**: An infinite number of diameters can be drawn in a circle, and every single one of them intersects precisely at the center point $O$ and equals any other diameter in length.
• **Naming Conventions**: Chords and diameters are universally named using uppercase letters representing their two distinct endpoints on the boundary (e.g., chord $AB$ or diameter $PQ$).
• **Distinction Between Radius and Chord**: A radius always has one endpoint at the center and one on the boundary, whereas a chord has both endpoints on the boundary without necessarily touching the center.
• **Center Intersection Rule**: A chord becomes a diameter if and only if its straight-line path passes through the exact center point of the circle.

• **Definition of an Arc**: An **arc** is defined as any connected, unbroken portion, segment, or piece of the circumference of a circle.
• **Continuous Curvature**: An arc preserves the exact curvature of the circle from which it is taken, maintaining a constant distance from the center along its length.
• **Naming Arcs**: Arcs are typically designated by three points on the circle to avoid ambiguity, where the first and last letters represent the endpoints and the middle letter represents an intermediate point on the arc.
• **Minor Arc Definition**: A **minor arc** is an arc that measures less than half of the total circumference of the circle, representing the shorter path between two boundary points.
• **Major Arc Definition**: A **major arc** is an arc that measures more than half of the total circumference of the circle, representing the longer complementary path between the same two boundary points.
• **Semicircular Arcs**: When a diameter divides a circle into two equal parts, each resulting arc is called a **semicircle**, which represents exactly half of the total circumference.
• **Arc Addition Property**: The lengths of adjacent, non-overlapping arcs on the same circle can be added together to find the length of the combined larger arc.
• **Degrees and Angles of Arcs**: Arcs can also be measured in degrees, where the entire circle's circumference corresponds to a total central angle measure of $360^\circ$.
• **Definition of a Semicircle**: A **semicircle** is formally defined as half of a circle, formed when a diameter cuts completely across the circle from one side of the circumference to the other.
• **Semicircle Boundary**: The boundary of a semicircle consists of a straight-line diameter segment combined with a curved arc that spans $180^\circ$.
• **Area of a Semicircle**: A semicircle encloses exactly half of the total area of the parent circular region, bounded by the diameter and the curved semi-circular arc.
• **Symmetry in Semicircles**: Two identical semicircles join together along their common diameter to reconstitute a full, complete circle.
• **Real-World Semicircles**: Semicircles are frequently observed in architectural designs, such as arched doorways, windows, protractors used in geometry boxes, and running track turns.

• **Definition of Circumference (Advanced)**: The **circumference** is the linear distance one would travel by walking all the way around the outer boundary of a circle once.
• **Relationship to Perimeter**: While the term **perimeter** is used for polygons with straight sides, the special term **circumference** is exclusively reserved for the perimeter of a curved circle.
• **Visualizing Circumference**: Imagine wrapping a flexible piece of string tightly around the circular boundary of a coin, straightening out the string, and measuring its length with a ruler; that length is the circumference.
• **Proportionality to Diameter**: A fundamental geometric discovery is that the circumference of any circle is always directly proportional to its diameter.
• **Introduction to Pi ($\pi$)**: The ratio of the circumference ($C$) of any circle to its diameter ($d$) is a universal mathematical constant denoted by the Greek letter **pi ($\pi$)**, pronounced "pie".
• **Constant Value of Pi**: The mathematical constant $\pi$ is an irrational number whose exact decimal representation is infinite and non-repeating, universally approximated for calculations as $\frac{22}{7}$ or $3.14$.
• **Circumference Formula**: Derived from the ratio $\frac{C}{d} = \pi$, the formula to calculate the circumference of a circle is $C = \pi \times d$, or substituting $d = 2r$, expressed as $C = 2\pi r$.
• **Problem-Solving with Circumference**: To find the circumference when given the radius, multiply 2 by $\pi$ and by the radius length; to find it when given the diameter, simply multiply $\pi$ by the diameter.
• **Units of Circumference**: Because circumference is a linear measurement of length, its calculated units are always expressed in simple linear units like $cm$, $m$, or $km$, never in square units.
• **Historical Approximation**: Ancient mathematicians like Archimedes approximated the value of $\pi$ by inscribing and circumscribing polygons inside and outside circles, getting closer and closer to the true value.
• **Practical Application in Wheels**: Calculating circumference is crucial for real-world applications, such as determining how far a bicycle wheel travels in a single complete rotation ($1 \text{ rotation} = 1 \text{ circumference}$).
• **Scaling Effects**: If the radius of a circle is doubled, its circumference also doubles proportionally, maintaining a linear scaling relationship unlike area.
• **Precision in DAV Curriculum**: In DAV Class 6 exercises, students are typically instructed whether to use $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$ for fractions or $\pi = 3.14$ for decimal computations.

• **Definition of Interior and Exterior Regions**: Beyond the boundary line, the plane containing a circle is strictly partitioned into two fundamental topological domains: the interior and the exterior.
• **Interior Region Properties**: The **interior** contains all points whose distance from the center $O$ is strictly less than the radius length $r$ (i.e., $\text{Distance} < r$).
• **Exterior Region Properties**: The **exterior** contains all points whose distance from the center $O$ is strictly greater than the radius length $r$ (i.e., $\text{Distance} > r$).
• **Boundary Region Properties**: Points that lie directly on the circle's circumference maintain a distance from the center that is exactly equal to the radius length ($r$).
• **Concentric Circles**: Two or more circles that share the exact same center point $O$ but possess different, distinct radii are known as **concentric circles**.
• **Visualizing Concentric Circles**: Concentric circles resemble a traditional target board, ripples forming in a pond after dropping a stone, or the grooves on a vinyl record.
• **Non-Intersecting Concentric Rings**: Concentric circles can never intersect or cross paths with one another because their radii are unequal while their centers remain identical.
• **Annulus Formation**: The region trapped strictly between two concentric circles of different radii is known geometrically as an **annulus**, resembling a flat circular ring.
• **Eccentric Circles**: Circles that have completely different center points and do not share the same origin are referred to as **eccentric circles**.
• **Intersection of Circles**: Two separate circles drawn on the same plane can intersect each other at zero points, exactly one point (tangent), or exactly two distinct points.
• **Exterior Tangency**: When two circles touch each other externally at one single common point without overlapping their interiors, they are said to be **touching externally**.
• **Interior Tangency**: When one circle lies entirely inside another and touches its inner boundary at exactly one point, they are said to be **touching internally**.
• **Non-Intersecting Separate Circles**: Two circles can exist entirely outside each other without touching, where the distance between their centers is greater than the sum of their radii.

• **Definition of a Sector**: A **sector** of a circle is defined as the enclosed region bounded by two radii and the intervening arc connecting their outer endpoints.
• **Visual Analogy of a Sector**: A sector resembles a typical slice of a round pizza, a wedge of a circular pie, or a piece of a sliced birthday cake radiating from the center.
• **Center-Bound Region**: Because a sector's boundaries include two radii, the region always extends directly inward to touch the center point $O$ of the circle.
• **Minor Sector Definition**: A **minor sector** is a sector whose bounding arc is a minor arc, enclosing less than half of the total area of the circular disc.
• **Major Sector Definition**: A **major sector** is a sector whose bounding arc is a major arc, enclosing more than half of the total area of the circular disc.
• **Semicircular Sector**: When the two radii form a straight line (i.e., a diameter), the sector splits the circle into two identical semicircular sectors, each representing a $180^\circ$ wedge.
• **Definition of a Segment**: A **segment** of a circle is defined as the region bounded entirely by a chord and the arc intercepted by that chord.
• **Visual Distinction from Sectors**: Unlike a sector, a segment does not necessarily reach the center of the circle; its interior is trapped strictly between a straight chord and the curved boundary.
• **Minor Segment Definition**: A **minor segment** is the smaller region cut off by a chord that is not a diameter, containing the minor arc.
• **Major Segment Definition**: A **major segment** is the larger region cut off by the same chord, containing the major arc and spanning most of the circular area.
• **Diameter as Segment Divider**: When a chord is a diameter, it divides the circle into two equal parts, meaning the segments formed are identical semicircles rather than traditional minor and major segments.
• **Key Structural Difference**: Students must carefully distinguish between a **sector** (bounded by two radii and an arc, touching the center) and a **segment** (bounded by a chord and an arc, not necessarily touching the center).
• **Comprehensive Area Composition**: The total area of a circular disc can be completely decomposed into non-overlapping combinations of sectors, segments, and internal geometric shapes.

• **Definition of Circle Symmetry**: A circle possesses a remarkable degree of geometric symmetry, making it one of the most balanced shapes in mathematics.
• **Line Symmetry (Axis of Symmetry)**: A **line of symmetry** is a straight line that divides a geometric figure into two identical halves that match up perfectly when folded along the line.
• **Infinite Lines of Symmetry**: A circle has an **infinite number of lines of symmetry**, because every single straight line that passes through its center is a valid line of symmetry.
• **Mirror Reflection Property**: If you place a mirror along any diameter of a circle, the reflection in the mirror completes the exact shape of the full circle, proving reflectional symmetry.
• **Rotational Symmetry**: A figure has **rotational symmetry** if it can be rotated around a fixed center point by a certain angle and still look exactly the same as its original position.
• **Infinite Order of Rotational Symmetry**: A circle possesses an **infinite order of rotational symmetry**, meaning it maps onto itself after a rotation of any arbitrary angle $\theta$ around its center $O$.
• **No Translational Asymmetry**: Every point on a circle's boundary is equidistant from the center, ensuring that no matter how much you rotate the circle, its outline remains completely unchanged.
• **Center as Point of Symmetry**: The center point $O$ of a circle acts as the exact **center of symmetry** (or point of rotational balance) for the entire figure.
• **Equidistant Chord Symmetry**: Equal chords within the same circle are always located at an equal perpendicular distance from the center point.
• **Perpendicular Bisector Axiom**: A perpendicular drawn from the center of a circle to any chord will always bisect (cut exactly in half) that chord.
• **Equal Chords and Subtended Angles**: Chords of equal length within a circle subtend equal angles at the center of the circle, reflecting the deep underlying symmetry of circular geometry.
• **Symmetrical Distribution of Points**: The infinite points on the circumference are distributed with absolute uniformity around the center, leaving no gaps or irregularities in curvature.
• **DAV Problem-Solving Insight**: Recognizing the symmetrical properties of circles helps DAV Class 6 students quickly solve advanced geometric reasoning problems regarding lengths, angles, and distances.

• **Step-by-Step Compass Construction**: Constructing a circle accurately requires mastering the use of a **pair of compasses**, a sharp pencil, and a clear plastic ruler.
• **Step 1 - Radius Determination**: Open the compass legs and place the metal pointer at the $0 \text{ cm}$ mark on the ruler, adjusting the pencil lead until it rests precisely at the required radius length (e.g., $4 \text{ cm}$).
• **Step 2 - Center Marking**: Mark a sharp, clear point on your paper using a sharp pencil and label it with a capital letter, such as point $O$, to represent the center.
• **Step 3 - Anchor the Pointer**: Place the sharp metal tip of the compass firmly and securely onto the marked center point $O$, ensuring it does not slip during rotation.
• **Step 4 - Smooth Rotation**: Hold the top knob of the compass between your thumb and index finger, tilting the compass slightly in the direction of rotation, and sweep the pencil lead smoothly around to draw the complete circle.
• **Construction of Concentric Circles**: To draw concentric circles, keep the metal pointer anchored at the exact same center point $O$, but adjust the compass radius (e.g., first draw a circle of radius $3 \text{ cm}$, then adjust to $5 \text{ cm}$ and draw again).
• **Construction of Circles Through Given Points**: Understanding how to construct circles passing through specific points requires locating the perpendicular bisectors of lines joining those points to find the circumcenter.
• **Common Construction Errors**: Students must avoid loose compass hinges, slipping metal pointers, and inaccurate ruler measurements, all of which result in distorted, egg-shaped figures instead of true circles.
• **Pencil Lead Maintenance**: The pencil lead in the compass should be sharpened to a fine point and kept aligned level with the metal pointer to ensure maximum geometric precision.
• **Labeling Conventions**: Every constructed circle must have its center labeled clearly, and dashed or solid lines representing the radius or diameter should be drawn and labeled with their respective measurements.
• **Checking Work**: A properly constructed circle can be verified by measuring the distance from the center $O$ to multiple random points on the circumference using a ruler; all measurements must be identical.
• **DAV Examination Standards**: In DAV examinations, neatness, sharp pencil work, visible center points, and correct instrument handling are mandatory criteria for scoring full marks in geometrical construction questions.
• **Foundation for Higher Geometry**: Mastering the construction of circles in Class 6 lays the critical foundation for advanced constructions involving tangents, circumcircles, and incircles in higher secondary classes.
