• **Chapter Overview**: Welcome to the DAV Class 7 Kid Wiz Chapter 12 titled "दोहे" (Dohas), a profound exploration of traditional Indian didactic poetry that imparts timeless moral values, practical wisdom, and ethical life lessons.
• **Educational Objective**: This chapter is curated by the DAV Academic Board to introduce young learners to the poetic brilliance and philosophical depth of medieval Indian saint-poets like Kabir and Rahim.
• **Definition of a Doha**: A 'Doha' is a traditional Hindi poetic couplet form characterized by a specific metrical structure, precise syllable count, and a profound self-contained philosophical or moral message.
• **Role in General Knowledge**: Studying dohas in a General Knowledge curriculum bridges the gap between language literature, moral science, and cultural heritage, fostering holistic student development.
• **Cultural Significance**: Dohas serve as cultural anchors in Indian society, preserving centuries of oral wisdom, societal observations, and spiritual insights in easily memorable rhyming couplets.
• **Accessibility of Wisdom**: Despite their profound meanings, dohas are structured with simple vocabulary and rhythmic flow, making complex life philosophies accessible to school-aged children.
• **Core Themes**: The dohas featured in this curriculum primarily revolve around friendship, truthful speech, humility, the importance of time management, and the avoidance of negative company.
• **Historical Context**: The dohas studied in this chapter belong to the *Bhakti Movement* and the *Reeti Kaal* eras of Hindi literature, where poets used vernacular poetry to guide society toward righteousness.
• **Relevance to Modern Students**: The life lessons embedded in these couplets remain remarkably relevant for modern Class 7 students navigating interpersonal relationships, academic pressures, and moral dilemmas.
• **Pedagogical Approach**: The DAV methodology for teaching this chapter emphasizes memorization, literal translation, contextual understanding, and practical application of the verses in daily life.
• **Linguistic Enrichment**: Analyzing these classical couplets enhances students' vocabulary, introducing them to classical Hindi and regional dialects (like Braj Bhasha and Awadhi) used by the original poets.
• **Memorization Value**: Because of their rhyming couplet structure (*Tukbandi*), dohas act as mnemonic devices, training the human brain to retain ethical guidelines effortlessly.
• **Universal Human Values**: The chapter reinforces universal values such as compassion, respect for elders, patience during hardships, and the cultivation of inner purity over external appearances.
• **Interactive Learning**: Students are encouraged not just to read the text, but to contemplate the underlying metaphors that compare human nature to natural phenomena, animals, and everyday objects.
• **Foundational Knowledge**: This chapter sets the stage for advanced literary and philosophical studies in higher classes, building a strong foundation in Indian classical ethos and ethical reasoning.

• **Sant Kabir Das Introduction**: Sant Kabir Das is celebrated as one of India's greatest mystic poets and social reformers, whose verses fiercely challenged religious hypocrisy, caste discrimination, and superficial rituals.
• **Kabir’s Background**: Operating in the 15th century, Kabir was a weaver by trade who lived in Varanasi, blending Islamic monotheism and Hindu Vedantic philosophy into a universal path of devotion and truth.
• **Literary Style of Kabir**: Kabir’s dohas are characterized by blunt honesty, uncompromising directness, striking domestic metaphors, and a complete rejection of scholarly pretension.
• **The Concept of 'Sakhi'**: Kabir’s couplets are often referred to as *Sakhis* (witnesses), signifying that they are direct eyewitness testimonies of ultimate spiritual and social reality.
• **Kabir on Truth (Satya)**: A foundational teaching of Kabir emphasized in the chapter is that truth is the highest form of worship, and no spiritual practice can equal the virtue of absolute honesty.
• **Kabir on Speech**: Kabir famously warned about the power of the spoken word, teaching that sweet speech wins over hearts and pacifies anger, while harsh words act like sharp arrows.
• **The Metaphor of the Weaver**: Drawing from his profession, Kabir frequently used weaving metaphors—such as the loom, threads, and fabric—to explain the intricate design of human karma and destiny.
• **Kabir on Ego (Ahankar)**: A recurring theme in Kabir's dohas is the eradication of ego, teaching that true wisdom dawns only when the false sense of self-importance is completely dissolved.
• **Kabir on Company (Sangat)**: Kabir emphasized the profound influence of peer groups, asserting that proximity to wise and virtuous people elevates an individual, just as contact with sandalwood cools the surrounding air.
• **Kabir on Present Moment**: Kabir advocated for mindful living, urging individuals to accomplish good deeds immediately rather than procrastinating, using the metaphor of tomorrow's tasks.
• **Kabir's Social Critique**: Through his biting dohas, Kabir criticized blind faith, urging people to look inward for divinity rather than wandering to pilgrimage sites with a polluted mind.
• **Kabir's Simplicity**: The language used by Kabir, known as *Sadukkari* or a blend of multiple dialects, ensured that common laborers and scholars alike could grasp his revolutionary ideas.
• **Enduring Legacy**: Kabir’s dohas continue to be sung by folk artists and studied in academic institutions globally as monuments of secular humanism and moral clarity.
• **Integration in Kid Wiz**: The inclusion of Kabir’s verses in Class 7 General Knowledge broadens students' historical awareness beyond textbook dates into the realm of socio-cultural awakening.
• **Reflective Questioning**: Kabir’s dohas challenge students to introspect, prompting them to evaluate their own daily actions against the yardstick of timeless ethical standards.

• **Acharya Rahim Das Introduction**: Abdul Rahim Khan-i-Khana, popularly known simply as Rahim, was a multi-faceted genius of the Mughal era, serving as a prominent statesman, commander, linguist, and celebrated poet.
• **Rahim’s Unique Position**: Despite being a courtier in Emperor Akbar's court (one of the *Navratnas* or nine gems), Rahim's dohas reflect deep empathy for the common man and profound philosophical resignation.
• **Synthesis of Cultures**: Rahim was a Muslim by birth who possessed deep knowledge of Hindu scriptures, Sanskrit literature, and Lord Krishna's mythology, making his poetry a shining example of India's composite culture.
• **Literary Brilliance of Rahim**: Rahim's dohas are renowned for their immaculate prosody, elegant vocabulary, sophisticated similes drawn from nature, and gentle, persuasive tone of advice.
• **Rahim on Friendship (Mitrata)**: One of Rahim’s most famous dohas compares true friendship to thread, warning that once a relationship is broken by harsh words or suspicion, it can never be joined without leaving a knot.
• **Rahim on Wealth and Generosity**: Rahim taught that accumulated wealth is meaningless unless it is shared with the needy, comparing generous souls to fruit-laden trees that bow down under the weight of their bounty.
• **Rahim on Self-Reliance**: A core message in Rahim's poetry is the virtue of self-reliance, advising individuals not to depend entirely on others for their survival and success.
• **Rahim on Difficult Times**: Rahim advised patience during adversities, noting that true friends and inner strengths are tested only when financial or emotional storms arrive, just as water tests the purity of gold.
• **Rahim on Humility**: Rahim used the example of clouds that rain heavily to symbolize true achievers who remain silent and humble, contrasting them with noisy clouds that produce no rain.
• **Rahim on Small Things**: In a famous couplet, Rahim noted that even a small needle can perform tasks that a massive sword cannot, teaching students never to underestimate anyone based on their size or status.
• **Rahim’s Mastery of Metaphor**: Rahim possessed an extraordinary ability to observe everyday rural and urban Indian life—rivers, trees, oceans, needles, and clouds—and convert them into timeless philosophical axioms.
• **Rahim on Silence and Secrets**: Rahim advised individuals to keep their sorrows and personal grief hidden within their hearts, explaining that sharing them with others only invites mockery rather than genuine sympathy.
• **Rahim’s Patronage of Sanskrit**: Rahim composed poetry not just in Persian and Arabic, but mastered Braj Bhasha and Sanskrit, writing treatises on astrology and poetry alongside his famous dohas.
• **Educational Value in Kid Wiz**: Rahim's dohas train students in social etiquette, diplomatic communication, emotional intelligence, and the art of maintaining harmonious interpersonal relationships.
• **The Timelessness of Rahim**: Centuries after his passing, Rahim’s couplets remain household adages across North India, frequently quoted by elders to teach children the nuances of graceful living.

• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 1 (Kabir - On Speech)**: *“ऐसी वाणी बोलिए, मन का आप खोए। औरन को शीतल करे, आपहु शीतल होए।”* — This foundational doha teaches the art of gracious, ego-free communication that brings peace to both the speaker and the listener.
• **Deconstruction of 'Vani'**: In this couplet, 'Vani' refers to speech or verbal expression, highlighting that words are carriers of energy that can either heal wounds or inflict deep emotional trauma.
• **Eradicating Ego in Communication**: The phrase *'man ka aap khoye'* instructs individuals to shed their pride, arrogance, and defensive attitude when interacting with others to foster genuine human connection.
• **The Cooling Metaphor**: The imagery of *'shital'* (cooling/soothing) illustrates that polite and loving words lower the emotional temperature of conflicts, creating an environment of harmony and mutual respect.
• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 2 (Kabir - On Action and Time)**: *“काल करे सो आज कर, आज करे सो अब। पल में प्रलय होएगी, बहुरी करेगा कब?”* — This doha is a timeless anthem for time management, urgency, and the elimination of procrastination.
• **Urgency of Good Deeds**: Kabir cautions that life is fleeting and unpredictable, symbolized by *'pralaya'* (sudden catastrophe or dissolution), urging students never to postpone virtuous actions or academic responsibilities.
• **Micro-Scheduling Wisdom**: The progression from *'kal'* (tomorrow) to *'aaj'* (today) to *'ab'* (now) demonstrates how procrastination steals potential productivity, emphasizing the power of the present moment.
• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 3 (Kabir - On Friendship and Company)**: *“जैसी संगत बैठिए, तैसे फल दीन। ज्यों चंदन के वास रस, नीम न भयौ सुमीन।”* — This couplet delves deep into environmental determinism and the psychological impact of peer groups.
• **The Sandalwood Metaphor**: Sandalwood trees (*'chandan'*) are legendary for their cooling fragrance; Kabir notes that even nearby trees absorb some of this aromatic quality, illustrating the uplifting effect of noble company.
• **The Limits of Association**: The second line notes that despite growing near sandalwood, certain trees like the bitter Neem retain their fundamental nature, showing that while company influences us, innate character also matters.
• **Peer Pressure Management**: For Class 7 students, this doha serves as a vital psychological guide for choosing friends wisely, avoiding toxic peer pressure, and seeking environments that promote personal growth.
• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 4 (Rahim - On Broken Trust)**: *“रहिमन धागा प्रेम का, मत तोड़ो चटकाय। टूटे फिर ना जुरे, गांठी पड़ि जाय।”* — Rahim uses the delicate metaphor of a cotton thread to explain the fragility of human relationships and trust.
• **Irreversibility of Damage**: Just as snapping a thread leaves an ugly knot even if it is tied back together, hurtful words and betrayals leave permanent emotional scars on friendships that can never be fully erased.
• **Preventive Care in Relationships**: This doha teaches students to handle interpersonal bonds with extreme care, patience, and sensitivity, preventing minor disagreements from escalating into permanent rifts.
• **Cross-Cultural Relevance**: The universal imagery of thread and knots makes this doha instantly relatable to students across diverse cultural, linguistic, and economic backgrounds.

• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 5 (Rahim - On Generosity and Wealth)**: *“तरुवर फल नहिं खात है, सरवर प पियहि न पान। कहि रहिमन परकाज हित, संपत्ति संचहि सुजान।”* — This couplet establishes altruism and philanthropy as the ultimate markers of a noble, successful life.
• **The Tree and River Metaphors**: Rahim points out that fruit trees do not consume their own fruit, and rivers do not drink their own water; they exist entirely for the sustenance of others.
• **Definition of 'Sujan'**: A *'sujan'* refers to a wise, enlightened person who accumulates wealth, knowledge, and resources not for selfish hoarding, but for the welfare of society (*'parkaj hit'*).
• **The Philosophy of Giving**: This verse instills the value of charity and community service in young minds, teaching that true fulfillment comes from contribution rather than accumulation.
• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 6 (Rahim - On Small Things)**: *“मात न दीजै कोऊ को, छोटन कहि उपजीय। जो काम सुई करे, तरवारि क्या काम लेय?”* — A powerful lesson against arrogance and the systemic underestimation of marginalized or smaller entities.
• **The Needle vs. Sword Metaphor**: Rahim asks a rhetorical question: where a tiny sewing needle is required to stitch torn fabric, a mighty, intimidating sword is completely useless.
• **Appreciating Diversity of Skills**: This doha teaches Class 7 students that every individual—regardless of physical size, economic status, or academic rank—possesses unique skills essential for societal functioning.
• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 7 (Rahim - On Adversity and Testing Friends)**: *“विपति भए नहिं कोउ धन, सो मित्र दुखि दुख होय। रहिमन दुखन में सुखी, देखें खोट्टा कोय।”* — An insightful psychological guide on identifying fair-weather friends during times of crisis.
• **The Litmus Test of Hardship**: Rahim explains that genuine friends experience physical and emotional pain when they see their companions suffering, while false friends remain indifferent or happy.
• **Emotional Intelligence**: This verse trains students to look beyond superficial popularity and flattery, cultivating discernment to surround themselves with loyal, empathetic companions.
• **Detailed Analysis of Doha 8 (Rahim - On Hiding Grief)**: *“रहिमन मन की व्यथा, मन ही राखों गोय। सुनी इठलैहैं लोग सब, बांट न लैहैं कोइ।”* — A pragmatic piece of advice on emotional self-regulation and vulnerability management.
• **The Danger of Over-Sharing**: Rahim cautions that when people hear about our personal sorrows, they often gossip or mock (*'ithalhaihn'*) rather than offering genuine help, so it is wiser to endure with quiet grace.
• **Balanced Emotional Expression**: While encouraging emotional resilience, this doha teaches students the maturity of distinguishing between trusted mentors and casual acquaintances when seeking support.
• **Synthesis of Moral Teachings**: Together, these detailed dohas provide a comprehensive manual for navigating the complexities of human emotion, social ethics, and personal integrity.
• **Pedagogical Reinforcement**: DAV instructors use these verses to spark classroom discussions, role-play scenarios, and creative writing exercises where students apply these principles to modern dilemmas.

• **Linguistic Structure of Dohas**: A standard Hindi doha is composed of two lines, divided into four segments called *Charans* (feet), following a precise quantitative or qualitative syllabic meter.
• **Syllabic Breakdown (Matras)**: Each doha line consists of 24 matras (beats), subdivided into an 13:11 rhythmic pause pattern known as *Yati* (cesura) between the first and second halves of the line.
• **Rhyme Scheme (Tuk)**: The concluding words of the second and fourth feet rhyme with each other, creating a musical cadence (*Tukbandi*) that facilitates effortless auditory retention.
• **Role of Braj Bhasha**: Most medieval dohas were composed in Braj Bhasha, Awadhi, or Khari Boli, serving as the linguistic bridge between Sanskrit classics and modern Hindi literature.
• **Figures of Speech (Alankar)**: Dohas make extensive use of poetic devices such as * दृष्टान्त (Drishtanta - Exemplification)*, * उपमा (Upama - Simile)*, and * रूपक (Rupak - Metaphor)* to convey abstract moral concepts.
• **The Art of Compression**: The genius of a doha lies in its micro-poetry format—expressing massive philosophical truths, warnings, and psychological insights within a span of just 24 to 30 words.
• **Oral Tradition Transmission**: Before the widespread availability of printing presses, dohas were transmitted orally across generations of bards, saints, village elders, and school teachers.
• **Ethical Metaphors**: Natural elements—water, wind, fire, trees, clouds, rivers, and mountains—are regularly employed as metaphors to explain human psychology, morality, and social dynamics.
• **Didactic vs. Lyrical**: While ghazals and bhajans focus heavily on musical rendering, dohas focus primarily on didacticism (teaching) wrapped in a memorable rhythmic package.
• **Memorization Techniques in DAV Schools**: Students are taught to chant dohas with proper intonation (*Raga*), stress, and pause, ensuring that the rhythm anchors the meaning permanently in their memory.
• **Comparative Poetics**: Comparing Kabir's direct, fiery style with Rahim's gentle, courtly style helps students appreciate the diversity of thought and expression within the same poetic format.
• **Grammatical Analysis**: Teachers utilize doha texts to teach students about classical word formations, prefixes, suffixes, silent letters, and archaic grammatical forms in Hindi.
• **Cultural Continuity**: Analyzing how medieval poets used animals and nature to teach ethics connects modern urban students with traditional agrarian wisdom and ecological consciousness.
• **Vocabulary Enrichment**: Students maintain a glossary of classical terms found in dohas—such as *Vani, Man, Shital, Kal, Pralaya, Sugan, Parkaj, Hit, Vyatha, and Goy*—expanding their linguistic repertoire.
• **Holistic Assessment**: DAV assessments for this chapter test not only rote memorization of the verses, but also the student's ability to explain the contextual meaning in contemporary language.

• **Classroom Pedagogy for Chapter 12**: DAV public schools employ a multi-sensory teaching methodology for "दोहे", combining recitation drills, visual aids, dramatic enactment, and philosophical discussions.
• **Recitation Drills (*Swar-Vachan*)**: Teachers lead choral recitation sessions where the entire class chants the dohas with correct rhythm (*Laya*), cadence, and pitch to instill auditory familiarity.
• **Literal Translation (*Shabdarth*)**: Instructors break down each couplet word-by-word, ensuring students understand archaic vocabulary before moving on to the broader contextual meaning (*Bhavarth*).
• **Contextual Explanation (*Bhavarth-Spashtikaran*)**: Teachers narrate historical anecdotes, parables, and real-life scenarios to illustrate how a 15th-century doha applies to 21st-century student life.
• **Role-Play and Dramatization**: Students frequently enact situations described in the dohas—such as choosing between honest and dishonest friends, or practicing polite speech—to internalize the lessons.
• **Art Integration Projects**: DAV art departments collaborate with Kid Wiz lessons, having students create illustrated calligraphy scrolls featuring their favorite dohas alongside nature sketches.
• **Notebook Maintenance**: Students maintain structured notes featuring the doha text, word meanings, thematic summaries, and moral takeaway points for systematic exam preparation.
• **Peer Teaching Activities**: Advanced students are paired with peers to conduct reading practice and vocabulary review sessions, reinforcing collaborative learning within the classroom.
• **Quizzes and Oral Testing**: Regular rapid-fire quizzes on doha meanings, poet identifications, and vocabulary definitions keep students engaged and responsive during revision cycles.
• **Creative Writing Extensions**: Students are encouraged to write short paragraphs or modern diary entries explaining how a specific doha helped them resolve a personal conflict or academic hurdle.
• **Moral Science Integration**: The ethical tenets of the chapter are frequently referenced during school assembly talks, morning thought-sharing sessions, and counseling hours.
• **Parent-Teacher Engagement**: Parents are encouraged to discuss the dohas at home, sharing regional proverbs or family anecdotes that align with Kabir's and Rahim's teachings.
• **Remしまいました Support**: For students struggling with classical vocabulary, remedial worksheets with simplified Hindi glosses and visual clues are provided by DAV instructors.
• **Digital Learning Aids**: Smartboard modules featuring animated depictions of the weaver, the sandalwood tree, the thread, and the river enrich the classroom learning experience.
• **Continuous Evaluation**: Rather than relying solely on end-term exams, teachers assess students continuously through participation, recitation fluency, and conceptual clarity.

• **Key Glossary Term 1 - Doha (दोहा)**: A traditional two-line Hindi poetic couplet characterized by a specific metrical structure (24 matras) and a profound moral or philosophical message.
• **Key Glossary Term 2 - Kabir Das (कबीर दास)**: A 15th-century mystic poet, weaver, and social reformer whose verses challenged religious hypocrisy and championed universal truth and love.
• **Key Glossary Term 3 - Rahim Das (रहीम दास)**: A 16th-century Mughal statesman, court poet, and philanthropist renowned for his elegant dohas on friendship, generosity, and human nature.
• **Key Glossary Term 4 - Vani (वाणी)**: Speech, spoken words, or verbal expression; emphasized in Kabir's poetry as a powerful tool that must be used gently and thoughtfully.
• **Key Glossary Term 5 - Ahankar (अहंकार)**: Pride, arrogance, or ego; identified by saint-poets as the primary internal obstacle to spiritual growth and harmonious human relationships.
• **Key Glossary Term 6 - Shital (शीतल)**: Cooling, soothing, or calming; used metaphorically to describe the peaceful impact of sweet speech and virtuous company.
• **Key Glossary Term 7 - Pralaya (प्रलय)**: Sudden catastrophe, dissolution, or end of the world; used metaphorically by Kabir to emphasize the unpredictable and fleeting nature of human life.
• **Key Glossary Term 8 - Sangat (संगत)**: Company, peer group, or social association; highlighted as a decisive factor in shaping an individual's character and moral development.
• **Key Glossary Term 9 - Chandan (चंदन)**: Sandalwood tree; a classic metaphor representing noble, fragrant souls whose positive influence elevates everyone in their vicinity.
• **Key Glossary Term 10 - Sujan (सुजान)**: A wise, enlightened, and benevolent person who utilizes their wealth and knowledge for the betterment of society.
• **Key Glossary Term 11 - Parkaj Hit (परकाज हित)**: Altruism, charity, or working selflessly for the welfare and service of others, exemplified by trees and rivers.
• **Key Glossary Term 12 - Tarvari (तरवारि)**: A sword; used in Rahim's doha to contrast intimidating physical might with the subtle, indispensable utility of a tiny sewing needle.
• **Key Glossary Term 13 - Vyatha (व्यथा)**: Internal grief, sorrow, or emotional pain; advised by Rahim to be kept private rather than broadcasted to unsympathetic crowds.
• **Key Glossary Term 14 - Matra (मात्रा)**: A linguistic unit of syllabic weight and rhythmic duration used in Indian classical poetry to measure metrical feet.
• **Key Glossary Term 15 - Bhavarth (भावार्थ)**: The core contextual meaning, underlying theme, or philosophical essence of a poetic verse, moving beyond literal translation.
