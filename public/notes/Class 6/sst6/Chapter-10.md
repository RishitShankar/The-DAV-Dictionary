• **Subject and Grade Context:** This comprehensive study note is prepared for Class 6 students studying "We and Our World: Social Science," specifically focusing on Chapter 10, titled "The Earliest Societies," under the DAV Dictionary academic framework.
• **Core Chapter Theme:** The chapter explores the evolutionary journey of human beings from being mere food gatherers and wanderers to becoming food producers, settlers, and the architects of the earliest human civilizations.
• **Time Span of Early Humans:** The history of early human societies spans millions of years, beginning with the emergence of hominids and extending through the Stone Age up to the dawn of the Bronze Age civilizations.
• **Significance of Archaeology:** Since early human societies did not leave behind written records, our understanding of their lifestyles, tools, and culture relies entirely on archaeological excavations and discoveries.
• **Role of Artifacts:** Archaeologists unearth stone tools, pottery, bones, charcoal, and cave paintings, which act as primary clues to reconstruct the daily lives of our earliest ancestors.
• **The Stone Age Division:** Prehistoric human history is primarily categorized into different phases based on the type of stone tools used, collectively known as the Stone Age.
• **Three Main Phases:** The Stone Age is broadly divided into the Paleolithic (Old Stone Age), Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age), and Neolithic (New Stone Age) periods.
• **Geological Timeline Context:** These early human milestones occurred during the Pleistocene and Holocene geological epochs, which witnessed major climatic shifts across the Earth.
• **Survival Challenges:** The earliest humans faced severe environmental challenges, including extreme weather, dense forests, and fierce competition with large carnivorous predators for survival and food.
• **Nomadic Lifestyle:** Early humans lived a nomadic lifestyle, moving constantly from place to place in small groups or bands in search of edible plants, animals, and reliable water sources.
• **Lack of Permanent Shelter:** Because of their constant movement, these groups did not build permanent houses, instead taking natural refuge in caves, under rock overhangs, or beneath tree branches.
• **Social Organization:** Groups were typically small kinship-based bands where cooperation was essential for hunting large animals, defending against predators, and sharing gathered food resources.
• **Division of Labor:** Within these early bands, a primitive division of labor existed, where men typically engaged in hunting while women and children gathered fruits, nuts, roots, and small insects.
• **Communication Skills:** Early humans communicated initially through gestures, body language, and primitive vocalizations, which gradually evolved into more complex spoken languages as their brains developed.
• **Adaptation to Nature:** Survival depended heavily on close observation of nature, including animal migration patterns, seasonal plant cycles, and weather indicators.

• **Paleolithic Period Definition:** The **Paleolithic Period** or Old Stone Age derives its name from Greek words 'palaios' (old) and 'lithos' (stone), spanning roughly from 2 million years ago to about 10,000 BCE.
• **Dominant Climate:** The Paleolithic era largely coincided with the Ice Age, during which large parts of the Earth's surface were covered under thick sheets of ice and glaciers.
• **Core Subsistence Strategy:** Paleolithic humans were strictly **hunter-gatherers**, meaning they did not produce their own food but entirely depended on nature's bounty for survival.
• **Nature of Paleolithic Tools:** Tools from this period were extremely crude, heavy, unpolished, and made by chipping large stones against each other to create sharp edges or blunt ends.
• **Core and Flake Technique:** Early toolmakers struck a large stone core with another stone to knock off sharp flakes, which were then used for various cutting and scraping tasks.
• **Hand Axes and Choppers:** Common Paleolithic stone implements included hand axes, cleavers, choppers, and scrapers, used for skinning animals, cutting meat, and breaking bones.
• **Material Variety:** Besides stone, Paleolithic humans also utilized bones, antlers, and heavy pieces of wood to fashion rudimentary spears, digging sticks, and clubs.
• **Discovery of Fire:** One of the greatest milestones of the Paleolithic Age was the discovery and control of fire, achieved through friction by striking stones together like flint and pyrite.
• **Uses of Fire:** Fire provided multiple life-saving benefits: it offered warmth during freezing Ice Age winters, kept dangerous wild predators away from camp sites, and illuminated dark caves.
• **Culinary Revolution:** Fire allowed early humans to cook raw meat, making it easier to chew, digest, and more palatable, while also killing harmful parasites and bacteria.
• **Paleolithic Shelter Types:** While caves provided natural protection, communities also constructed windbreaks and temporary branch-and-leaf huts when traveling across open plains.
• **Clothing Materials:** To protect themselves from biting cold, Paleolithic humans wrapped animal skins, furs, and large leaves around their bodies, fastening them with thin leather thongs.
• **Artistic Expression:** Late Paleolithic humans created stunning art, including cave paintings and small carved figurines, found in locations like Bhimbetka in India and Lascaux in France.
• **Subject Matter of Art:** Cave paintings frequently depicted large wild animals such as bison, mammoths, deer, and hunting scenes, often imbued with ritualistic or spiritual significance.
• **Spiritual Beliefs:** The presence of burial practices, grave goods, and ritualistic art indicates that Paleolithic humans possessed early religious beliefs, reverence for nature, and concepts of an afterlife.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Transition Period:** The **Mesolithic Period** or Middle Stone Age represents the transitional phase between the Paleolithic and Neolithic eras, roughly spanning from 10,000 BCE to 8,000 BCE.
• **Climatic Shift:** The end of the Ice Age brought a major climatic warming trend, causing glaciers to melt, sea levels to rise, and vast forests and grasslands to expand globally.
• **Faunal Changes:** As the climate became warmer and drier, large Ice Age mammals like woolly mammoths and saber-toothed cats became extinct, replaced by smaller, faster animals like deer, boar, and rabbits.
• **Microliths:** The defining technological innovation of the Mesolithic Age was the creation of **microliths**, which were extremely small, sharp stone blades typically measuring between one to five centimeters in length.
• **Composite Tools:** Mesolithic humans ingeniously fixed these tiny microliths into handles made of wood, bone, or antler using plant resin and animal sinew to create composite tools like sickles, arrows, and harpoons.
• **Advanced Weaponry:** The invention of the bow and arrow during the Mesolithic period revolutionized hunting by allowing humans to kill fast-moving small game from a safe distance with greater accuracy.
• **Domestication Begins:** The Mesolithic era marked the earliest beginnings of the **domestication of animals**, with the dog being the first animal to be successfully tamed for companionship, guarding, and hunting assistance.
• **Gathering Intensification:** Due to the abundance of new plant species in forests and grasslands, gathering became more specialized, with communities systematically harvesting wild grains, grasses, and tubers.
• **Settlement Tendency:** Improved food availability and efficient hunting tools encouraged Mesolithic groups to stay longer in favorable locations, leading to semi-permanent settlements near rivers and lakes.
• **Fishing Economies:** The expansion of rivers and lakes led to the development of sophisticated fishing techniques, utilizing bone hooks, harpoons, and woven plant-fiber fishnets.
• **Basketry and Pottery:** The need to store gathered seeds and berries led to the earliest forms of basketry, sometimes lined with clay to hold water, paving the way for later pottery inventions.
• **Burial Custom Evolution:** Mesolithic burials became more elaborate, often including red ochre sprinkled over the body and placing tools, weapons, and ornaments in the grave as offerings.
• **Regional Diversity:** Different Mesolithic cultures developed unique tool-making traditions tailored to their specific regional environments, whether in dense forests, river valleys, or coastal areas.
• **Bhimbetka Rock Shelters:** Famous Indian sites like Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh contain extensive evidence spanning from the Paleolithic through the Mesolithic period, showcasing continuous human habitation and evolving art styles.
• **Foundation for Agriculture:** The environmental adaptations, semi-sedentary lifestyles, and close observation of plant life during the Mesolithic period directly laid the groundwork for the Agricultural Revolution.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Neolithic Period Definition:** The **Neolithic Period** or New Stone Age marks the final stage of the Stone Age, beginning around 8,000 BCE and lasting until the introduction of metal tools.
• **The Neolithic Revolution:** Termed by historians as the "Neolithic Revolution," this era witnessed a radical transformation in human history from food gathering to **food production**.
• **Invention of Agriculture:** Humans discovered that seeds dropped on fertile soil sprouted and grew into plants, leading to the deliberate cultivation of crops like wheat, barley, rice, and millet.
• **Domestication of Animals:** Neolithic communities tamed sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs, raising them for milk, meat, wool, and labor, which provided a reliable, continuous supply of food.
• **Shift to Sedentary Life:** Agriculture and animal husbandry required humans to stay in one place continuously to tend fields, water crops, and protect livestock, ending the nomadic lifestyle.
• **Birth of Permanent Villages:** The need for permanent settlement near agricultural fields and water sources led to the construction of the world's first permanent villages and farming communities.
• **Architecture and Housing:** Neolithic houses were sturdier, built using mud-bricks, stone, wattle, and daub, often featuring thatched roofs and subterranean storage pits for surplus grain.
• **Technological Advancements:** Stone tools during the Neolithic period were no longer just chipped; they were finely ground, polished, and sharpened to create smooth, durable axes, adzes, and chisels for tree-felling and carpentry.
• **Invention of Pottery:** The production of pottery revolutionized storage and cooking; pots were shaped by hand and fired in kilns, allowing humans to store surplus grains, liquids, and cook stews efficiently.
• **The Spinning Wheel and Weaving:** Neolithic people invented spindles and looms to weave natural fibers like flax, hemp, and wool into cloth, replacing animal skins with comfortable textile clothing.
• **Concept of Property:** With permanent homes, cultivated fields, and stored grain surpluses, the concepts of private property, land ownership, and inheritance began to emerge within society.
• **Social Stratification:** As communities grew larger and some families accumulated more surplus wealth than others, early forms of social division and hierarchy started to develop.
• **Role of Community Leadership:** Complex agricultural tasks, irrigation, and village defense required community organization, leading to the emergence of village elders or chieftains to resolve disputes and guide decisions.
• **Religious Beliefs and Rituals:** Neolithic religion focused heavily on fertility rites, worship of earth goddesses, nature spirits, and the sun, reflecting their deep dependence on agriculture, weather, and harvests.
• **Megalithic Monuments:** Many Neolithic and Chalcolithic communities erected **megaliths** (large stone structures) as burial sites, memorials, or astronomical markers, showing engineering skill and communal effort.

• **Case Study: Mehrgarh:** **Mehrgarh** is one of the most important early Neolithic archaeological sites in South Asia, located in the fertile Kachi plain of Balochistan, near the Bolan Pass in present-day Pakistan.
• **Chronology of Mehrgarh:** Excavations reveal that Mehrgarh was inhabited from around 7000 BCE, providing a clear archaeological bridge between nomadic pastoralism and settled village agriculture.
• **Early Agriculture at Mehrgarh:** The earliest inhabitants of Mehrgarh cultivated barley and wheat, and raised sheep, goats, and cattle, transitioning from hunting wild animals to controlled animal husbandry.
• **Evolution of Housing:** Early levels at Mehrgarh featured simple mud-brick houses divided into small compartments, which were likely used for grain storage rather than living quarters.
• **Gradual Architectural Sophistication:** Over centuries, Mehrgarh architecture evolved to include multi-roomed rectangular mud-brick houses, demonstrating advanced construction techniques and urban planning concepts.
• **Burial Practices at Mehrgarh:** Graves at Mehrgarh often contained grave goods such as stone and bone tools, beads, baskets, and sometimes animal sacrifices, indicating beliefs in an afterlife and social differentiation.
• **Craft Specialization:** Mehrgarh residents excelled in various crafts, including flint-knapping, tanning, bead-making from semi-precious stones like lapis lazuli and turquoise, and early metallurgy.
• **Invention of Terracotta Figurines:** Artisans at Mehrgarh created intricate terracotta figurines of humans and animals, particularly female figures adorned with elaborate hairstyles and jewelry, pointing to fertility worship.
• **Evidence of Early Dentistry:** Astonishing archaeological findings at Mehrgarh include human teeth with drill holes dating back over 9,000 years, providing evidence of the world's earliest proto-dentistry.
• **Trade and Exchange Networks:** Mehrgarh was an active trading hub, importing raw materials like turquoise from Central Asia and shells from the coast, and exporting finished crafts and agricultural goods.
• **Foundation of Indus Civilization:** The agricultural success, technological innovations, and social organization developed at Mehrgarh directly paved the way for the emergence of the mature Indus Valley Civilization.
• **Significance of Site Preservation:** Systematic excavations by French archaeologists Jean-François Jarrige and Catherine Jarrige made Mehrgarh a benchmark site for understanding early Neolithic life in the Indian subcontinent.
• **Environmental Setting:** Mehrgarh’s location near the seasonal Bolan River provided alluvial soil ideal for early flood-retreat agriculture, minimizing the need for complex irrigation systems.
• **Dietary Habits:** Analysis of plant and animal remains at Mehrgarh indicates a rich diet consisting of cultivated cereals, dairy products, and meat from both domesticated and hunted animals.
• **Transition to Chalcolithic:** The upper layers of Mehrgarh show the introduction of copper tools, marking the site's seamless transition from the Neolithic age into the Chalcolithic period.

• **Case Study: Burzahom:** **Burzahom** is a prominent Neolithic archaeological site located in the Kashmir Valley of India, providing unique insights into the adaptation of early humans to cold, mountainous environments.
• **Meaning of Name:** The local term "Burzahom" translates to "place of the birch tree," indicating the ancient vegetation of the region during the Neolithic era.
• **Unique Pit Dwellings:** The most distinctive feature of Burzahom is the presence of **pit dwellings**, which were circular or oval pits dug into the earth, lined with plaster, and covered with thatched roofs supported by wooden poles.
• **Purpose of Pit Dwellings:** These underground and semi-underground pit dwellings protected early inhabitants from the harsh, freezing winds and heavy snowfall characteristic of winters in the Kashmir Valley.
• **Access to Pits:** The pit dwellings featured stone or wooden steps leading down into the interior, with post-holes surrounding the rim suggesting superstructure additions for warmth and shelter.
• **Later Ground-Level Housing:** In subsequent phases of occupation, Burzahom residents abandoned pit dwellings and constructed rectangular mud-brick and timber houses directly on the ground surface.
• **Stone and Bone Tool Industry:** Burzahom is exceptionally rich in **bone tools**, including polished harpoons, needles, awls, and arrowheads, crafted from animal antlers and bones to suit cold-weather survival needs.
• **Polished Stone Implements:** Excavations have also yielded finely polished stone axes, pestles, and harvesters, indicating a well-developed tool-making technology among the inhabitants.
• **Subsistence Economy:** Burzahom settlers practiced a mixed economy of agriculture (growing wheat, barley, and lentils), animal husbandry, hunting, and extensive fishing in nearby lakes.
• **Unique Burial Customs:** Burzahom is famous for its unique animal burials, where domestic dogs were buried alongside their human masters in graves, highlighting a deep bond between humans and domesticated animals.
• **Human Burials:** Human graves at Burzahom were often placed within the residential area beneath house floors, sometimes featuring red ochre smeared over skeletal remains.
• **Pottery Tradition:** The site yielded handmade coarse grey, black, and red pottery, sometimes decorated with incised geometric patterns or impressions of woven mats at the base.
• **Rock Art and Carvings:** Engravings on large stone slabs found at Burzahom depict hunting scenes, human figures, dogs, and celestial objects like the sun and stars, reflecting their worldview.
• **External Cultural Links:** Artifacts found at Burzahom show cultural connections and trade links with contemporary Neolithic cultures in Central Asia and the Indus plains.
• **Chronological Span:** The occupation at Burzahom spans from the Neolithic period down into the Megalithic period, documenting a long, continuous sequence of cultural development in northern India.

• **Dawn of Metallurgy:** As human societies advanced past the Neolithic period, they discovered how to extract and shape metals from ores, marking the end of the Stone Age and the beginning of the metal ages.
• **First Metal Used:** **Copper** was the very first metal to be discovered and used by humans because it could be found in a relatively pure state on the earth's surface and was easy to shape.
• **Chalcolithic Period Definition:** The **Chalcolithic Period** (or Copper-Stone Age) is the transitional era where humans continued to use stone tools while simultaneously beginning to manufacture and use copper tools.
• **Name Etymology:** The word 'Chalcolithic' comes from Greek words 'chalkos' (copper) and 'lithos' (stone), perfectly describing this technological hybrid period of human history.
• **Limitations of Copper:** While copper was useful, it was a relatively soft metal, meaning copper tools easily bent, lost their sharp edges quickly, and could not replace heavy-duty stone implements entirely.
• **Discovery of Bronze:** To overcome copper's softness, ancient metallurgists discovered that melting copper and mixing it with a small amount of **tin** created a much harder, tougher alloy called **bronze**.
• **Bronze Age Revolution:** The invention of bronze ushered in the **Bronze Age**, enabling the creation of extremely sharp, durable weapons, armor, heavy agricultural tools, and intricate ornaments.
• **Impact on Craft Specialization:** Metallurgy required specialized skills, leading to the emergence of full-time **metal smiths** who separated themselves from general farming and gathering tasks.
• **Expansion of Trade:** Because copper and tin ores were rarely found in the same place, the metal age sparked long-distance trade networks, merchants traveling vast distances to secure raw metal ores.
• **Rise of Urbanization:** The combination of surplus agricultural production, metal technology, and complex trade networks laid the socioeconomic foundation for the rise of the world's first true cities and civilizations.
• **The Wheel Innovation:** Another monumental technological leap during this era was the invention of the **potter's wheel** and transport wheels, speeding up pottery production and revolutionizing travel and cart transport.
• **Invention of the Plow:** The introduction of the metal-tipped plow allowed farmers to till deeper, harder soils, significantly increasing agricultural yields and supporting larger populations.
• **Monumental Architecture:** Societies of the Bronze Age pooled vast labor resources to construct massive public works, including irrigation canals, city walls, palaces, and monumental temples.
• **Writing Systems:** The increasing complexity of trade, taxation, and administrative record-keeping during the Bronze Age led directly to the invention of the earliest **writing systems**, such as cuneiform and hieroglyphics.
• **Transition to History:** The invention of writing marked the boundary between **prehistory** (the period before written records) and **history** (the period documented by written texts).

• **Role of Archaeology in Prehistory:** Because early societies left no written accounts, our comprehensive understanding of their lives depends entirely on the painstaking work of **archaeologists** and anthropologists.
• **Excavation Methodology:** Archaeologists systematically excavate ancient sites layer by layer, carefully recording the exact depth and position of every artifact to maintain a relative chronological timeline.
• **Stratigraphy Principle:** The principle of **stratigraphy** dictates that older layers of earth and artifacts lie beneath newer, more recent layers, helping scientists establish the chronological sequence of human cultures.
• **Dating Techniques: Carbon-14:** **Radiocarbon dating** (Carbon-14 dating) measures the radioactive decay of carbon isotopes in organic remains like bones, wood, and charcoal to calculate their exact age in years.
• **Dating Techniques: Potassium-Argon:** For extremely ancient volcanic rock layers associated with early hominid fossils, scientists use **potassium-argon dating** to determine geological timeframes spanning millions of years.
• **Dating Techniques: Dendrochronology:** **Tree-ring dating** (dendrochronology) analyzes the annual growth rings of ancient wood beams to date archaeological structures and climatic variations precisely.
• **Analysis of Animal Bones (Zooarchaeology):** Studying animal bone fragments recovered from sites reveals the diet of early humans, which species were hunted, and which were domesticated for meat and milk.
• **Plant Remains (Paleoethnobotany):** Flotation techniques allow scientists to recover microscopic plant remains, seeds, and pollen, revealing what crops were cultivated, gathered, and used for medicine.
• **Study of Human Skeletons (Bioarchaeology):** Anthropologists examine human skeletal remains to determine age at death, gender, nutritional health, diseases, physical injuries, and genetic relationships within ancient communities.
• **Rock Art Analysis:** Studying prehistoric cave paintings and petroglyphs helps researchers understand early human ritual practices, symbolic thinking, psychological development, and interactions with local wildlife.
• **Ethnoarchaeology:** Archaeologists study modern traditional hunter-gatherer and indigenous farming societies to draw parallel models that help interpret the archaeological record of ancient human behavior.
• **Preservation Challenges:** Fragile organic materials like textiles, wooden tools, and leather decompose rapidly in humid, acidic soils, meaning much of early human material culture has been lost forever.
• **Accidental Discoveries:** Many critical prehistoric sites, such as the Lascaux caves, were discovered accidentally by children, farmers, or construction workers, highlighting the element of chance in archaeological history.
• **Museums and Conservation:** Uncovered artifacts are carefully cleaned, cataloged, preserved in controlled laboratory environments, and displayed in museums to educate the public about our shared human heritage.
• **Global Significance of Heritage:** Understanding the earliest societies fosters a deep appreciation for human resilience, ingenuity, and our species' profound historical connection to the natural world.

• **Evolutionary Journey Summary:** The history of early human societies is a remarkable journey of adaptation, where humans transformed from vulnerable, wandering foragers into dominant ecological shapers and urban builders.
• **From Foraging to Farming:** The shift from nomadic food gathering to settled agriculture (the Neolithic Revolution) stands as the single most profound economic turning point in human history.
• **Security of Surplus:** Food production generated food surpluses, eliminating the constant daily panic of starvation and allowing communities to invest time in non-agricultural pursuits.
• **Birth of Specialization:** With guaranteed food supplies, individuals specialized in specific crafts such as pottery making, tool crafting, weaving, metalworking, and jewelry design, elevating societal productivity.
• **Community Interdependence:** Craft specialization created economic interdependence among villagers, reinforcing social bonds, trade, and cooperative labor practices essential for village expansion.
• **Urbanization Catalyst:** The accumulation of surplus wealth, trade networks, and complex social hierarchies in farming villages naturally acted as the catalyst for the rise of the world's first cities.
• **Environmental Impact:** Early societies drastically altered their local environments through deforestation for agriculture, domesticating wild species, controlling watercourses, and building permanent settlements.
• **Resilience and Problem Solving:** Early humans demonstrated exceptional problem-solving skills, inventing fire-making, microlithic weaponry, agriculture, pottery, metallurgy, and the wheel without modern scientific tools.
• **Cultural Continuity:** Many cultural traditions, spiritual practices, art forms, and social organizational structures established during the Stone Age continue to resonate in modern indigenous cultures.
• **The Unity of Humankind:** Archaeological evidence from early societies demonstrates that human beings across all continents faced similar survival challenges and arrived at remarkably similar inventive solutions.
• **Importance of Prehistoric Studies:** Studying Chapter 10 enables students to appreciate that civilization was not created overnight, but was built through millions of years of cumulative human effort and ingenuity.
• **Role of Technology:** From the first chipped stone hand axe to polished Neolithic adzes and copper-bronze metallurgy, technology has always been the primary engine driving human societal evolution.
• **Interdisciplinary Nature of History:** Reconstructing early societies requires integrating archaeology, anthropology, geology, botany, zoology, and chemistry, highlighting that history is deeply connected to all sciences.
• **Respect for Nature:** The earliest societies maintained an intensely spiritual and respectful relationship with nature, viewing plants, animals, rivers, and the earth as sacred forces sustaining life.
• **Legacy of Early Societies:** Every modern technological, social, and cultural convenience we enjoy today is built upon the foundational discoveries, survival struggles, and innovations of our earliest ancestors.
