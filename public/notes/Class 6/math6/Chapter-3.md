• **DAV Dictionary Academic Notes**: Official Class 6 Secondary Mathematics Study Guide for Chapter 3: Integers, prepared for DAV Public School Board students and educators.
• **Chapter Overview**: This comprehensive study note provides an exhaustive examination of the concept of integers, extending the number system beyond whole numbers to include negative values.
• **Need for Negative Numbers**: Natural numbers ($1, 2, 3, \dots$) and whole numbers ($0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$) are insufficient for representing situations involving opposites, debts, or temperatures below zero.
• **Introduction of Negative Integers**: Numbers less than zero, denoted with a minus sign ($-1, -2, -3, \dots$), are introduced to solve real-world problems involving directional opposites and deficits.
• **Definition of Integers**: The collection of numbers consisting of whole numbers and negative numbers, represented by the set $\{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, is officially defined as the set of integers.
• **Symbolic Representation**: Integers are denoted by the standard mathematical symbol $\mathbb{Z}$, derived from the German word *Zahlen*, meaning "numbers".
• **Partition of Integers**: The set of integers is broadly categorized into three distinct components: positive integers, negative integers, and the neutral integer zero.
• **Positive Integers**: Integers greater than zero ($+1, +2, +3, \dots$ or simply $1, 2, 3, \dots$) which are used to represent profits, heights above sea level, and forward movements.
• **Negative Integers**: Integers less than zero ($-1, -2, -3, \dots$) which are used to represent losses, depths below sea level, and backward movements.
• **The Role of Zero**: Zero ($0$) is a unique integer that is neither positive nor negative, serving as the neutral reference point or origin on the number line.
• **Sign Convention**: Every non-zero integer carries a sign, either a plus ($+$) sign indicating a positive integer or a minus ($-$) sign indicating a negative integer.
• **Magnitude of an Integer**: The absolute value or numerical value of an integer without regard to its sign represents its magnitude or distance from zero.
• **Real-World Applications**: Integers find extensive applications in recording temperatures, financial bookkeeping (credits and debits), elevation tracking, and time calculations (BCE and CE).
• **Temperature Scale Context**: On a standard Celsius thermometer, temperatures above freezing are positive integers, freezing point is zero, and temperatures below freezing are negative integers.
• **Elevation Context**: Sea level is designated as the reference point $0$, elevations above sea level are positive integers, and depths below sea level are negative integers.

• **The Number Line Concept**: A horizontal straight line used for the visual representation of numbers, where each point corresponds to a unique real number.
• **Construction of the Integer Number Line**: A line is drawn with a chosen point labeled as $0$ (origin), and equally spaced intervals are marked on both sides.
• **Placement of Positive Integers**: On the horizontal number line, positive integers are marked at equal intervals to the right side of the origin ($0$).
• **Placement of Negative Integers**: Negative integers are marked at equal intervals to the left side of the origin ($0$) corresponding to the positive units.
• **Symmetry Around Zero**: The number line demonstrates symmetry around zero, where for every positive integer $+n$, there is a corresponding negative integer $-n$ at an equal distance from the origin.
• **Directional Property of the Number Line**: Moving to the right along the number line represents an increase in value, while moving to the left represents a decrease in value.
• **Graphical Representation of Magnitude**: The distance of any integer point from the origin on the number line represents its absolute numerical value.
• **Successor on the Number Line**: The successor of any integer on the number line is the integer located immediately to its right (obtained by adding $1$).
• **Predecessor on the Number Line**: The predecessor of any integer on the number line is the integer located immediately to its left (obtained by subtracting $1$).
• **Comparison Rule - Right vs Left**: Any integer lying to the right of another integer on the number line is always greater than the one on the left.
• **Comparison Rule - Positive vs Negative**: Every positive integer is strictly greater than every negative integer, regardless of their numerical magnitudes.
• **Comparison Rule - Zero vs Positive**: Zero is always less than any positive integer ($0 < +n$).
• **Comparison Rule - Zero vs Negative**: Zero is always greater than any negative integer ($0 > -n$).
• **Comparison Rule - Two Negatives**: Between two negative integers, the one with the smaller numerical magnitude is greater (e.g., $-2 > -5$).
• **Visualizing Ordering**: The number line serves as an infallible visual tool for determining ascending and descending orders of mixed positive and negative integers.

• **Absolute Value Definition**: The absolute value of an integer is its numerical value regardless of whether it is positive or negative, representing its pure magnitude.
• **Mathematical Notation for Absolute Value**: The absolute value of an integer $a$ is denoted by vertical bars enclosing the integer, written as $|a|$.
• **Absolute Value of Positive Integers**: The absolute value of any positive integer is the integer itself; for example, $|+5| = 5$.
• **Absolute Value of Negative Integers**: The absolute value of any negative integer is its corresponding positive counterpart; for example, $|-5| = 5$.
• **Absolute Value of Zero**: The absolute value of zero is zero itself, expressed mathematically as $|0| = 0$.
• **Distance Interpretation**: Geometrically, the absolute value $|a|$ represents the exact distance of the integer $a$ from $0$ on the number line, ignoring direction.
• **Non-Negative Nature**: The absolute value of any integer is always a non-negative number; it can be zero or positive, but never negative ($|a| \ge 0$).
• **Equality of Opposites**: An integer and its opposite have the same absolute value; for instance, $|-7| = |+7| = 7$.
• **Comparison Using Absolute Value**: When comparing two negative integers, the integer with the smaller absolute value is the greater number.
• **Addition of Integers Overview**: Combining two or more integers following specific sign rules to find their combined sum or total effect.
• **Addition Rule 1 - Same Signs**: To add two integers with the same sign, add their absolute values and prefix the common sign to the result.
• **Example of Positive Addition**: Adding two positive integers ($+5 + +3$) results in $+8$, following standard whole number addition rules.
• **Example of Negative Addition**: Adding two negative integers ($-5 + -3$) results in $-8$, by adding absolute values ($5+3=8$) and keeping the negative sign.
• **Addition Rule 2 - Opposite Signs**: To add two integers with different signs, find the difference of their absolute values and prefix the sign of the integer with the larger absolute value.
• **Example of Mixed Sign Addition**: Adding $+7$ and $-4$ involves finding the difference ($7-4=3$) and applying the positive sign of $7$, yielding $+3$.

• **Additive Inverse Definition**: The additive inverse of an integer is that integer which, when added to the original integer, yields a sum of zero.
• **Rule for Additive Inverse**: The additive inverse of any integer $a$ is $-a$, and the additive inverse of $-a$ is $a$.
• **Zero's Additive Inverse**: Zero is its own additive inverse, as $0 + 0 = 0$.
• **Property of Opposites**: An integer and its additive inverse are often called opposites; their sum is always equal to the additive identity ($0$).
• **Subtraction of Integers Definition**: Subtraction of an integer is defined as the addition of its additive inverse.
• **Fundamental Subtraction Rule**: For any two integers $a$ and $b$, subtracting $b$ from $a$ is mathematically equivalent to adding the additive inverse of $b$ to $a$ ($a - b = a + (-b)$).
• **Rule for Subtracting a Positive Integer**: To subtract a positive integer, move to the left on the number line or change the operation to addition with a negative integer.
• **Rule for Subtracting a Negative Integer**: Subtracting a negative integer is equivalent to adding its corresponding positive integer ($a - (-b) = a + b$).
• **Double Negative Rule**: The juxtaposition of two negative operational signs transforms into a positive sign (e.g., $- (-)$ becomes $+$).
• **Closure Property for Addition**: The sum of any two integers is always an integer; if $a$ and $b$ are integers, then $a + b$ is also an integer.
• **Closure Property for Subtraction**: The difference of any two integers is always an integer; if $a$ and $b$ are integers, then $a - b$ is always an integer.
• **Commutative Property of Addition**: The order of addition does not affect the sum for any integers $a$ and $b$: $a + b = b + a$.
• **Lack of Commutativity in Subtraction**: Subtraction is not commutative for integers; generally, $a - b \neq b - a$ (except when $a = b$).
• **Associative Property of Addition**: When adding three or more integers, the grouping does not affect the final sum: $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$.
• **Lack of Associativity in Subtraction**: Subtraction is not associative for integers; the grouping of terms alters the final result.
----PAGE-BREAK---
• **Additive Identity Property**: Zero is the additive identity for integers, meaning that adding zero to any integer leaves the integer unchanged ($a + 0 = 0 + a = a$).
• **Representation on Number Line - Addition**: Adding a positive integer to a given integer requires moving to the right on the number line by that many units.
• **Representation on Number Line - Negative Addition**: Adding a negative integer requires moving to the left on the number line by that many units.
• **Representation on Number Line - Subtraction**: Subtracting a positive integer corresponds to moving to the left on the number line.
• **Representation on Number Line - Negative Subtraction**: Subtracting a negative integer corresponds to moving to the right on the number line.
• **Simplifying Expressions**: Mathematical expressions containing multiple additions and subtractions of integers are simplified by grouping positive integers together and negative integers together.
• **Step-by-Step Simplification**: To evaluate strings of integer operations, convert all subtraction operations to addition of additive inverses first.
• **Combining Like Signs in Strings**: Sum all positive terms together into a single positive total, and sum all negative terms together into a single negative total.
• **Final Resolution of Strings**: Combine the resulting single positive integer and single negative integer using the opposite-sign addition rule.
• **Word Problems Involving Profit and Loss**: Profit is represented by a positive integer, while loss or debt is represented by a negative integer.
• **Word Problems Involving Banking**: Deposits into a bank account are recorded as positive integers, while withdrawals are recorded as negative integers.
• **Word Problems Involving Time**: Dates or years Before Common Era (BCE) are represented using negative integers, while Common Era (CE) years are positive integers.
• **Word Problems Involving Movement**: Movement forward or upward is denoted by positive integers; movement backward or downward is denoted by negative integers.
• **Checking Solutions**: The validity of integer calculations can be verified by reverting to number line displacements or checking inverse operations.
• **Error Prevention in Signs**: A common error in integer operations is misinterpreting the sign resulting from subtraction of negative numbers; meticulous adherence to rules prevents this.

• **Multiplication of Integers Overview**: Multiplication is repeated addition, but with integers, sign rules dictate whether the product is positive or negative.
• **Multiplication Rule - Same Signs**: The product of two integers with the same sign (both positive or both negative) is always a positive integer.
• **Multiplication Rule - Positive $\times$ Positive**: Multiplying two positive integers yields a positive product ($+ \times + = +$).
• **Multiplication Rule - Negative $\times$ Negative**: Multiplying two negative integers yields a positive product ($- \times - = +$).
• **Multiplication Rule - Opposite Signs**: The product of two integers with different signs (one positive and one negative) is always a negative integer.
• **Multiplication Rule - Mixed Signs**: Multiplying a positive integer by a negative integer, or vice versa, yields a negative product ($+ \times - = -$ and $- \times + = -$).
• **Product of Multiple Integers - Even Negatives**: If the number of negative integers in a multiplication product is even, the final product is positive.
• **Product of Multiple Integers - Odd Negatives**: If the number of negative integers in a multiplication product is odd, the final product is negative.
• **Closure Property of Multiplication**: The product of any two integers is always an integer; if $a$ and $b$ are integers, then $a \times b$ is an integer.
• **Commutative Property of Multiplication**: The order of multiplication does not affect the product for any integers $a$ and $b$: $a \times b = b \times a$.
• **Associative Property of Multiplication**: The grouping of integers in multiplication does not affect the final product: $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$.
• **Multiplicative Identity Property**: The integer $1$ is the multiplicative identity for integers, as multiplying any integer by $1$ leaves it unchanged ($a \times 1 = 1 \times a = a$).
• **Property of Zero in Multiplication**: Multiplying any integer by zero results in a product of zero ($a \times 0 = 0 \times a = 0$).
• **Distributive Property of Multiplication over Addition**: Multiplication distributes over addition for integers: $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$.
• **Distributive Property of Multiplication over Subtraction**: Multiplication distributes over subtraction for integers: $a \times (b - c) = (a \times b) - (a \times c)$.

• **Division of Integers Overview**: Division is the inverse operation of multiplication, governed by systematic sign rules analogous to multiplication.
• **Division Rule - Same Signs**: The quotient of two integers with the same sign (both positive or both negative) is a positive integer.
• **Division Rule - Positive $\div$ Positive**: Dividing a positive integer by a positive integer yields a positive quotient ($+ \div + = +$).
• **Division Rule - Negative $\div$ Negative**: Dividing a negative integer by a negative integer yields a positive quotient ($- \div - = +$).
• **Division Rule - Opposite Signs**: The quotient of two integers with different signs (one positive and one negative) is a negative integer ($+ \div - = -$ and $- \div + = -$).
• **Division by One**: Dividing any integer by $1$ results in the integer itself ($a \div 1 = a$).
• **Division of Integer by Itself**: Dividing a non-zero integer by itself results in $1$ ($a \div a = 1$ for $a \neq 0$).
• **Division of Integer by its Opposite**: Dividing a non-zero integer by its opposite yields $-1$ ($a \div (-a) = -1$ for $a \neq 0$).
• **Division of Zero**: Dividing zero by any non-zero integer results in zero ($0 \div a = 0$ where $a \neq 0$).
• **Division by Zero (Undefined)**: Division of any integer by zero is mathematically undefined; $a \div 0$ has no meaning in the integer system.
• **Closure Property Failure in Division**: The set of integers is not closed under division; dividing one integer by another does not always result in an integer (e.g., $3 \div 2 = 1.5$, which is not an integer).
• **Lack of Commutativity in Division**: Division is not commutative for integers; generally, $a \div b \neq b \div a$.
• **Lack of Associativity in Division**: Division is not associative for integers; the grouping of terms changes the result.
• **Order of Operations (BODMAS/PEMDAS)**: When evaluating complex integer expressions, operations must follow the strict hierarchy: Brackets, Orders, Division, Multiplication, Addition, Subtraction.
• **Application of BODMAS with Integers**: Parentheses and brackets are evaluated from inside out, followed by multiplication and division (left to right), and finally addition and subtraction (left to right).

• **Properties Summary Table - Addition**: Integers are closed, commutative, associative, possess an additive identity ($0$), and have additive inverses ($-a$).
• **Properties Summary Table - Subtraction**: Integers are closed under subtraction, but subtraction is neither commutative nor associative, and has no identity element.
• **Properties Summary Table - Multiplication**: Integers are closed, commutative, associative, possess a multiplicative identity ($1$), and follow the zero property.
• **Properties Summary Table - Division**: Integers are not closed under division; division is neither commutative nor associative, and division by zero is undefined.
• **Summary of Sign Rules for Addition**: Same signs $\rightarrow$ add magnitudes and keep sign. Different signs $\rightarrow$ subtract smaller magnitude from larger and keep sign of larger.
• **Summary of Sign Rules for Subtraction**: Change subtraction to addition of the additive inverse, then apply addition rules.
• **Summary of Sign Rules for Multiplication**: Same signs $\rightarrow$ positive product. Different signs $\rightarrow$ negative product.
• **Summary of Sign Rules for Division**: Same signs $\rightarrow$ positive quotient. Different signs $\rightarrow$ negative quotient.
• **Historical Context of Integers**: Negative numbers were used historically in ancient China (via counting rods of different colors) and in India by Brahmagupta to represent debts.
• **Brahmagupta's Rules**: The 7th-century Indian mathematician Brahmagupta formulated rules for operations with fortunes (positive numbers) and debts (negative numbers).
• **Fortunes and Debts Metaphor**: A fortune represents a positive integer, a debt represents a negative integer, providing an intuitive pedagogical framework for DAV Class 6 students.
• **Addition of Fortunes and Debts**: Adding two fortunes increases the fortune; adding two debts increases the debt; a fortune combined with a debt cancels out partially or fully.
• **Subtraction of Fortunes and Debts**: Taking away a debt is equivalent to receiving a fortune; taking away a fortune is equivalent to incurring a debt.
• **Sign Convention Mastery**: Mastery of sign conventions in Class 6 lays the critical algebraic foundation for solving linear equations in higher classes.
• **Mental Math Strategies**: Utilizing properties like associativity and commutativity allows students to mentally group integers that sum to multiples of ten or zero for efficient calculation.

• **Simplification Rule for Adjacent Signs**: Like adjacent signs ($+$ and $+$, or $-$ and $-$) simplify to a positive sign; unlike adjacent signs ($+$ and $-$, or $-$ and $+$) simplify to a negative sign.
• **Error Analysis - Sign Dropping**: A frequent student error is dropping the negative sign during intermediate steps of multi-step addition or subtraction; careful documentation prevents this.
• **Error Analysis - Multiplication Sign Confusion**: Confusing the addition sign rules with multiplication sign rules (e.g., thinking negative plus negative is positive) is mitigated through consistent practice.
• **Symmetry in Mathematics**: The integer number line exemplifies mathematical symmetry, balancing positive and negative domains across the central axis of zero.
