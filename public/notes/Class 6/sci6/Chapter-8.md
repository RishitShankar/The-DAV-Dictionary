• **Introduction to Plant Morphology**: The study of external forms and structural features of plants is known as morphology, which provides a foundational understanding of how plants are organized for survival.
• **Basic Plant Body Plan**: The flowering plant body is fundamentally divided into two major systems: the underground subterranean root system and the aerial shoot system.
• **The Root System**: This underground part of the plant develops directly from the radicle of the germinating seed and is primarily responsible for anchorage and absorption.
• **The Shoot System**: This aerial part of the plant grows upwards against gravity, developing from the plumule, and bears stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits.
• **General Functions of Roots**: Roots firmly anchor the plant body into the soil, preventing it from being blown away by wind or washed away by flowing water.
• **Absorption of Water and Minerals**: Root hairs vastly increase the surface area of the root to efficiently absorb water and dissolved mineral nutrients from the soil.
• **Storage of Food**: In many plants, roots become fleshy and swollen to store reserve food material, serving as a survival mechanism during unfavorable conditions.
• **Primary Root Types**: The root system in plants is broadly classified into two main types: the tap root system and the fibrous root system.
• **Tap Root System Structure**: A tap root consists of a single, thick, primary main root that grows straight down into the soil, giving off smaller lateral side branches called secondary and tertiary roots.
• **Examples of Tap Root Plants**: Common examples of plants possessing a tap root system include mustard, bean, pea, carrot, radish, and mango.
• **Fibrous Root System Structure**: A fibrous root system lacks a main primary root; instead, a cluster of thin, thread-like, similar-sized roots arises from the base of the stem.
• **Examples of Fibrous Root Plants**: Plants such as wheat, rice, maize, grass, onion, and sugarcane typically exhibit a well-developed fibrous root system.

• **Modification of Roots**: Besides their primary functions, roots in certain plants undergo structural modifications to perform specialized secondary functions like food storage, support, and respiration.
• **Fusiform Roots**: These are spindle-shaped roots that are swollen in the middle and taper towards both ends, as seen distinctively in the radish.
• **Coniform Roots**: These roots are broad at the base near the stem and gradually taper down to a sharp point at the apex, as observed in carrots.
• **Napiform Roots**: These roots are swollen almost spherically at the top and taper sharply like a tail at the bottom, characteristically found in turnips and beetroot.
• **Storage Root Significance**: Fleshy tap roots store large amounts of starch and nutrients, which are consumed by humans and animals and utilized by the plant during the flowering season.
• **Prop Roots for Support**: Certain large trees, such as the banyan tree, develop hanging aerial roots from their heavy horizontal branches that grow downwards to enter the soil and act as sturdy pillars.
• **Stilt Roots for Support**: Plants with weak or top-heavy stems, such as maize and sugarcane, produce specialized supporting roots from the lower nodes of the stem that grow obliquely downwards into the soil.
• **Breathing Roots (Pneumatophores):** Plants growing in marshy, saline, or waterlogged soils, like mangroves, develop specialized roots that grow vertically upwards out of the soil and water to take in oxygen from the air.
• **The Stem Structure**: The stem is the ascending, upright axis of the plant shoot system that bears leaves, buds, flowers, and fruits, maintaining the structural framework of the plant.
• **Nodes and Internodes**: The points on the stem where leaves and lateral buds are attached are called nodes, while the clean stretches of stem lying between two successive nodes are termed internodes.
• **Axillary Buds**: The angle between the upper surface of a leaf and the stem is known as the axil, and it houses a bud that has the potential to grow into a branch or a flower.
• **Terminal (Apical) Bud**: Located at the very tip or apex of the stem, this bud is responsible for the upward linear growth and elongation of the plant shoot.
• **General Functions of Stems**: The stem holds leaves in a manner that maximizes their exposure to sunlight and positions flowers and fruits for optimal pollination and seed dispersal.
• **Conduit Role of Stems**: Stems act as the primary two-way transport highway of the plant, conducting water and minerals upwards and organic food manufactured in leaves downwards.
• **Herbaceous Stems**: Soft, green, and delicate stems characteristic of herbs and young shrubs are termed herbaceous stems, which contain active chlorophyll for minor photosynthesis.
• **Woody Stems**: Hard, thick, and rigid brown stems characteristic of trees and shrubs are known as woody stems, protected by a tough outer layer of bark.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Stem Modifications**: Stems in various plant species are modified structurally to perform specialized functions such as food storage, vegetative propagation, climbing support, and defense.
• **Underground Stems for Storage**: Certain plants possess underground stems like tubers, rhizomes, corms, and bulbs that remain dormant underground during adverse seasons and store food.
• **Potato as a Stem Tuber**: A potato is a modified underground stem tuber, as evidenced by the presence of 'eyes' (buds), scale leaves, and nodes, rather than true root structures.
• **Ginger as a Rhizome**: Ginger is a fleshy, horizontally growing underground stem featuring distinct nodes, internodes, scale leaves, and terminal buds.
• **Onion as a Bulb**: An onion consists of a greatly reduced, disc-like underground stem surrounded by numerous fleshy, scale-like leaves that store water and nutrients.
• **Stem Tendrils for Support**: Plants with weak stems, such as grapevines and gourds, develop thin, wiry, sensitive, spirally coiled structures called tendrils derived from stems to climb up supports.
• **Stem Thorns for Protection**: In plants like citrus, bougainvillea, and pomegranate, axillary buds are modified into sharp, hard, woody structures called thorns that protect the plant from grazing herbivores.
• **Phylloclades (Photosynthetic Stems)**: In xerophytic plants like cacti, leaves are reduced to spines to prevent water loss, and the stems become flattened, fleshy, green, and take over the process of photosynthesis.
• **Introduction to Leaf Morphology**: The leaf is a flat, thin, green, lateral outgrowth borne on the stem at a node, specialized primarily to carry out photosynthesis and transpirational gas exchange.
• **Parts of a Typical Leaf**: A standard angiosperm leaf structurally consists of three main parts: the leaf base, the petiole (leaf stalk), and the lamina or leaf blade.
• **Leaf Base and Stipules**: The leaf base is the lowest part of the leaf attached to the stem node, often bearing two small, lateral, appendage-like structures called stipules.
• **Petiole Function**: The petiole is the cylindrical stalk that connects the leaf blade to the stem, holding the lamina outward and allowing it to flutter gently in the breeze for air circulation.
• **Lamina (Leaf Blade)**: The lamina is the expanded, green, flat surface of the leaf that provides a massive surface area to trap sunlight and absorb carbon dioxide.
• **Midrib and Veins**: Running through the center of the lamina is a prominent thick vein called the midrib, which branches into a network of lateral veins that transport water, minerals, and food.
• **Stomata Overview**: Microscopic pores known as stomata are present predominantly on the underside of the leaf surface, regulated by specialized guard cells for gas exchange.
• **Stomatal Function**: Stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter the leaf for photosynthesis and permit oxygen and excess water vapor to exit during transpiration.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Venation Patterns**: The arrangement of veins and veinlets on the leaf lamina is known as venation, which serves as a vital taxonomic feature closely correlated with root types.
• **Reticulate Venation**: When veins form a complex, web-like, interconnected network across the lamina, the pattern is termed reticulate venation, characteristic of dicotyledonous plants.
• **Parallel Venation**: When all the veins run parallel to one another from the leaf base to the apex or from the midrib to the margin, it is called parallel venation, typical of monocotyledonous plants.
• **Correlation between Venation and Roots**: Plants with reticulate venation in their leaves invariably possess a tap root system, whereas plants with parallel venation possess a fibrous root system.
• **Simple Leaves**: A leaf is classified as simple when its lamina is entire and undivided, or if incisions do not reach down to the midrib, as seen in mango, peepal, and banana.
• **Compound Leaves**: A leaf is termed compound when the incision of the lamina goes down completely to the midrib or petiole, breaking the single blade into multiple distinct segments called leaflets.
• **Pinnately Compound Leaves**: In this type of compound leaf, a number of leaflets are arranged laterally on a common axis called the rachis, as observed in neem, rose, and tamarind.
• **Palmately Compound Leaves**: In this arrangement, several leaflets are attached together at a common point at the tip of the petiole, resembling the fingers of a palm, as seen in silk cotton.
• **Leaf Modifications**: Leaves in certain plants undergo structural adaptations to perform non-foliar functions like climbing, trapping insects, and defense against herbivores.
• **Leaf Tendrils**: In some weak-stemmed plants like peas and glorious lilies, the entire leaf or its terminal leaflets are modified into sensitive, coiling tendrils to help the plant climb.
• **Leaf Spines for Protection**: In desert plants like cacti and opuntia, leaves are modified into sharp, pointed needles or spines to drastically reduce transpirational water loss and deter herbivores.
• **Insectivorous Leaf Traps**: Plants growing in nitrogen-deficient soils, such as the Pitcher plant, Venus flytrap, and Sundew, modify their leaves into specialized traps to catch and digest insects for nitrogen.
• **Pitcher Plant Mechanism**: The leaf lamina of the pitcher plant is modified into a hollow pitcher-like structure with a lid, containing digestive juices to dissolve trapped insects.
• **Venus Flytrap Mechanism**: The leaf lobes of the Venus flytrap bear sensitive trigger hairs that snap shut rapidly when an insect brushes against them, trapping the prey securely.
• **Storage Leaves**: Succulent plants like Bryophyllum and jade plants possess thick, fleshy leaves that store large quantities of water and nutrients to survive prolonged drought.
• **Vegetative Propagation via Leaves**: Leaves of certain plants like Bryophyllum develop adventitious buds along their margins that grow into tiny new plantlets when they touch damp soil.

• **Introduction to Flower Morphology**: The flower is the specialized reproductive shoot of flowering plants (angiosperms), designed exclusively for sexual reproduction and the formation of seeds and fruits.
• **Pedicel and Thalamus**: The flower is attached to the stem by a stalk called the pedicel, and its swollen upper tip where all floral whorls are attached is known as the thalamus or receptacle.
• **Four Floral Whorls**: A complete typical flower is arranged in four concentric rings or whorls attached to the thalamus, progressing from the outermost to the innermost ring.
• **Calyx (Outermost Whorl)**: The calyx is the outermost non-essential whorl of the flower, composed of individual green, leaf-like units called sepals that protect the flower in the bud stage.
• **Corolla (Second Whorl)**: The corolla lies just inside the calyx, composed of brightly colored, showy, scented units called petals designed to attract insect pollinators.
• **Perianth**: In flowers where sepals and petals look nearly identical and cannot be distinguished (such as in lilies and onions), the combined whorl is termed the perianth, and individual units are tepals.
• **Androecium (Male Reproductive Whorl)**: The androecium is the third whorl from the outside, representing the male reproductive organ of the flower, consisting of individual units called stamens.
• **Structure of a Stamen**: A standard stamen is structurally differentiated into two distinct parts: a slender stalk called the filament and a two-lobed pollen-bearing sac at the top called the anther.
• **Pollen Grains**: The anther produces millions of microscopic, powdery grains called pollen grains, which contain the male gametes necessary for plant fertilization.
• **Gynoecium (Female Reproductive Whorl)**: The gynoecium (or pistil) is the innermost fourth whorl, representing the female reproductive organ, composed of one or more carpels.
• **Structure of a Carpel/Pistil**: A typical carpel is structurally divided into three distinct regions: the basal swollen ovary, the middle tubular style, and the terminal sticky stigma.
• **Ovary, Ovules, and Eggs**: The ovary forms the basal cavity containing tiny, bead-like structures called ovules, inside of which female gametes or egg cells reside.
• **Style and Stigma Function**: The style is a slender neck connecting the ovary to the stigma, while the stigma provides a sticky landing platform to trap compatible pollen grains.
• **Complete vs. Incomplete Flowers**: A complete flower possesses all four floral whorls (calyx, corolla, androecium, and gynoecium), whereas an incomplete flower lacks one or more of these whorls.
• **Bisexual (Hermaphrodite) Flowers**: Flowers that contain both male (androecium) and female (gynoecium) reproductive organs within the same individual flower are termed bisexual, e.g., mustard, rose, hibiscus.
• **Unisexual Flowers**: Flowers that contain exclusively either male organs (staminate flowers) or female organs (pistillate flowers) are termed unisexual, e.g., papaya, cucumber, maize.

• **Introduction to Plant Physiology**: Plant physiology is the branch of biology that studies the life processes, metabolic activities, and internal mechanisms that sustain plant life and growth.
• **Photosynthesis Definition**: Photosynthesis is the vital anabolic biochemical process by which green plants manufacture their own organic food (glucose) using carbon dioxide and water in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll.
• **Role of Chlorophyll**: Chlorophyll is the green pigment housed inside specialized plant cell organelles called chloroplasts, possessing the unique ability to trap and absorb radiant solar energy.
• **Raw Materials of Photosynthesis**: The essential inputs required for photosynthesis are carbon dioxide (absorbed from the air via stomata) and water (absorbed from the soil via roots).
• **Chemical Equation of Photosynthesis**: The overall summary process is expressed as: Carbon Dioxide + Water $\xrightarrow{\text{Sunlight/Chlorophyll}}$ Glucose (Food) + Oxygen (Byproduct).
• **Byproduct Release**: Oxygen gas is released as a vital gaseous byproduct of photosynthesis into the atmosphere through the stomata, sustaining aerobic life on Earth.
• **Storage of Carbohydrates**: The glucose manufactured during photosynthesis is rapidly converted into an insoluble storage carbohydrate called starch for safe cellular storage in leaves, stems, or roots.
• **Testing for Starch**: The presence of starch in a leaf is confirmed using the Iodine test, where a starch-containing leaf turns a deep blue-black color upon treatment with iodine solution.
• **Significance of Photosynthesis**: Photosynthesis is the ultimate foundation of all life on Earth, as it provides organic food for all heterotrophic organisms and maintains atmospheric oxygen levels.
• **Respiration in Plants**: Respiration is a continuous catabolic process where plants break down stored food (glucose) using oxygen to release chemical energy (ATP) required for various cellular metabolic activities.
• **Respiration Gas Exchange**: Unlike photosynthesis, respiration consumes oxygen and releases carbon dioxide and water vapor, occurring day and night in all living plant cells.
• **Mechanisms of Plant Respiration**: Plants do not possess specialized respiratory organs; instead, gas exchange occurs efficiently through stomata in leaves, lenticels in woody stems, and intercellular spaces in roots.
• **Transpiration Definition**: Transpiration is the biological process by which a living plant loses excess water in the form of water vapor from its aerial parts, predominantly through the stomata of leaves.
• **Mechanism of Transpiration**: Solar heat causes liquid water inside the leaf's internal spongy mesophyll cells to evaporate into intercellular spaces, from where it diffuses out through open stomatal pores.
• **Transpirational Pull**: As water transpires from the leaves, a continuous suction force called transpirational pull is generated, which draws water and dissolved minerals upward from the roots through xylem vessels.
• **Cooling Effect of Transpiration**: Transpiration acts as a natural air-conditioning mechanism for plants, protecting delicate leaf tissues from overheating under intense tropical sunlight.

• **Introduction to Plant Nutrition**: Plants require various chemical elements, broadly classified as nutrients, for their proper growth, metabolic functioning, development, and completion of their life cycle.
• **Nutrient Classification**: Plant nutrients are broadly categorized into macronutrients (required in large quantities) and micronutrients (required in trace, minute quantities).
• **Macronutrients Examples**: Essential macronutrients include nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and sulfur, which form structural components of proteins, nucleic acids, and cell walls.
• **Micronutrients Examples**: Essential micronutrients include iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, and chlorine, which act primarily as enzymatic cofactors in biochemical reactions.
• **Autotrophic Nutrition**: The vast majority of plants are autotrophs, meaning they synthesize their own complex organic food from simple inorganic raw materials using light energy.
• **Heterotrophic Nutrition in Plants**: A small group of specialized plants lack chlorophyll or cannot obtain sufficient nutrients from soil, adopting heterotrophic modes of nutrition (parasitic, saprophytic, or insectivorous).
• **Parasitic Plants**: Parasitic plants derive their nutrition entirely or partially from another living host plant using specialized root-like structures called haustoria that tap into the host's vascular system.
• **Total Parasites**: Plants like Cuscuta (Dodder) are total stem parasites that lack both leaves and chlorophyll, winding tightly around a host plant and extracting all their food and water.
• **Partial Parasites**: Plants like Mistletoe contain chlorophyll and manufacture their own food through photosynthesis, but they depend on their host plant for water and minerals by tapping into xylem tissue.
• **Saprophytic Plants**: Saprophytes are non-green plants that obtain their nutrition by secreting digestive enzymes onto dead, decaying organic matter and absorbing the resulting soluble nutrients.
• **Examples of Saprophytes**: Classic examples of saprophytic flowering plants include Monotropa (Indian pipe) and Neottia (Bird's-nest orchid), alongside various soil fungi and bacteria.
• **Insectivorous Plants**: Carnivorous or insectivorous plants grow in nitrogen-deficient bogs and marshes, supplementing their nutritional needs by trapping, killing, and digesting insects.
• **Adaptations of Insectivorous Plants**: These plants possess remarkable leaf modifications (such as pitchers, sticky glandular hairs, or snap traps) equipped with glands that secrete proteolytic enzymes.
• **Symbiotic Associations**: Symbiosis is a mutually beneficial biological relationship where two different organisms live in close physical association and share resources.
• **Lichens as Symbionts**: A lichen is a classic symbiotic partnership between a green alga (or cyanobacterium) and a fungus, where the alga provides food via photosynthesis and the fungus provides shelter, water, and minerals.
• **Rhizobium and Legume Symbiosis**: Nitrogen-fixing bacteria belonging to the genus *Rhizobium* inhabit root nodules of leguminous plants (peas, beans), converting atmospheric nitrogen into nitrates while receiving shelter and sugars from the host plant.

• **Introduction to Reproduction in Plants**: Reproduction is the fundamental biological process by which living organisms produce new offspring of their own kind, ensuring the continuation of their species across generations.
• **Modes of Plant Reproduction**: Plant reproduction occurs primarily through two distinct mechanisms: asexual reproduction and sexual reproduction.
• **Asexual Reproduction Definition**: Asexual reproduction involves the production of new plants from a single parent without the fusion of male and female gametes, resulting in genetically identical offspring (clones).
• **Methods of Asexual Reproduction**: Natural asexual reproduction in plants commonly occurs via vegetative propagation, spore formation, budding, and fragmentation.
• **Vegetative Propagation Definition**: Vegetative propagation is a form of asexual reproduction where new plants sprout from vegetative parts of the parent plant such as roots, stems, or leaves.
• **Natural Vegetative Propagation by Roots**: Sweet potatoes, dahlias, and asparagus develop fleshy adventitious roots that can sprout new aerial shoots and roots when planted in soil.
• **Natural Vegetative Propagation by Stems**: Plants propagate naturally through modified stems such as runners (grasses), stolons, tubers (potato), bulbs (onion), and rhizomes (ginger, fern).
• **Artificial Vegetative Propagation**: Humans exploit plant regeneration capabilities through artificial techniques such as cutting, layering, grafting, and micropropagation (tissue culture).
• **Cutting Technique**: A healthy stem or branch containing a few nodes is severed from a parent plant and planted in moist soil, where it develops roots and grows into a new independent plant (e.g., rose, hibiscus, sugarcane).
• **Layering Technique**: A lower branch of a plant is bent down, covered with moist soil while still attached to the parent plant, and allowed to develop roots before being severed (e.g., jasmine, lemon).
• **Grafting Technique**: The stem of one plant with desirable root characteristics (called the stock) is joined with the stem cutting of another plant with desirable shoot/fruit characteristics (called the scion) so they grow together.
• **Significance of Vegetative Propagation**: Vegetative propagation enables rapid production of new plants, ensures plants bear flowers and fruits earlier, and produces exact genetic clones of superior parent varieties.
• **Tissue Culture (Micropropagation)**: A modern biotechnology technique where tiny plant tissue samples or cells are cultured on a nutrient growth medium under aseptic laboratory conditions to produce thousands of identical plantlets.
• **Sexual Reproduction Definition**: Sexual reproduction involves the formation and fusion of male and female gametes to produce a zygote, which subsequently develops into a seed enclosed within a fruit.
• **Role of Flowers in Sexual Reproduction**: Flowers are the exclusive reproductive organs of angiosperms, housing the male androecium (producing pollen) and female gynoecium (containing ovules).
• **Pollination Overview**: Pollination is the crucial transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the receptive stigma of the same flower or another flower of the same species.
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Self-Pollination**: When pollen grains are transferred from the anther to the stigma of the same flower, or to another flower on the exact same plant, it is termed self-pollination.
• **Cross-Pollination**: When pollen grains are transferred from the anther of one flower to the stigma of another flower borne on a completely different plant of the same species, it is termed cross-pollination.
• **Agents of Pollination**: Since plants are rooted in place, cross-pollination relies heavily on external environmental vectors known as pollinating agents, which can be biotic or abiotic.
• **Biotic Pollinating Agents**: Living organisms such as insects (bees, butterflies, moths), birds (hummingbirds, sunbirds), and bats act as vital biotic agents, attracted by bright petals, nectar, and scent.
• **Abiotic Pollinating Agents**: Non-living physical vectors such as wind (anemophily) and water (hydrophily) transfer pollen grains randomly across great distances in certain plant species.
• **Fertilization Definition**: Fertilization is the precise fusion of the male gamete (nucleus contained within the pollen grain) with the female gamete (egg cell inside the ovule) to form a diploid zygote.
• **Post-Pollination Events**: Upon landing on a compatible stigma, a pollen grain absorbs moisture and germinates, growing a microscopic pollen tube down through the style into the ovary.
• **Entry into the Ovule**: The pollen tube enters the ovule through a tiny pore called the micropyle, discharging its male gametes to accomplish fertilization with the egg cell.
• **Formation of Seeds and Fruits**: Following fertilization, the zygote develops into an embryonic plant, the fertilized ovule transforms into a protective seed, and the mature ovary wall swells to form a fruit.
• **Transformation of Floral Parts**: After fertilization, sepals, petals, stamens, style, and stigma typically wither and fall off, while the ovary matures into a fleshy or dry fruit enclosing the seeds.
• **Seed Structure**: A typical seed consists of a tough protective outer coat (testa), a tiny embryonic plant (comprising radicle and plumule), and cotyledons (seed leaves) packed with stored food.
• **Cotyledon Classification**: Seeds are classified based on the number of cotyledons: dicotyledonous seeds possess two seed leaves (e.g., pea, gram, bean), while monocotyledonous seeds possess one (e.g., maize, wheat, rice).
• **Seed Dispersal Definition**: Seed dispersal is the ecological process by which seeds or fruits are transported away from the parent plant to new locations, reducing competition for light, water, and nutrients.
• **Dispersal by Wind (Anemochory)**: Plants produce lightweight, winged, or hairy seeds and fruits (like dandelion, cotton, maple, and madar) that are easily carried aloft by gentle breezes.
• **Dispersal by Water (Hydrochory)**: Plants growing near water bodies, such as coconut palms and lotus, produce buoyant, fibrous, or waterproof fruits that float across rivers, lakes, or oceans to new shores.
• **Dispersal by Animals (Zoochory)**: Animals disperse seeds in two main ways: by eating fleshy fruits and excreting indigestible seeds elsewhere (endozoochory), or by having hooked, sticky fruits cling to animal fur or human clothing (epizoochory).
----PAGE-BREAK----
• **Dispersal by Explosion (Autochory)**: Certain plants, such as pea, balsam, and touch-me-not, develop seed pods that dry out unevenly and burst open explosively, scattering seeds in all directions away from the parent plant.
• **Seed Germination Definition**: Seed germination is the resumption of active metabolic growth by a dormant plant embryo within a seed, resulting in the emergence of a new seedling under favorable conditions.
• **Essential Requirements for Germination**: Successful seed germination strictly requires three critical environmental factors: adequate water (moisture), optimal temperature, and a fresh supply of oxygen.
• **Role of Water in Germination**: Water softens the hard seed coat (testa), activates dormant enzymes, and allows metabolic reactions to begin within the cotyledons and embryonic tissues.
• **Role of Oxygen in Germination**: Oxygen is vital for cellular respiration within the germinating embryo, supplying the chemical energy needed for rapid cell division and radical growth.
• **Role of Temperature in Germination**: A suitable warmth or optimal temperature range activates the specific enzymes required to break down stored food reserves in the seed.
• **Stages of Seed Germination**: The radicle (embryonic root) is the first structure to emerge through the ruptured seed coat, anchoring downward into the soil to absorb water and nutrients.
• **Emergence of Plumule**: Following the radicle, the plumule (embryonic shoot) grows upward against gravity toward the soil surface, unfolding the first green seed leaves to begin photosynthesis.
• **Hypogeal Germination**: In this type of germination, the cotyledons remain completely buried underground while the epicotyl elongates, pushing the plumule upward (e.g., pea, gram, maize).
