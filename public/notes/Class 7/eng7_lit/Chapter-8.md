• **Chapter Context**: Welcome to the DAV Dictionary academic study notes for Class 7 English Literature, focusing on Chapter 8, the classic cautionary poem "The Spider and the Fly" authored by Mary Bothitt.
• **Literary Classification**: This chapter is presented as a narrative cautionary verse that utilizes anthropomorphism, where animals possess human traits, to deliver a timeless moral lesson.
• **Core Theme**: The central theme revolves around the dangers of flattery, vanity, manipulation, and the importance of resisting sweet words from deceitful individuals.
• **Target Audience Alignment**: Designed specifically for Class 7 students under the DAV Public School Board curriculum to enhance literary appreciation, critical thinking, and moral understanding.
• **Authorial Intent**: Mary Bothitt wrote this poem to caution young, impressionable minds against falling prey to smooth-talking tricksters who use charm to mask malicious intentions.
• **Structural Layout**: The poem is structured as a dialogue between a cunning spider and an initially cautious, but ultimately gullible, fly across seven distinct narrative stanzas.
• **Character Dynamic - The Spider**: Represents the archetype of the manipulative predator who uses patience, psychological tactics, and false praise rather than brute force.
• **Character Dynamic - The Fly**: Represents the naive, vain individual who is susceptible to flattery, lowering her guard incrementally until it is too late to escape.
• **Setting the Scene**: The poem opens with the spider inviting the fly into his parlour, establishing an immediate atmosphere of suspense and deceptive hospitality.
• **The Parlour Metaphor**: The spider’s web is euphemistically referred to as a "parlour" and a "winding stair," masking a lethal trap as a cozy, welcoming domestic space.
• **First Line Analysis**: The famous opening line, *"Will you walk into my parlour?" said the spider to the fly*, sets the conversational yet sinister tone of the entire narrative.
• **The Fly’s Initial Resistance**: In the beginning, the fly wisely refuses the invitation, recognizing that anyone who enters the spider's web never returns.
• **Recognition of Danger**: The fly acknowledges the physical reality of the web, stating that those who go up the winding stair do not come down again to tell the tale.
• **Tactical Shift by the Spider**: Realizing that direct invitation fails, the spider shifts his strategy from persistence to psychological warfare using flattery and compliments.
• **Exploitation of Vanity**: The spider praises the fly's physical beauty, specifically focusing on her gauzy wings, brilliant eyes, and crested head to appeal to her ego.

• **The Final Trap Closing**: Convinced by the sweet words, the foolish fly prepares to accept the invitation, marching directly into the jaws of danger.

• **Stanza 5 Breakdown**: The fly, deeply touched by the praise of her beauty, begins to think of her own loveliness and lowers her guard.
• **The Dangerous Transformation**: The fly moves from cautious observer to willing participant, blinded by the ego boost provided by her enemy.
• **Stanza 6 Breakdown**: The spider seals the deal, singing of her brilliant eyes, delicate wings, and robes, urging her to come up and look at herself in his looking-glass.
• **The Looking-Glass Metaphor**: The spider uses a mirror as a final enticement, appealing to the fly's vanity and desire for self-admiration.
• **Stanza 7 Breakdown**: The tragic climax occurs where the foolish fly comes wheeling near, gets caught, and is dragged up the winding stair to the spider's den.
• **Final Warning Stanza**: The narrator delivers the ultimate moral takeaway, urging children to close their ears to flattering tongues and take heed of this story.
