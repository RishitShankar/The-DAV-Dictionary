• **Course Introduction:** Welcome to The DAV Dictionary Academic Notes for Class 5 English Practice Book, focusing on Chapter 12: Emphasising and Reflexive Pronouns.
• **Academic Objective:** This comprehensive study module is meticulously designed for DAV Class 5 students to master the theoretical concepts, grammatical functions, and practical applications of emphasizing and reflexive pronouns.
• **Core Definition of Pronoun:** A **Pronoun** is fundamentally defined as a word that is used in place of a noun to avoid unnecessary repetition within sentences and to ensure linguistic fluidity.
• **Introduction to Special Pronouns:** Chapter 12 specifically introduces specialized pronouns that end in the suffixes **-self** (for singular nouns) and **-selves** (for plural nouns).
• **Compound Personal Pronouns:** Grammatically, both reflexive and emphasizing pronouns are structurally categorized as **Compound Personal Pronouns** because they are formed by combining personal pronouns with the words 'self' or 'selves'.
• **Root Personal Pronouns:** The formation of these special pronouns relies directly on first-person, second-person, and third-person personal pronouns in both subjective and objective cases.
• **Singular Suffix Rule:** The singular suffix **-self** is strictly attached to singular base words such as *my*, *your*, *him*, *her*, and *it*.
• **Plural Suffix Rule:** The plural suffix **-selves** is strictly attached to plural base words and second-person pronouns such as *our*, *your* (plural), and *them*.
• **First Person Singular Formation:** Combining the possessive/objective base *my* with the suffix *-self* creates the singular first-person compound pronoun **myself**.
• **First Person Plural Formation:** Combining the possessive base *our* with the suffix *-selves* creates the plural first-person compound pronoun **ourselves**.
• **Second Person Singular Formation:** Combining the possessive base *your* with the suffix *-self* creates the singular second-person compound pronoun **yourself**.
• **Second Person Plural Formation:** Combining the possessive base *your* with the suffix *-selves* creates the plural second-person compound pronoun **yourselves**.
• **Third Person Masculine Formation:** Combining the objective pronoun *him* with the suffix *-self* creates the third-person masculine singular compound pronoun **himself**.
• **Third Person Feminine Formation:** Combining the objective pronoun *her* with the suffix *-self* creates the third-person feminine singular compound pronoun **herself**.
• **Third Person Neuter Formation:** Combining the possessive/objective base *it* with the suffix *-self* creates the third-person neuter singular compound pronoun **itself**.
• **Third Person Plural Formation:** Combining the objective pronoun *them* with the suffix *-selves* creates the third-person plural compound pronoun **themselves**.

• **Dual Nature of Compound Pronouns:** The central grammatical mechanism of Chapter 12 is understanding that the exact same list of words (*myself*, *yourself*, *himself*, *herself*, *itself*, *ourselves*, *yourselves*, *themselves*) serves two entirely different functions in English grammar.
• **Function One:** The first major grammatical role explored in this chapter is the **Reflexive Pronoun**, where the action done by the subject turns back upon or reflects upon the subject itself.
• **Function Two:** The second major grammatical role explored in this chapter is the **Emphasising Pronoun** (also known as an intensive pronoun), which is used purely to place strong stress or emphasis upon a noun or another pronoun.
• **Reflexive Pronoun Definition:** A **Reflexive Pronoun** is used when the subject and the object of a sentence refer to the exact same person or thing.
• **Subject-Object Identity:** In a reflexive construction, the person performing the action (subject) is simultaneously the receiver of that action (object).
• **Structural Position of Reflexive Pronouns:** Reflexive pronouns universally occupy the **object position** within a sentence, typically appearing immediately following the main verb or a preposition.
• **Essential Sentence Component:** Unlike emphasizing pronouns, reflexive pronouns are grammatically **indispensable**; removing a reflexive pronoun often changes the meaning of the sentence or renders it ungrammatical because transitive verbs require an object.
• **Emphasising Pronoun Definition:** An **Emphasising Pronoun** is utilized exclusively to draw special attention to a noun or pronoun that has just been mentioned, highlighting that the entity did something entirely on its own without external help.
• **Structural Position of Emphasising Pronouns:** Emphasising pronouns are typically placed **immediately after the noun or pronoun** they are emphasizing, or alternatively at the very end of the clause.
• **Optional Sentence Component:** Unlike reflexive pronouns, emphasizing pronouns are grammatically **optional**; if removed from the sentence, the core grammatical structure and basic meaning remain completely intact.
• **Diagnostic Test for Distinction:** DAV students can easily differentiate between the two types by applying the **removal test**: if the pronoun can be removed without breaking the sentence, it is an emphasizing pronoun; if removing it makes the sentence nonsensical or changes its meaning, it is reflexive.
• **Action Reflexivity:** The term 'reflexive' originates from the Latin root *reflectere*, meaning 'to bend back', perfectly illustrating how the action of the verb 'bends back' to the doer.
• **Self-Reliance Concept:** Emphasising pronouns heavily convey the semantic nuance of independence, autonomy, and personal execution (*"by oneself"* or *"without assistance"*).
• **Agreement Rule:** Both reflexive and emphasizing pronouns must strictly agree in **person, number, and gender** with the noun or pronoun they relate to.
• **Gender Specificity:** Third-person singular forms (*himself*, *herself*, *itself*) require DAV learners to carefully analyze the biological or grammatical gender of the antecedent noun.
• **Number Specificity:** Selecting between singular *-self* and plural *-selves* depends entirely on whether the antecedent noun represents a single individual or multiple entities.

• **Detailed Study of Reflexive Pronouns:** We now transition into an in-depth examination of **Reflexive Pronouns** as outlined in the Class 5 DAV English Practice Book.
• **Core Rule of Reflexivity:** A reflexive pronoun must always corefer with the subject of the exact same clause in which it is located.
* **First Person Singular Example (Reflexive):** In the sentence *"I hurt **myself** while playing football,"* the pronoun *myself* is reflexive because 'I' (subject) and 'myself' (object) refer to the same person.
• **Verb Transitivity:** Many reflexive verbs in English are transitive actions where the doer performs the action directly upon their own body or mind (e.g., *cut, wash, hurt, prep, look*).
• **First Person Plural Example (Reflexive):** Consider the sentence *"We locked **ourselves** out of the house by mistake,"* where *ourselves* reflects the action of locking back onto the plural subject *we*.
• **Second Person Singular Example (Reflexive):** In the imperative or declarative sentence *"You should look at **yourself** in the mirror,"* the pronoun *yourself* functions reflexively as the object of the prepositional phrase or verb.
• **Second Person Plural Example (Reflexive):** When addressing a group of students, the teacher says, *"Children, you must behave **yourselves** during the museum tour,"* where *yourselves* acts as a plural reflexive object.
• **Third Person Masculine Example (Reflexive):** In the sentence *"The old man talked to **himself** while walking down the quiet street,"* *himself* indicates that the man is both the speaker and the listener.
• **Third Person Feminine Example (Reflexive):** Consider the sentence *"She prepared **herself** for the upcoming annual school debate competition,"* where *herself* reflects the act of preparation back to the female subject.
• **Third Person Neuter Example (Reflexive):** For inanimate or animal subjects, we use *itself*, as seen in *"The clever cat cleaned **itself** meticulously after eating."*
• **Third Person Plural Example (Reflexive):** In the plural narrative context, DAV students note sentences like *"The thieves hid **themselves** behind the thick bushes before the police arrived."*
• **Prepositional Reflexive Structures:** Reflexive pronouns frequently appear directly after prepositions that link the subject's action back to itself, such as *by oneself, proud of oneself, talking to oneself*.
• **Absence of Direct Objects:** Verbs that naturally take an identity object often require a reflexive pronoun in English where other languages might omit it.
• **Common Reflexive Pitfalls:** DAV students must avoid using standard personal pronouns (*me, him, her, us, them*) in place of reflexive pronouns when the subject and object are identical.
• **Incorrect vs. Correct Analysis:** Saying *"He cut him with the knife"* implies he cut another person, whereas saying *"He cut **himself** with the knife"* correctly indicates a reflexive action upon his own body.
• **Pedagogical Note on Reflexives:** DAV practice exercises emphasize identifying the subject first, locating the verb, and then verifying whether the pronoun following the verb bounces the action back to that subject.

• **Detailed Study of Emphasising Pronouns:** We now shift our comprehensive focus to **Emphasising Pronouns** (also known as intensive pronouns), the second major linguistic category in Chapter 12.
• **Definition of Emphasis:** An **Emphasising Pronoun** is a compound personal pronoun used strictly to add dramatic force, stress, or emphasis to a noun or a personal pronoun in the sentence.
• **Intensive Nature:** Because they intensely highlight the subject's direct involvement in an action, grammarians often refer to them technically as **intensive pronouns**.
• **Immediate Juxtaposition:** In standard DAV sentence structures, emphasizing pronouns are placed immediately after the noun or pronoun they modify to maximize their rhetorical impact.
• **First Person Singular Example (Emphasising):** In the sentence *"I **myself** baked this delicious chocolate cake for the school fete,"* the word *myself* emphasizes that no bakery or parent helped the speaker.
• **First Person Plural Example (Emphasising):** Consider the sentence *"We **ourselves** cleaned up the entire classroom after the art and craft session,"* highlighting collective, unassisted labor.
• **Second Person Singular Example (Emphasising):** In the sentence *"You **yourself** admitted to breaking the window pane,"* the pronoun *yourself* places heavy, undeniable stress on the listener's personal admission.
• **Second Person Plural Example (Emphasising):** When addressing a sports team, a coach might say, *"You **yourselves** wanted to participate in this rigorous tournament, so you must practice harder."*
• **Third Person Masculine Example (Emphasising):** In the literary sentence *"The king **himself** distributed the gold coins to the poor villagers,"* *himself* stresses the high rank and direct personal action of the monarch.
• **Third Person Feminine Example (Emphasising):** Consider the sentence *"The principal **herself** inspected the DAV science laboratory,"* showing that the highest authority took direct, unmediated action.
• **Third Person Neuter Example (Emphasising):** For non-human entities, DAV students examine sentences such as *"The old computer **itself** deleted all the unsaved files without any warning."*
• **Third Person Plural Example (Emphasising):** In descriptive writing, note the sentence *"The villagers **themselves** built the wooden bridge across the rushing mountain river."*
• **End-Position Emphasising Pronouns:** Emphasising pronouns can also be shifted to the very end of the clause for dramatic effect, such as *"I solved this complex mathematical puzzle **myself**."*
• **Semantic Equivalent of 'Alone' or 'Without Help':** When analyzing emphasizing pronouns in the DAV English Practice Book, students should recognize that they carry the underlying meaning of doing something entirely unaided.
• **Contrast with Reflexive Position:** DAV learners must note that while reflexive pronouns are anchored to the object slot, emphasizing pronouns are untethered grammatical modifiers that can be lifted out of the sentence structure completely.
• **Avoiding Confusion:** Mastering the structural placement rules is the absolute key to preventing confusion between reflexive and emphasizing pronouns in DAV examinations.

• **Comparative Analysis (Reflexive vs. Emphasising):** A master-level skill required in DAV Class 5 English is the ability to instantly compare and contrast reflexive and emphasizing pronouns within identical lexical forms.
• **Identical Word Forms:** DAV students must remember that the exact same words (*myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves*) belong to both grammatical categories depending entirely on syntax.
• **Syntax Dictates Category:** The grammatical category of a compound personal pronoun is never determined by the word itself, but strictly by its **syntactic position** and **functional relationship** to the verb and subject.
• **Comparison Point 1: Grammatical Role:** Reflexive pronouns function strictly as the **direct object, indirect object, or object of a preposition**, whereas emphasizing pronouns function purely as **appositives or intensive modifiers**.
• **Comparison Point 2: Sentence Necessity:** Reflexive pronouns are **obligatory** elements required by the argument structure of transitive/prepositional verbs; emphasizing pronouns are **optional** rhetorical flourishes.
• **Comparison Point 3: Movement Flexibility:** Reflexive pronouns cannot be moved around the sentence without destroying grammatical correctness; emphasizing pronouns can often be placed either right after the subject or at the end of the sentence.
• **Side-by-Side Example A (Reflexive):** *"He cut **himself** with DAV craft scissors."* (Here, *himself* is reflexive because removing it leaves the transitive verb *cut* without a necessary object: *"He cut with scissors"* changes the meaning entirely).
• **Side-by-Side Example B (Emphasising):** *"He **himself** repaired the broken craft scissors."* (Here, *himself* is emphasizing because removing it leaves a grammatically complete and coherent sentence: *"He repaired the broken craft scissors"*).
• **Diagnostic Question 1:** To determine if a pronoun is reflexive, DAV students must ask: *"Does the object receive the action performed by the subject?"* If yes, it is reflexive.
• **Diagnostic Question 2:** To determine if a pronoun is emphasizing, DAV students must ask: *"Is this word simply stressing the subject and can it be deleted?"* If yes, it is emphasizing.
• **Semantic Nuance Comparison:** Reflexive pronouns describe an action turning inward; emphasizing pronouns describe an action performed with exclusive personal agency.
• **Tabular Mental Framework:** DAV teachers recommend visualizing a mental table where Column A lists Subject Antecedents, Column B lists Reflexive Sentences with object functions, and Column C lists Emphasising Sentences with modifier functions.
• **Grammatical Integrity Test:** If a DAV student crosses out the compound pronoun in a sentence and the sentence collapses into grammatical error, it is invariably a **reflexive pronoun**.
• **Stylistic Enrichment Test:** If a DAV student crosses out the compound pronoun and the sentence remains 100% grammatically pristine while losing only a shade of rhetorical emphasis, it is invariably an **emphasising pronoun**.
• **Common Student Error:** A frequent mistake in Class 5 tests is mislabeling an emphasizing pronoun placed at the end of a sentence (*"I built the model aeroplane **myself**"*) as a reflexive pronoun; DAV notes clarify that end-placed pronouns of this type are emphasizing.
• **Summary of Distinction:** Understanding this dual taxonomy elevates a Class 5 student's grammatical precision, preparing them perfectly for advanced syntax in middle and high school DAV curricula.

• **Prerequisite Knowledge:** Before tackling Chapter 12, DAV students must possess a firm, working knowledge of **personal pronouns** (I, you, he, she, it, we, they) and their objective/possessive cases.
• **Understanding Antecedents:** An **antecedent** is defined in DAV grammar as the specific noun or pronoun that a pronoun refers back to; mastering antecedents is vital for pronoun agreement in Chapter 12.
• **Person Agreement Rules:** DAV exercises require strict adherence to person agreement—never mix first-person subjects with second or third-person compound pronouns (e.g., *I* must match with *myself*, never with *himself*).
• **Gender Agreement Rules:** Third-person singular antecedents demand rigorous gender matching: male nouns take *himself*, female nouns take *herself*, and neutral/inanimate nouns take *itself*.
• **Number Agreement Rules:** Singular nouns (*child, dog, leader*) demand *-self* suffixes, while plural nouns (*children, dogs, leaders*) demand *-selves* suffixes without exception.

• **Contextual Clues in Paragraphs:** When solving DAV workbook passages, students are trained to scan the surrounding sentence for subject cues (like pronouns at the start of the sentence) to deduce the correct compound pronoun.
• **Imperative Sentence Nuance:** In imperative sentences (commands or requests where the subject 'you' is implied), the reflexive or emphasizing pronoun must always be **yourself** or **yourselves**.
• **Imperative Example:** Consider the DAV workbook sentence: *"Please help **yourself** to the snacks, Rohan."* Here, the implied subject is 'you', making *yourself* the correct reflexive choice.
• **Reflexive Verbs in DAV Vocabulary:** DAV practice books frequently feature specific verbs that regularly take reflexive pronouns, such as *enjoy, hurt, pride, absent, avail, adapt, exert, introduce*.

• **Punctuation and Spelling Check:** A critical mechanical focus in DAV grading is correct spelling; students often misspell *ourselves* as *ourself* or *themselves* as *theirself* (which is a severe grammatical error).

• **Advanced Syntactic Analysis:** To achieve top academic performance in DAV examinations, students must explore how compound pronouns interact with complex verb phrases.
• **Compound Subjects and Pronouns:** When a sentence features a compound subject (e.g., *"Rahul and I"*), DAV students must carefully select the corresponding plural compound pronoun (**ourselves**) for reflexive or emphasizing use.
• **Compound Subject Example:** Examine the DAV-style sentence: *"Rahul and I drove **ourselves** to the DAV science exhibition."* Here, the joint subject requires the first-person plural reflexive form *ourselves*.
• **Mixed Person Compound Subjects:** When a compound subject combines second and third persons, DAV grammar rules dictate specific pronoun resolution, though elementary Class 5 exercises keep these structures straightforward and transparent.
• **Reflexive Pronouns as Indirect Objects:** In advanced sentence patterns, reflexive pronouns can act as indirect objects following verbs of giving or getting, such as *"She bought **herself** a new set of DAV colored pencils."*
• **Beneficiary Reflexives:** When the subject performs an action that benefits the subject directly, the reflexive pronoun often functions as a benefactive object, emphasizing self-reliance.
• **Emphasizing Pronouns with Proper Nouns:** Emphasising pronouns frequently modify proper names of historical figures, leaders, or school authorities to add prestige and stress direct action.
• **Proper Noun Example:** *"Mahatma Gandhi **himself** preached the core values of truth and non-violence that inspire our DAV ethos today."*
• **Emphasizing Pronouns with Common Nouns:** They can also modify common nouns representing groups or occupations, as in *"The DAV principal **herself** congratulated the young scholars."*
• **Reflexive Passives Caution:** DAV students are taught that reflexive pronouns rarely appear in true passive voice constructions because the subject of a passive sentence is the receiver, not the doer, of the action.
• **Infinitive and Gerund Phrases:** Compound pronouns can sometimes follow non-finite verb forms like infinitives (*to help oneself*) or gerunds (*praised for helping oneself*), maintaining strict coreferential links.
• **Idiomatic Expressions:** Chapter 12 introduces common English idioms built around reflexive pronouns that DAV students must memorize for fluency.
• **Idiom 1 - 'By oneself':** Means completely alone or without assistance (e.g., *"Aarav solved the difficult crossword puzzle **by himself**"*).
• **Idiom 2 - 'Of oneself':** Means spontaneously or naturally without external push (e.g., *"The automatic sliding doors opened **of themselves**"*).
• **Idiom 3 - 'Beside oneself':** Means overcome with extreme emotion, usually grief, anger, or joy (e.g., *"Mother was **beside herself** with joy upon hearing my DAV academic results"*).
• **Pedagogical Value of Idioms:** Learning these idiomatic uses expands a Class 5 student's vocabulary beyond mechanical grammar drills into rich, expressive literary communication.

• **Common Errors and Troubleshooting:** A dedicated study section in our DAV notes addresses the frequent pitfalls and mistakes made by students in pronoun chapters.
• **Error Type 1 - Object Pronoun Substitution:** Students often incorrectly use objective pronouns (*me, him, her*) instead of reflexive pronouns when the subject and object are identical.
* **Correction of Error Type 1:** Incorrect: *"John washed him after playing in the mud."* Correct: *"John washed **himself** after playing in the mud."*
• **Error Type 2 - Non-Existent Suffixes:** Students frequently invent ungrammatical words by attaching *-self* to possessive determiners improperly, such as writing *hisself* or *theirself*.
• **Correction of Error Type 2:** DAV dictionaries strictly mandate the correct historical forms: **himself** (never *hisself*) and **themselves** (never *theirself*).
• **Error Type 3 - Plural Mismatch:** Students frequently use singular *-self* with plural third-person antecedents, writing *themself* instead of the correct plural form *themselves*.
• **Correction of Error Type 3:** Always ensure plural subjects (*the students, the teachers, the books*) link exclusively to plural compound pronouns ending in **-selves**.
• **Error Type 4 - Unnecessary Reflexives:** Students sometimes tack reflexive pronouns onto intransitive verbs that cannot take objects, creating awkward or ungrammatical sentences.
• **Correction of Error Type 4:** Incorrect: *"She vanished herself into the thin air."* Correct: *"She vanished into the thin air"* (since *vanish* is an intransitive verb and cannot take a reflexive object).
• **Error Type 5 - Confusing Reflexive and Emphasising:** Misidentifying the grammatical role during parsing exercises; DAV students must rigorously apply the deletion test to avoid this trap.
• **Error Type 6 - Misplaced Emphasising Pronouns:** Placing an emphasizing pronoun too far away from its antecedent, which creates ambiguity in sentence meaning.
• **Correction of Error Type 6:** Incorrect: *"Myself, I completed the DAV homework."* (While colloquial in some regions, standard DAV grammar prefers *"I myself completed the DAV homework"*).
• **Systematic Proofreading Strategy:** DAV teachers advise students to use a three-step proofreading checklist for Chapter 12: 
  1. Check the suffix (*-self* vs *-selves*).
  2. Check the person/gender agreement with the subject.
  3. Check the syntactic function (reflexive object vs emphasizing modifier).
• **Mastery Checklist:** By eliminating these six common errors, a Class 5 student achieves flawless execution in all DAV English Practice Book assessments.
