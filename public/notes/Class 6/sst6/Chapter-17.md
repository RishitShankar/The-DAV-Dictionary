• **Chapter Title**: Chapter 17 - "The Era of Harsha".
• **Historical Context**: The chapter explores the political, social, economic, and cultural developments in India during the 7th century CE following the decline of the Gupta Empire.
• **Decline of the Guptas**: The golden age of the Guptas gradually disintegrated due to weak successors and continuous invasions by the Hunas (Huns).
• **Political Fragmentation**: Northern India broke up into numerous small, independent kingdoms, leading to political instability and constant warfare among regional chieftains.
• **Rise of the Pushyabhuti Dynasty**: Out of the political chaos, the Pushyabhuti family rose to power in Thanesar (modern-day Haryana), laying the foundation for a powerful empire.
• **Early Rulers**: The early rulers of the Pushyabhuti dynasty included Prabhakaravardhana, who strengthened the kingdom and assumed the title of *Maharajadhiraja*.
• **Prabhakaravardhana's Successors**: He had two sons, Rajyavardhana and Harsha, and a daughter, Rajyashri, whose marriage forged a strategic alliance with the Maukhari rulers of Kanauj.
• **Tragic Turn of Events**: The king of Malwa, aided by the ruler of Bengal (Sasanka), murdered Rajyashri's husband (Grahavarma) and imprisoned her, plunging the kingdom into crisis.
• **Assassination of Rajyavardhana**: When Rajyavardhana marched to rescue his sister, he was treacherously killed by Sasanka, the king of Gauda (Bengal).
• **Accession of Harsha**: In 606 CE, at the young age of sixteen, Harshavardhana ascended the throne of Thanesar, facing immediate military and political challenges.
• **Dual Responsibility**: Harsha rescued his sister Rajyashri from the Vindhya forests just as she was about to commit *sati*, and took over the administration of Kanauj as well.
• **Unification of Crowns**: Harsha merged the kingdoms of Thanesar and Kanauj, shifting his capital permanently to Kanauj, which soon became the political and cultural hub of North India.
• **Imperial Expansion**: Harsha embarked on a military campaign to unite Northern India, waging campaigns for six years until he established undisputed supremacy over the northern plains.
• **Encounter with Pulakeshin II**: Harsha's southward expansion was checked on the banks of the river Narmada by the mighty Chalukya ruler, Pulakeshin II, around 630 CE.
• **Empire's Boundaries**: Harsha's empire eventually extended from the Himalayas in the north to the Narmada River in the south, and from Assam in the east to Gujarat in the west.

• **Primary Sources**: Information about Harsha's reign comes primarily from contemporary literary texts, inscriptions, and foreign accounts, making it one of the best-documented periods of ancient India.
• **Banabhatta**: The greatest literary source for this period is the *Harshacharita*, a Sanskrit biography of Harsha written by his court poet, Banabhatta.
• **Style of Harshacharita**: Banabhatta’s work is written in *Kavya* style, praising Harsha's virtues and achievements, while also providing invaluable historical glimpses into social life.
• **Banabhatta’s Other Work**: Banabhatta also authored *Kadambari*, a celebrated romantic novel in Sanskrit literature that showcases the high development of classical Sanskrit prose.
• **Harsha as a Playwright**: Harsha himself was a gifted scholar and dramatist who composed three famous Sanskrit plays: *Ratnavali*, *Priyadarsika*, and *Nagananda*.
• **Theme of Nagananda**: *Nagananda* tells the inspiring Buddhist-inspired story of Jimutavahana, who sacrificed his own life to save a naga (serpent) from the mythical bird Garuda.
• **Xuanzang (Hsuan Tsang)**: The most detailed foreign account of Harsha's reign comes from the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang, who visited India between 629 and 645 CE.
• **Title of Xuanzang’s Work**: Xuanzang recorded his observations in his famous travelogue, *Si-yu-ki* (Buddhist Records of the Western World), which serves as an authentic historical window.
• **Purpose of Xuanzang's Visit**: Xuanzang came to India to study authentic Buddhist scriptures at the prestigious Nalanda University and to collect sacred manuscripts to take back to China.
• **Xuanzang’s Relationship with Harsha**: Harsha held Xuanzang in high esteem, patronized him, and even invited him to participate in major religious assemblies organized by the emperor.
• **Inscriptions as Evidence**: Epigraphical sources, such as the Madhuban and Banskhera copper plate inscriptions, provide authentic official records of land grants and administrative orders issued by Harsha.
• **Seal of Harsha**: The copper plate inscriptions bear the royal seal of Harsha, featuring the bull emblem (*Nandi*) and authenticating the royal authority of the Pushyabhuti monarch.
• **Numismatic Evidence**: Coins issued during Harsha's reign, featuring portraits and Brahmi inscriptions, help corroborate his regnal dates and economic policies.
• **Banskhera Inscription**: This specific inscription provides a specimen of Harsha's own handwriting, indicating that the emperor was well-educated and proficient in calligraphy.
• **Aihole Inscription**: Composed by Ravikirti, the court poet of Pulakeshin II, this inscription gives the Chalukya perspective on the famous battle between Harsha and Pulakeshin II.
• **Synthesis of Sources**: Combining literary texts like the *Harshacharita*, foreign travelogues like Xuanzang’s, and archaeological inscriptions allows historians to reconstruct a balanced view of Harsha's era.

• **Central Administration**: Harsha was an absolute monarch, but unlike some despots, he viewed his royal duties as a form of public service and tirelessly worked for the welfare of his subjects.
• **Daily Routine**: According to Banabhatta and Xuanzang, Harsha divided his day into strict schedules, dedicating time equally to state administration, religious duties, and personal welfare.
• **Council of Ministers**: The king was assisted by a council of high-ranking ministers and officials (*Mantris*) who advised him on matters of war, peace, taxation, and domestic policy.
• **Administrative Hierarchy**: The empire was divided into provinces called *Bhuktis*, which were further subdivided into districts called *Vishayas*.
• **Sub-divisions of Vishayas**: Districts (*Vishayas*) were further split into smaller administrative units like *Pathakas* and ultimately into villages (*Gramas*), which were the base of administration.
• **Local Administration**: Village administration was managed by local headmen (*Gramikas*) and village elders, who maintained law and order and collected local revenues.
• **Provincial Governors**: *Bhuktis* were governed by high-ranking officials, often royal princes or trusted nobles, known as *Uparikas*.
• **Taxation System**: The main source of state revenue was land tax (*Bhaga*), which usually amounted to one-sixth of the total agricultural produce.
• **Other Taxes**: Additional revenues came from trade taxes, tolls levied at ferries and roads, and mandatory commercial duties imposed on merchants.
• **Public Expenditure**: The state revenue was divided into four equal parts: one for running the government, one for paying royal officials, one for rewarding scholars and artists, and one for religious charities.
• **Forced Labor (*Vishti*)**: Although taxes were relatively moderate, certain communities were subjected to unpaid forced labor (*Vishti*) for public works and military transport.
• **Judicial System**: The legal system under Harsha was strict, but overall punishments were considered milder than those in the Mauryan period, though severe crimes faced mutilation or exile.
• **Trial Procedures**: Ordeals by fire, water, and poison were sometimes used to determine the guilt or innocence of the accused, reflecting the religious customs of the era.
• **Law and Order**: Law and order were generally well-maintained across the empire, though Xuanzang occasionally noted instances of highway robbery, which he attributed to the rugged terrain.
• **Charitable Works**: Harsha utilized state funds and personal wealth to build rest houses (*Dharmsalas*), hospitals, and feeding centers for travelers, the poor, and the destitute.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Military Organization**: Harsha maintained a vast, well-organized standing army to maintain internal security and defend the vast frontiers of his empire against foreign aggressors.
• **Four-Fold Army (*Chaturanga*)**: The military comprised the traditional four divisions: infantry (foot soldiers), cavalry (horsemen), war chariots, and a massive elephant corps.
• **Expansion of the Elephant Corps**: The elephant corps was considered the most decisive and formidable branch of Harsha's army, symbolizing imperial might and tactical strength.
• **Cavalry Strength**: Horses were imported from Central Asia and Arabia to maintain a superior cavalry force, which was crucial for rapid deployment across the northern plains.
• **Military Leadership**: Harsha himself acted as the commander-in-chief of his forces, personally leading military campaigns and boosting the morale of his soldiers on the battlefield.
• **Military Camps (*Skandhavaras*)**: During campaigns, Harsha stayed in temporary imperial military camps (*Skandhavaras*) that functioned as mobile administrative capitals equipped with full governmental machinery.
• **Economic Prosperity**: Agriculture remained the backbone of the economy, supported by fertile soil in the Gangetic plains and advanced irrigation techniques like wells, tanks, and canals.
• **Major Crops**: The Gangetic valley produced abundant yields of rice, wheat, sugarcane, barley, oilseeds, and a wide variety of fruits and vegetables.
• **Trade and Commerce**: Internal trade flourished with goods moving freely along well-connected river routes, particularly the Ganges, and overland trade networks linking major cities.
• **Urban Centers**: Cities like Kanauj, Mathura, Prayag (Allahabad), and Thanesar emerged as bustling commercial hubs filled with bustling markets and wealthy merchants.
• **Guilds (*Shrenis*)**: Trade and artisan guilds played a vital role in regulating prices, ensuring product quality, and training apprentices in various crafts and industries.
• **Foreign Trade**: India maintained active maritime and overland trade links with Southeast Asia, China, Persia, and the Byzantine Empire, exporting textiles, spices, and precious stones.
• **Medium of Exchange**: Gold and silver coins were minted, though barter and cowrie shells were also commonly used for day-to-day transactions among ordinary villagers.
• **Crafts and Industries**: Textile manufacturing, metalworking, pottery, and ivory carving reached high levels of sophistication, catering to both domestic demands and lucrative export markets.
• **Economic Status of Classes**: While cities were affluent and merchants lived in luxury, the rural peasantry bore the brunt of taxation, though basic food grains were reported to be cheap and abundant.

• **Social Structure**: Society during Harsha's reign was organized around the traditional caste system (*Varna Vyavastha*), though it was becoming increasingly rigid and complex.
• **Brahmins**: Brahmins held a position of high respect, receiving rent-free land grants (*Agraharas*) from the king and wealthy patrons in exchange for performing religious rituals and imparting education.
• **Kshatriyas**: Kshatriyas formed the ruling and military elite, responsible for administration, warfare, and defending the realm against external threats.
• **Vaishyas**: Vaishyas engaged primarily in agriculture, trade, commerce, and banking, contributing significantly to the economic vitality of the empire.
• **Shudras and Untouchables**: Shudras worked as laborers and artisans, while the lowest strata, including outcastes like the *Chandalas*, lived isolated lives on the fringes of cities and villages.
• **Position of Women**: The status of women witnessed a decline compared to the Vedic and early Gupta periods, though royal women often enjoyed high status and political influence.
• **Education for Women**: Women from elite and royal families received education in the arts, literature, and philosophy, as exemplified by Harsha’s sister Rajyashri, who was a learned scholar.
• **Social Evils**: Practices like child marriage, polygamy among the upper classes, and the restrictions on the remarriage of widows began to take root during this era.
• **The Practice of Sati**: *Sati* (the self-immolation of widows upon their husband's funeral pyre) was practiced among the royal and upper classes, as noted when Rajyashri nearly committed *sati*.
• **Observance of Purdah**: While strict seclusion (*Purdah*) was not yet universally enforced, modesty and veiling among upper-class women were beginning to emerge in royal courts.
• **Dietary Habits**: Vegetarianism was gaining popularity, especially under the influence of Buddhism and Jainism, though meat-eating was not strictly forbidden among non-religious populations.
• **Dress and Ornaments**: People wore garments made of cotton, silk, and wool; men wore stitched tunics and dhotis, while women draped elegant sarees and adorned themselves with rich jewelry.
• **Amusements and Entertainment**: Popular forms of entertainment included chariot races, animal fights, theatrical performances, music, dance, gambling, and outdoor sports like hunting.
• **Religious Tolerance**: Harsha was a remarkable model of religious tolerance, balancing his personal faith with generous patronage of multiple religious traditions coexisting peacefully.
• **Religious Transformation**: Harsha originally worshipped Shiva and the Sun God (*Surya*), but under the influence of his sister Rajyashri and Xuanzang, he later became a devout patron of Mahayana Buddhism.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Kanauj Assembly (643 CE)**: Harsha organized a grand religious assembly at Kanauj to honor Xuanzang and to propagate the doctrines of Mahayana Buddhism to scholars across India.
• **Scale of Kanauj Assembly**: According to Xuanzang, representatives from twenty tributary kings, thousands of Buddhist monks, Brahmins, and Jains attended the magnificent gathering.
• **Golden Statue**: A life-sized golden statue of the Buddha was installed on a towering tower at the assembly site, reflecting the immense wealth and religious devotion of the emperor.
• **Security and Procession**: Elaborate security arrangements were made, and daily processions featured an ornate image of the Buddha carried on a decorated elephant led by Harsha himself.
• **The Prayag (Allahabad) Assembly**: Every five years, Harsha hosted a massive charity assembly at the confluence of the Ganga, Yamuna, and mythical Saraswati rivers at Prayag (Mahamoksha Parishad).
• **Tradition of Giving**: True to the Buddhist ideal of generosity (*Dana*), Harsha gave away all accumulated imperial wealth, gold, jewels, and royal garments to the poor, monks, and needy.
• **Depleted Treasury**: After distributing everything in charity, Harsha would humbly ask his sister Rajyashri for ordinary, worn-out clothing, demonstrating supreme personal detachment from material wealth.
• **Frequency of Prayag**: Harsha performed this grand act of universal charity six times during his reign, marking it as a pinnacle of ancient Indian philanthropic traditions.
• **Decline of Buddhism in Northern India**: Although Harsha patronized Buddhism lavishly, Buddhism was already losing ground in India due to internal corruption, lack of popular roots, and the revival of Hinduism.
• **Hinduism’s Resurgence**: Puranic Hinduism, centered around the worship of Vishnu, Shiva, and Shakti, was gaining immense popularity among the masses, supported by royal patronage.
• **Nalanda University**: The greatest center of learning during Harsha's era was Nalanda University in modern-day Bihar, which reached its zenith under his imperial patronage.
• **Support for Nalanda**: Harsha revenues from two hundred villages were allocated to maintain Nalanda University, providing free food, clothing, books, and lodging to over 10,000 students and 2,000 teachers.
• **Admission Test**: Admission to Nalanda was extremely competitive; candidates had to pass a rigorous oral entrance examination administered by strict gatekeepers (*Dwarapanditas*).
• **Curriculum at Nalanda**: The curriculum encompassed Buddhist philosophy (both Hinayana and Mahayana), Vedas, logic (*Hetuvidya*), grammar (*Sabdavidya*), medicine (*Chikitsavidya*), and astronomy.
• **Method of Instruction**: Teaching at Nalanda was conducted through rigorous debates, discussions, lectures, and direct master-student interactions rather than rote memorization.

• **Architecture and Art**: While stone architecture of the Harsha period is relatively sparse compared to the Guptas, literary sources paint a vivid picture of magnificent palaces, temples, and monasteries.
• **Brick and Timber Usage**: Many structures built during Harsha’s reign utilized brick and timber extensively, which explains why fewer monumental structural ruins have survived to the modern day.
• **Sculptural Style**: Sculptures of this period retained the classical grace and refinement of the Gupta tradition, with serene facial expressions and intricately carved drapery.
• **Terracotta Art**: Terracotta figurines depicting deities, animals, and common people were immensely popular in domestic shrines and structural decorations across the Gangetic plains.
• **Painting Traditions**: Though surviving murals from Harsha's immediate court are rare, the artistic sophistication of the era is preserved in the contemporary rock-cut paintings of Ajanta and Bagh.
• **Kanauj as an Architectural Marvel**: Xuanzang described Kanauj as a heavily fortified city adorned with hundreds of lofty Buddhist viharas, Hindu temples, beautiful gardens, and majestic multi-storied palaces.
• **Role of Kanauj**: Kanauj replaced Pataliputra as the premier political and cultural capital of North India, becoming a symbol of imperial prestige that later triggered the Tripartite Struggle.
• **Literary Flourishing**: The royal court of Harsha served as a magnet for poets, scholars, philosophers, and artists from all parts of India, creating a vibrant cultural Renaissance.
• **Banabhatta’s Literary Circle**: Banabhatta’s circle included several gifted poets who enjoyed royal patronage and contributed to the rich corpus of classical Sanskrit literature.
• **Mayura and Dandin**: Celebrated literary figures like Mayura (author of *Mayurashataka*) and Dandin added immense brilliance to the cultural landscape of 7th-century India.
• **Spread of Indian Culture Abroad**: Cultural ties with China, Southeast Asia, and Central Asia deepened during Harsha's reign through diplomatic missions, Buddhist monks, and merchant caravans.
• **Diplomatic Exchanges**: Harsha exchanged official diplomatic embassies with the Tang Dynasty of China, sending envoys to Emperor Taizong and receiving Chinese ambassadors in return.
• **Cultural Diffusion**: Indian astronomy, mathematics, medicine, and Buddhist philosophy were transmitted to China and Tibet, profoundly influencing East Asian civilizations.
• **Tibet Connection**: Buddhism was firmly established in Tibet during this era through the efforts of Indian scholars and the Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo, who adopted elements of Indian script and culture.
• **Legacy of Scholarship**: The preservation of ancient texts through meticulous copying at institutions like Nalanda ensured that India's rich intellectual heritage survived turbulent centuries.

• **Character of Harsha**: Harsha is remembered in Indian history as a benevolent ruler, a valiant warrior, a patron of arts, a devout religious seeker, and an intellectual dramatist.
• **Exemplary Work Ethic**: Harsha’s personal dedication to governance set a high standard for royal duty, ensuring that his subjects experienced peace, justice, and general prosperity.
• **Patron of Learning**: His generous support for scholars, universities like Nalanda, and poets like Banabhatta cemented his legacy as one of ancient India’s most enlightened sovereigns.
• **Religious Broad-Mindedness**: Harsha's ability to revere Shiva, the Sun God, and the Buddha simultaneously reflects the intrinsic spirit of religious pluralism and tolerance in ancient Indian culture.
• **Charitable Spirit**: His quadrennial charity festivals at Prayag symbolize the ultimate expression of royal selflessness and philanthropic compassion recorded in world history.
• **Limitations of the Empire**: Despite his greatness, Harsha's empire was essentially a personal creation held together by his charismatic leadership and military strength rather than strong institutional integration.
• **Absence of a Successor**: Harsha died in 647 CE without leaving a capable male heir, which instantly plunged his newly unified empire back into political turmoil and fragmentation.
• **Immediate Aftermath of Death**: Following Harsha's death, his minister Arjuna usurped the throne and mistreated foreign emissaries, prompting a joint military intervention by the Tang Chinese and Tibetan forces.
• **End of the Pushyabhuti Dynasty**: With Arjuna’s defeat, the Pushyabhuti dynasty came to an abrupt end, and Northern India splintered once again into warring regional kingdoms.
• **Rise of Regional Powers**: The collapse of Harsha's empire paved the way for the emergence of powerful regional dynasties such as the Pratiharas, Palas, and Rashtrakutas.
• **Historical Significance of Kanauj**: Although the empire disintegrated, Kanauj retained its coveted status as the ultimate symbol of imperial sovereignty in Northern India for centuries to come.
• **Transition to the Early Medieval Period**: The Era of Harsha marks the transitional bridge between the classical ancient period (Gupta age) and the early medieval period of Indian history.
• **Feudal Tendencies**: The widespread practice of granting land to officials and religious institutions during Harsha's era accelerated the growth of feudalism (*Samanta* system) in India.
• **Autonomy of Samantas**: Local lords and feudatories (*Samantas*) gained increasing military power and autonomy, which weakened central royal authority and contributed to the empire's eventual fragility.
• **Historical Memory**: Harsha remains immortalized in historical texts as the last great Hindu emperor of unified Northern India before the onset of medieval Rajput and Islamic polities.

• **Chapter Summary**: The Era of Harsha (7th century CE) represents a vital chapter in Indian history characterized by political unification, cultural brilliance, and intellectual achievement.
• **Rise from Adversity**: Starting from the small kingdom of Thanesar, Harsha successfully navigated personal tragedies and geopolitical conflicts to establish an expansive North Indian empire.
• **Administrative Excellence**: His well-structured civil administration, balanced taxation, and welfare-oriented policies ensured domestic stability and economic prosperity across his domains.
• **Military Might**: Backed by a formidable four-fold army featuring a massive elephant corps and disciplined cavalry, Harsha maintained order across the Gangetic plains.
• **Cultural Renaissance**: The literary contributions of Harsha, Banabhatta, and contemporary poets transformed Kanauj into a radiant center of Sanskrit drama, poetry, and philosophy.
• **Foreign Accounts**: The detailed chronicles left by the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang provide modern historians with an authentic, vivid, and invaluable record of life in 7th-century India.
• **Educational Hubs**: The flourishing of Nalanda University as an international center of higher learning demonstrated India's pre-eminence in global education and intellectual exchange.
• **Religious Harmony**: Harsha's personal evolution from traditional worship to Mahayana Buddhism, coupled with his deep respect for Hinduism and Jainism, exemplified ancient India’s tradition of tolerance.
• **Charitable Legacy**: Events like the Kanauj and Prayag assemblies highlighted the supreme cultural values of hospitality, spiritual devotion, and radical philanthropy.
• **Ephemeral Unity**: Like many ancient empires, Harsha's domain was an ephemeral political construct dependent on strong personal leadership, dissolving rapidly after his death in 647 CE.
• **Evolution of Feudalism**: The era witnessed the subtle rise of the *Samanta* system and land grants, planting the seeds of Indian feudalism that would dominate the medieval political landscape.
• **Enduring Heritage**: Harsha's era endures in historical memory as a golden twilight of ancient Indian classical civilization, blending administrative vigor with profound spiritual and artistic expression.
• **Study Takeaway 1**: Students must remember that Harsha shifted his capital from Thanesar to Kanauj, making Kanauj the geopolitical center of Northern India.
• **Study Takeaway 2**: Students must identify Banabhatta (*Harshacharita*, *Kadambari*) and Xuanzang (*Si-yu-ki*) as the two most critical primary sources for studying this period.
• **Study Takeaway 3**: Students should appreciate Nalanda University's historical role as an international residential university funded by Harsha's royal patronage.

• **Key Terms - Harshacharita**: The historical biography of Harsha written in ornate Sanskrit prose by his court poet, Banabhatta.
• **Key Terms - Xuanzang**: The renowned Chinese Buddhist pilgrim and scholar who visited India during Harsha's reign and wrote *Si-yu-ki*.
• **Key Terms - Kanauj**: The strategic city on the Ganges that served as Harshavardhana's imperial capital and the cultural heart of Northern India.
• **Key Terms - Pulakeshin II**: The powerful Chalukya king of Vatapi who halted Harsha's southward expansion on the banks of the Narmada River.
• **Key Terms - Bhuktis**: The primary administrative provinces into which Harsha's empire was divided for efficient governance.
• **Key Terms - Vishayas**: The administrative districts into which provincial *Bhuktis* were subdivided under local officials.
• **Key Terms - Uparikas**: The high-ranking imperial governors appointed by the king to administer the provincial *Bhuktis*.
• **Key Terms - Samantas**: Feudal lords, tributary chiefs, and local nobles who held land grants and owed allegiance to the central emperor.
• **Key Terms - Nalanda University**: The ancient international center of Buddhist and secular learning in Bihar that flourished under Harsha's generous patronage.
• **Key Terms - Mahamoksha Parishad**: The grand quinquennial charity assembly hosted by Harsha at Prayag where he distributed all imperial wealth to the needy.
• **Key Terms - Skandhavaras**: Temporary imperial military camps that functioned as mobile administrative capitals during Harsha's military campaigns.
• **Key Terms - Vishti**: The system of unpaid forced labor extracted by the state or local lords for public works and military logistics.
• **Key Terms - Agraharas**: Tax-free land grants bestowed upon learned Brahmins, religious institutions, and scholars by the king and wealthy elites.
