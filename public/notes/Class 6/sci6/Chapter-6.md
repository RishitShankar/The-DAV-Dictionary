• **Chapter Introduction:** The chapter "Measurement and Motion" explores the fundamental concepts of how we quantify physical dimensions and how objects change their positions relative to their surroundings.
• **Historical Context of Measurement:** In ancient times, human body parts such as the cubit, foot-span, and hand-span were extensively used as standard units for measuring lengths and distances.
• **Limitations of Ancient Units:** Body parts vary from person to person in dimensions, leading to a lack of uniformity, ambiguity, and inconsistent trade practices globally.
• **Need for Standard Units:** To eliminate discrepancies in measurements and ensure universal acceptance, scientists established standardized systems of units that remain invariant across time and space.
• **Introduction to SI Units:** The International System of Units (SI), derived from the French *Système International d'unités*, is the modern metric system adopted globally for scientific and commercial measurements.
• **Fundamental Quantity - Length:** Length is defined as the distance between two points, with the SI base unit of length being the metre ($m$).
• **Sub-units of Length:** Smaller lengths are measured in centimetres ($cm$) and millimetres ($mm$), where $1\ m = 100\ cm$ and $1\ cm = 10\ mm$.
• **Larger Units of Length:** Long distances, such as those between cities, are measured in kilometres ($km$), where $1\ km = 1000\ m$.
• **Fundamental Quantity - Mass:** Mass represents the quantity of matter contained in a body, with its SI base unit being the kilogram ($kg$).
• **Sub-units of Mass:** Lighter objects are measured in grams ($g$) and milligrams ($mg$), where $1\ kg = 1000\ g$ and $1\ g = 1000\ mg$.
• **Fundamental Quantity - Time:** Time refers to the duration between two events, with its SI base unit being the second ($s$).
• **Larger Units of Time:** Extended durations are measured in minutes, hours, days, months, and years, forming the chronological framework of daily existence.
• **Fundamental Quantity - Temperature:** Temperature is a measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body, with the SI unit being the kelvin ($K$) and commonly measured in degree Celsius ($^\circ C$).
• **Derived Quantities:** Quantities such as area, volume, and speed are derived quantities because they are calculated using fundamental quantities like length, mass, and time.
• **Definition of Measurement:** Measurement is the process of comparing an unknown physical quantity with a known, fixed standard quantity called a unit.

• **Core Components of Measurement:** Every complete measurement must comprise two essential elements: a numerical value and a specific unit.
• **Incompleteness of Numerical Value Alone:** Stating that the length of a table is "5" is meaningless without specifying the unit, such as "5 metres" or "5 centimetres."
• **Incompleteness of Unit Alone:** Stating just "metres" without a numerical value provides no information about the magnitude of the measured object.
• **Rule of Proportionality:** The numerical value of a measurement is inversely proportional to the size of the unit chosen; smaller units yield larger numerical values for the same physical dimension.
• **Proper Use of Metre Scale:** When measuring length with a metre scale, it must be placed exactly along the length of the object to avoid parallax errors.
• **Avoiding Zero-End Wear:** Measurements should ideally not start from a broken or worn-out zero mark of the scale; instead, another clear mark like $1.0\ cm$ should be used, and the reading subtracted accordingly.
• **Eye Position in Measurement (Parallax Error):** The eye must be kept vertically above the point where the measurement is being taken to prevent parallax errors caused by angled viewing.
• **Curved Line Measurement:** The length of a curved line cannot be measured directly with a straight metre scale and requires alternative techniques.
• **Technique for Measuring Curved Lines (Thread Method):** A cotton thread is laid carefully along the entire path of the curved line, marking both its starting and ending points on the thread.
• **Straightening the Thread:** The measured section of the thread is subsequently stretched straight along a standard metre scale to determine the exact length of the curved line.
• **Technique for Measuring Curved Lines (Divider Method):** A pair of dividers can be walked step-by-step along a curved path, counting the steps and multiplying by the fixed distance between the divider points.
• **Measurement of Volume of Liquids:** Liquids do not have a fixed shape and are measured in capacity units using graduated cylinders, measuring flasks, or pipettes.
• **Standard Units of Volume:** The SI unit of volume is the cubic metre ($m^3$), but in everyday practice, litres ($L$) and millilitres ($mL$) are widely utilized ($1\ L = 1000\ mL$).
• **Meniscus Reading:** When reading the liquid level in a graduated cylinder, the eye must be level with the bottom of the concave meniscus for transparent liquids like water.
• **Measurement of Irregular Solid Volume:** The volume of an irregularly shaped solid (like a stone) is measured using the water displacement method in a graduated measuring cylinder.

• **Concept of Rest:** An object is said to be at rest if its position does not change with respect to its immediate surroundings as time passes.
• **Concept of Motion:** An object is said to be in motion if its position changes continuously with respect to its surroundings as time passes.
• **Relative Nature of Rest and Motion:** Rest and motion are relative terms; a passenger sitting inside a moving train is at rest relative to fellow passengers, but in motion relative to trees outside.
• **Motion of a Train:** A train moving along a straight railway track exhibits multiple types of motion simultaneously.
• **Types of Motion:** Motion is classified into various categories based on the path traversed by the moving object, including rectilinear, circular, rotational, and periodic motion.
• **Rectilinear Motion (Linear Motion):** Motion in which an object travels along a straight line path is known as rectilinear motion, such as a car moving down a straight highway.
• **Examples of Rectilinear Motion:** Marching soldiers, a falling stone dropped from a height, and an athlete running on a $100$-metre straight track.
• **Curvilinear Motion:** Motion in which an object travels along a curved path, such as a ball thrown upward at an angle or a car taking a turn on a curved road.
• **Circular Motion:** Motion in which an object moves along a circular path such that its distance from a fixed center remains constant.
• **Examples of Circular Motion:** A stone tied to a string and whirled around, a satellite orbiting the Earth, and the tip of the hand of a clock.
• **Difference between Circular and Rotational Motion:** In circular motion, the entire body revolves around an external or internal center, whereas in rotational motion, the body spins about a fixed axis passing through it.
• **Rotational Motion Examples:** A spinning top, the blades of a ceiling fan turning around their central axis, and the rotation of the Earth on its own axis.
• **Periodic Motion:** Motion that repeats itself at regular, fixed intervals of time is called periodic motion.
• **Examples of Periodic Motion:** The oscillation of a simple pendulum, the beating of a human heart, and the revolution of the Earth around the Sun.
• **Non-Periodic Motion:** Motion that does not repeat itself at regular intervals of time, such as a child running randomly in a park or a leaf falling from a tree.
---PAGE-BREAK---
• **Translational Motion:** A type of motion in which all parts of an object move the same distance in the same direction, which can be either rectilinear or curvilinear.
• **Multiple Motions in a Single Object:** Many everyday objects exhibit more than one type of motion at the same time, combining linear, rotational, and periodic traits.
• **Motion of a Bicycle Wheel:** A bicycle wheel exhibits both rotational motion (spinning about its axle) and rectilinear/translational motion (moving forward along the road).
• **Motion of Earth:** The Earth exhibits rotational motion (spinning on its axis once every 24 hours) and periodic/revolution motion (orbiting the Sun once every 365.25 days).
• **Motion of a Drilling Machine:** The drill bit of a machine shows rotational motion while simultaneously advancing forward into the wall via rectilinear motion.
• **Motion of a Sewing Machine Needle:** The needle of a sewing machine undergoes periodic up-and-down motion, while its wheel undergoes rotational motion.
• **Motion of a Rolling Ball:** A ball rolling down an inclined plane displays rotational motion around its center and translational/rectilinear motion across the floor.
• **Oscillatory Motion:** A special type of periodic motion where an object moves back and forth about a mean or central position.
• **Examples of Oscillatory Motion:** The motion of a swing, the pendulum of a clock, and the string of a guitar when plucked.
• **Difference between Periodic and Oscillatory Motion:** All oscillatory motions are periodic, but not all periodic motions are oscillatory (e.g., the Earth's revolution around the Sun is periodic but not oscillatory).
• **Speed as a Concept:** Speed describes how fast or slow an object moves, defined mathematically as the distance covered by the object per unit of time.
• **Formula for Speed:** $\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance Travelled}}{\text{Time Taken}}$, forming the quantitative foundation for analyzing motion.
• **SI Unit of Speed:** The standard international unit of speed is metres per second ($m/s$), though kilometres per hour ($km/h$) is commonly used for vehicles.
• **Uniform Motion:** Motion of an object along a straight line in which the object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, no matter how small the intervals.
• **Non-Uniform Motion:** Motion in which an object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time, or equal distances in unequal intervals, representing accelerated travel.

• **Standardization History:** The General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) established the International System of Units in 1960 to maintain global uniformity in scientific data.
• **Metre Standard Definition:** Originally defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator along a meridian passing through Paris.
• **Prototype Metre Bar:** Historically, a standard metre was defined as the distance between two engraved lines on a platinum-iridium alloy bar kept at the International Bureau of Weights and Measures.
• **Modern Metre Definition:** Today, the metre is scientifically defined as the distance light travels in a vacuum in $\frac{1}{299,792,458}$ of a second.
• **Kilogram Standard Definition:** Historically tied to the International Prototype of the Kilogram (IPK), a platinum-iridium cylinder kept in Sèvres, France.
• **Modern Kilogram Definition:** The kilogram is now defined in terms of the Planck constant, providing an unalterable, fundamental physical constant standard.
• **Second Standard Definition:** Historically defined as a fraction of a mean solar day, and now defined by the radiation frequency corresponding to the transition between two hyperfine levels of the caesium-133 atom.
• **CGS System of Units:** Centimetre-Gram-Second system, an older metric system utilizing smaller base units for length, mass, and time.
• **FPS System of Units:** Foot-Pound-Second system, a British system of units using foot for length, pound for mass, and second for time.
• **MKS System of Units:** Metre-Kilogram-Second system, which laid the direct foundation for the modern SI system.
• **Scalar Quantities:** Physical quantities that have only magnitude and no specific direction, such as distance, speed, mass, time, and temperature.
• **Vector Quantities:** Physical quantities that possess both magnitude and a definite direction, such as displacement, velocity, and acceleration.
• **Distance Definition:** The total length of the actual path covered by an object between its initial and final positions, regardless of direction.
• **Displacement Definition:** The shortest straight-line distance from the initial position to the final position of a moving object, along with a specified direction.
• **Difference Between Distance and Displacement:** Distance is a scalar quantity that can never be zero for a moving body, whereas displacement is a vector quantity that can be zero if an object returns to its starting point.

• **Measuring Instruments for Length:** Metre scales, measuring tapes, vernier callipers, and screw gauges are used depending on the required precision and scale of measurement.
• **Measuring Tape Construction:** Flexible measuring tapes made of fiberglass, plastic, or coated steel are used by tailors, carpenters, and civil engineers to measure longer or curved lengths.
• **Vernier Calliper Function:** A precision instrument used to measure internal and external diameters of cylinders, spheres, and small depths with high accuracy.
• **Screw Gauge Function:** An ultra-precision instrument utilized to measure extremely small thicknesses, such as the diameter of a thin wire or the thickness of a glass plate.
• **Stopwatch Function:** A specialized timepiece designed to measure elapsed time intervals with high precision, often accurate to fractions of a second, used in sports and physics experiments.
• **Mechanical Clocks:** Traditional clocks relying on gear trains, springs, and an escapement mechanism powered by a mainspring or falling weights.
• **Quartz Clocks:** Modern timepieces that use an electronic oscillator regulated by a quartz crystal to keep exceptionally accurate time.
• **Atomic Clocks:** The most accurate timekeeping devices in existence, utilizing the resonant frequencies of atoms such as caesium or rubidium.
• **Thermometer Mechanism:** Devices that measure temperature based on the thermal expansion of liquids like mercury or alcohol, or through electronic sensors.
• **Clinical Thermometer:** A specialized mercury-in-glass thermometer designed specifically to measure human body temperature, typically ranging from $35^\circ C$ to $42^\circ C$.
• **Laboratory Thermometer:** A versatile temperature-measuring instrument used in science laboratories, usually covering a broader range from $-10^\circ C$ to $110^\circ C$.
• **Digital Thermometers:** Modern electronic devices that display temperature numerically on an LCD screen, eliminating hazardous mercury content.
• **Beam Balance:** A classic laboratory instrument used to measure the mass of an object by comparing it with known standard masses using the principle of moments.
• **Electronic Balance:** A highly accurate modern device that measures mass digitally using load cells, providing direct digital readouts in milligrams or grams.
• **Spring Balance:** An instrument used to measure the weight (gravitational force acting on mass) of an object by utilizing the stretching of a calibrated helical spring.

• **History of Transport:** Human transport evolved from walking on foot to domesticating animals, inventing the wheel, building boats, and eventually inventing steam engines, automobiles, and aeroplanes.
• **Invention of the Wheel:** The invention of the wheel revolutionized transport and machinery, significantly reducing friction and enabling heavy loads to be transported efficiently.
• **Animal-Powered Transport:** Horses, oxen, camels, and elephants served as primary modes of carrying goods and passengers over land before the invention of mechanical engines.
• **Water Transport Evolution:** Early humans used floating logs, rafts, canoes, and sailboats, leading to steamships and modern motorized cargo vessels.
• **Air Transport Evolution:** From hot-air balloons and gliders to modern supersonic jets and spacecraft, air travel transformed global connectivity.
• **Significance of Measurement in Trade:** Accurate measurement is vital in commerce for buying and selling goods by mass, volume, or length, ensuring fairness between buyers and sellers.
• **Significance of Measurement in Science:** Scientific experiments rely entirely on precise, reproducible measurements of physical quantities to formulate laws and validate theories.
• **Significance of Measurement in Construction:** Building houses, bridges, and roads requires exact dimensional measurements to ensure structural integrity and safety.
• **Significance of Measurement in Medicine:** Administering exact medicine dosages, measuring body temperature, and monitoring pulse rates depend on precise measurements.
• **Everyday Applications of Motion:** Walking, driving, cooking, running water, and breathing all involve various forms of motion studied in physics.
• **Earth's Hidden Motion:** Although humans feel stationary while standing on the ground, the Earth is constantly in motion, rotating on its axis and revolving around the Sun at tremendous speeds.
• **Galilean Relativity Foundation:** The principle that motion can only be defined relative to a chosen frame of reference, forming the bedrock of classical mechanics.
• **Importance of Reference Points:** Describing the location or motion of an object requires a clearly defined reference point, also known as the origin.
• **Coordinate Systems:** In physics, positions are mapped using Cartesian coordinate axes ($x, y, z$) relative to an origin point to mathematically analyze motion.
• **Path Length vs. Displacement Analysis:** Analyzing the difference between path length (total distance covered) and displacement (straight-line vector) is crucial in kinematics.

• **Mathematical Representation of Speed:** Speed ($v$) is expressed as $v = \frac{s}{t}$, where $s$ is distance and $t$ is time.
• **Conversion of Units ($km/h$ to $m/s$):** To convert speed from kilometres per hour to metres per second, multiply the value by $\frac{5}{18}$.
• **Conversion of Units ($m/s$ to $km/h$):** To convert speed from metres per second to kilometres per hour, multiply the value by $\frac{18}{5}$.
• **Numerical Problem Solving in Speed:** Calculating missing variables (distance, time, or speed) using algebraic transposition of the core speed formula.
• **Average Speed Definition:** The total distance travelled by an object divided by the total time taken to cover that distance, particularly useful for non-uniform motion.
• **Instantaneous Speed:** The speed of an object at a specific, exact instant in time, as indicated by a vehicle's speedometer.
• **Odometer Function:** An instrument built into vehicles (cars, motorcycles, bicycles) that automatically measures and displays the total cumulative distance travelled by the vehicle.
• **Speedometer Function:** A dashboard instrument that continuously measures and displays the instantaneous speed of a vehicle in real-time.
• **Graphical Representation of Motion:** Motion can be visually analyzed using distance-time graphs, where distance is plotted on the vertical axis ($y$-axis) and time on the horizontal axis ($x$-axis).
• **Interpretation of Distance-Time Graphs:** A straight, inclined line on a distance-time graph indicates uniform speed, while a curved line indicates non-uniform speed.
• **Stationary Object Graph:** A horizontal line parallel to the time axis on a distance-time graph indicates that the object is at rest (distance is not changing with time).
• **Slope of Distance-Time Graph:** The steepness (slope) of the line on a distance-time graph represents the speed of the object; a steeper slope denotes higher speed.
• **Kinematic Equations Introduction:** Advanced studies of motion involve equations connecting initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, time, and distance.
• **Galileo's Experiments on Motion:** Galileo Galilei conducted pioneering experiments with inclined planes to study falling bodies, proving that all objects fall at the same rate in a vacuum regardless of mass.
• **Newton's Laws of Motion Preview:** Sir Isaac Newton built upon earlier concepts of motion to formulate his three universal laws of motion, which govern classical mechanics.
